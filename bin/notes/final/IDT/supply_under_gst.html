1. Taxable Event Taxable Event under GST — The Concept of "Supply"

📌 Foundation Stone of any Taxing Statute

A taxable event is the fundamental transaction or occurrence that triggers a tax consequence. It is the bedrock of any tax statute; it determines the exact point at which tax is levied.

Under the earlier indirect tax regime, the taxable events in different statutes were fragmented and caused endless litigation over decades:

  • Central Excise: Manufacture or production of excisable goods. (Litigations: whether a process amounted to manufacture, marketability).
  • State VAT / Central Sales Tax (CST): Sale of goods. (Litigations: pre-determined sale, works contract vs outright sale).
  • Finance Act, 1994: Provision of taxable services. (Litigations: whether software or SIM card is goods or services).

The GST laws resolved these historical controversies by introducing one single, comprehensive taxable event: SUPPLY of goods or services or both. Various erstwhile taxable events (manufacture, sale, provision of service, entry into territory) have been completely done away with.

Under GST, tax is levied across the entire value chain in an integrated manner. The credit of tax paid at each prior stage is allowed as a set-off against the tax liability at the subsequent stage, effectively eliminating the cascading effect (tax-on-tax).

Annexure-1 Genesis of GST in India & Constitutional Architecture

Power to levy tax is drawn from the Constitution of India. To introduce Goods and Services Tax, Parliament passed the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016. Prior to this amendment, the Union had the exclusive power to tax manufacture (Entry 84, List I) and services (Residuary Entry 97, List I), whereas States had exclusive power to tax sales of goods (Entry 54, List II). There was no concurrent power to levy tax simultaneously.

Constitutional Article Significance & Architectural Power
Article 246A
Special provision for GST		 Empowers both Parliament and State Legislatures to make laws with respect to goods and services tax. Parliament has exclusive power to make laws with respect to GST where the supply of goods, or services, or both takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.
Article 269A
Levy & Collection of IGST		 GST on supplies in the course of inter-State trade or commerce is levied and collected by the Government of India and apportioned between the Union and the States in the manner provided by Parliament by law. Import of goods or services is deemed to be in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.
Article 279A
GST Council		 Constitutes the GST Council as a joint forum of the Centre and the States (headed by the Union Finance Minister) to make recommendations on taxes to be subsumed, exemptions, model laws, threshold limits, and rates.
Article 366(12A)
Definition of GST		 Defines "goods and services tax" as any tax on supply of goods, or services or both, except taxes on the supply of the alcoholic liquor for human consumption.
⚠️ Key Exam Distinction: Alcohol vs Petroleum

1. Alcoholic Liquor for Human Consumption: Outside the constitutional definition of GST under Article 366(12A). It is NOT leviable to GST at all. States continue to levy State Excise Duty on manufacture and State VAT/CST on sale.

2. Five Specified Petroleum Products (Section 9(2)): Petroleum crude, high speed diesel (HSD), motor spirit (commonly known as petrol), natural gas, and aviation turbine fuel (ATF) are constitutionally within GST. However, under Section 9(2) of the CGST Act, the levy of GST has been temporarily deferred and will be levied from a date to be notified on the recommendation of the GST Council. Meanwhile, Central Excise and State VAT apply.

📜 Principles of Statutory Interpretation: "Means" vs "Includes":
• Definitions in which the expression "means" is used are exhaustive definitions. Their scope cannot be expanded beyond what is explicitly stated (e.g. Section 2(21) Central tax means...).
• Definitions in which the expression "includes" is used are inclusive definitions. Their scope is extensive and covers both conventional meanings and specified inclusions (e.g. Section 2(17) Business includes..., Section 7 Supply includes...).

Section 2 Core Statutory Definitions Governing "Supply"

Term & Section Statutory Definition & Scope Exam Analysis & Inclusions/Exclusions
Goods
Section 2(52)		 Every kind of movable property other than money and securities, but includes:
  • Actionable claim;
  • Growing crops, grass;
  • Things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before supply or under a contract of supply.
 Excludes: Money and Securities.
Includes: Standing timber/crops to be severed, Actionable claims (note Schedule III limits taxability to Specified Actionable Claims).
Services
Section 2(102)		 Anything other than goods, money and securities, but includes activities relating to the use of money or its conversion by cash or by any other mode, from one form, currency or denomination, to another form, currency or denomination for which a separate consideration is charged.
Explanation: Includes facilitating or arranging transactions in securities.		 Vast residuary definition. If an activity is not goods, money, or securities, it falls within the definition of service.
Foreign Currency conversion: Separate commission/margin is a taxable service.
Money & Securities
Sec 2(75) & Sec 2(101)		 Money: Indian legal tender, foreign currency, cheque, promissory note, bill of exchange, letter of credit, draft, pay order, traveller cheque, money order.
Securities: Assigned the same meaning as in Section 2(h) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 (SCRA) — shares, stocks, bonds, debentures, derivatives, units of CIS/Mutual Funds, Govt securities. 		Neither Goods nor Services: Pure transactions in money/securities are outside GST.
Derivatives & Futures: Not subject to GST if cash-settled. However, if settled by actual physical delivery of commodity/currency, delivery attracts GST as goods.
Consideration
Section 2(31)		 Any payment made or to be made, whether in money or otherwise, in respect of, in response to, or for the inducement of, the supply of goods or services or both, whether by the recipient or by any other person; and the monetary value of any act or forbearance.
Exclusion: Any subsidy given by the Central Government or a State Government.
Deposits: A deposit is NOT consideration unless the supplier applies it as consideration.		 Quid Pro Quo: Consideration requires a reciprocal element ("something for something").
Non-reciprocal payments: Pure philanthropic donations without commercial advertising are NOT consideration (Circular 116/35/2019).
Business
Section 2(17)		 Includes:
  • Any trade, commerce, manufacture, profession, vocation, adventure, wager whether or not for monetary benefit;
  • Any activity incidental or ancillary thereto;
  • Any activity regardless of volume, frequency, continuity or regularity;
  • Supply/acquisition of goods upon commencement or closure;
  • Provision by a club/association of facilities to members;
  • Admission for a consideration to any premises;
  • Activities of race club including totalisator or bookmaker;
  • Any activity by Central/State Govt or local authority as public authority.
 No profit motive required!
No regularity required: Even an isolated commercial transaction constitutes business.
Personal capacity sales: Sale of a personal car or personal old gold jewellery does NOT qualify as business.
Actionable Claim
Section 2(1)		 Shall have the same meaning as assigned to it in Section 3 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (claim to any debt or beneficial interest in movable property not in possession). Includes lottery, betting, gambling, arrears of rent. Included in goods under Section 2(52). However, under Schedule III Para 6, actionable claims are non-supplies except Specified Actionable Claims.
Supplier & Recipient

Recipient: Where consideration is payable → the person liable to pay consideration; where no consideration is payable → person to whom goods are delivered/possession given or to whom service is rendered. 		Person paying the consideration is the recipient, even if the goods/services are physically delivered to an employee or third party.

Section 7(1)(a) General Scope of Supply — The 4 Essential Pillars

Statutory Language: Section 7(1)(a)

"Supply includes all forms of supply of goods or services or both such as sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease or disposal made or agreed to be made for a consideration by a person in the course or furtherance of business."

Section 7(1)(a) lays down the Four Essential Ingredients that must be cumulatively satisfied:

1. Form of Supply

Must be in the form of sale, transfer, barter, exchange, license, rental, lease, or disposal.

2. Goods or Services

The subject matter must be goods (Sec 2(52)) or services (Sec 2(102)).

3. For Consideration

There must be consideration flowing from recipient or any other person.

4. In Business Course

Must be made in the course or furtherance of business (commercial transactions).

Forms of Supply Analysis of the 8 Statutory Forms of Supply [ICAI Examples 1 to 4]

ICAI Example (1) • Sale

A shopkeeper sells a pen for ₹100 to the buyer. After the sale, the pen belongs to the buyer and the shopkeeper does not have any right on the pen. This is a conventional transaction of sale involving transfer of property in goods for a money consideration.

ICAI Example (2) • Transfer

A company transfers goods from its factory to the depot for sale purposes. This is a transfer of goods where the outright sale has not yet taken place. Transfer is wider than sale and covers situations where ownership or possession passes under commercial arrangement.

ICAI Example (3) • Exchange

When a new car worth ₹5,00,000 is purchased in exchange of an old car along with a monetary consideration of ₹4,00,000 paid for the purchase. This is an exchange transaction. Under GST, tax is payable on the full open market value of the new car (₹5,00,000) under Rule 27!

ICAI Example (4) • Barter

A doctor got his hair cut from a barber and provides him medical consultancy in return. In this barter transaction, two independent supplies occur simultaneously:

  • The barber provides haircutting services to the doctor for which the consideration is medical consultancy.
  • The doctor provides medical consultancy services to the barber for which the consideration is hair cutting services.

Both legs are independent taxable supplies under GST!

Licence, Rental, Lease, or Disposal: Where goods are given on licence, lease, or rental without transfer of title, there is no transfer of ownership. Under Schedule II, such transactions are specifically classified as Supply of Services. "Disposal" covers situations where business assets are sold, pledged, given away, or scrapped.

Consideration Consideration Nuances & High-Yield Circulars [ICAI Examples 5 to 6]

Consideration requires quid pro quo ("something for something"). It covers anything done, given, or promised in exchange for the supply. Consideration can flow from the recipient or a third person, but excludes subsidies given by the Central or State Governments.

📜 Circular No. 116/35/2019-GST: Donations Received by Charitable/Religious Institutions

When donations or gifts are received by charitable or religious institutions, schools, or hospitals, and the recipient places a nameplate expressing gratitude:
GST is NOT leviable if the following 3 cumulative conditions are satisfied:

  1. The gift or donation is made to a charitable organisation;
  2. The payment has the character of gift or donation; and
  3. The purpose is philanthropic (i.e. it leads to no commercial gain) and no commercial advertising is involved.
ICAI Example (5) • Philanthropic Donation

Bhushan donated a blackboard to Yoganisht Sansthan — a charitable yoga institution. Yoganisht Sansthan printed underneath the blackboard so donated: “Good wishes from Mr. Bhushan”.
Conclusion: Pure expression of gratitude without advertising the donor's business → No supply of service → NO GST liability.

ICAI Example (6) • Philanthropic Room Construction

Smt. Durga Devi donated money to a temple in the memory of her late father. The Temple Trust constructed a room in the temple complex from such donation and wrote: “Donated by Smt. Durga Devi in the memory of her father” on the door floor of the room.
Conclusion: No mention of any commercial or business activity → NO GST.

⚠️ Contrast with Commercial Sponsorship / Advertising: If the inscription states “Room donated by ABC Electronics Ltd., Best Air Conditioners, Contact 9876543210”, it is an advertisement/sponsorship service with clear commercial benefit (quid pro quo). It qualifies as a taxable supply of service!

Transaction Circular Reference Clarification & GST Taxability Determination
Art Works sent to Galleries Cir 22/22/2017 Artists send artwork to galleries for exhibition. No consideration flows from gallery to artist at the time of sending. Therefore, sending artwork for exhibition is NOT a supply. The supply takes place only when a buyer selects and purchases the artwork displayed at the gallery.
No Claim Bonus (NCB) in Motor Insurance Cir 186/18/2022 Deduction on account of NCB allowed by the insurance company from the gross insurance premium is an industry pricing mechanism. The insured is under no contractual obligation to refrain from lodging a claim. It is clarified that there is no supply of service by the insured to the insurer; GST is payable on the net insurance premium after deducting NCB.
Salvage / Wreck Value in Motor Insurance Cir 215/9/2024 When insurance companies settle claims for damaged motor vehicles:
(i) Insurer deducts salvage value: Salvage remains property of the insured. Insurer is NOT liable to pay GST on salvage.
(ii) Insurer does NOT deduct salvage value and pays full claim: Salvage becomes property of the insurer. The insurer is obligated to discharge GST when selling the salvage to a salvage buyer.

Course of Business Supplies in the Course or Furtherance of Business [ICAI Examples 7 to 14]

GST is essentially a tax on commercial transactions. Under Section 7(1)(a), supplies must be made "in the course or furtherance of business". Personal transactions carried out by an individual in his personal capacity do not attract GST:

ICAI Example (7) • Sale of Personal Car

Rishabh buys a car for his personal use and after a year sells it to a car dealer. Sale of car by Rishabh to car dealer is NOT a supply under CGST Act because said supply is not made by Rishabh in the course or furtherance of business.

ICAI Example (8) • Sale of Old Personal Jewellery

Manikarnika sold her old gold bangles and earrings to ‘Aabhushan Jewellers’. Sale of old gold jewellery by an individual to a jeweller will NOT constitute supply as the same cannot be said to be in the course or furtherance of business of the individual (Press Release dated 13.07.2017).

ICAI Example (9) • Supply as a Vocation

Sundaram Acharya, a famous actor, paints some paintings and sells them. The consideration from such sale is to be donated to a Charitable Trust — ‘Kind Human’.
Conclusion: Since Section 2(17) explicitly defines business to include "vocation", the sale of paintings by the actor qualifies as supply made in the course or furtherance of business, irrespective of the philanthropic destination of proceeds!

ICAI Example (10) • Resident Welfare Association Services

A Resident Welfare Association (RWA) provides the service of depositing the electricity bills of the residents in lieu of some nominal charges.
Conclusion: Provision of facilities/services by a club, association, or society to its members is specifically included in the definition of business under Section 2(17)(e). It is a taxable supply.

ICAI Example (11) • Admission to Premises

Services by way of admission to circus, cinema halls, amusement parks including theme parks, water parks, etc. for an entry fee are taxable supplies because admission of persons to any premises for consideration is included in business under Section 2(17)(f).

ICAI Example (12) • Race Club Wagering Services

Royal Turf Race Club is engaged in facilitating wagering (betting) transactions on horses placed through totalisator. For providing the service of facilitating wagering transactions, Royal Turf Race Club gets commission which is deducted and retained by the club from total bet value. Said services amount to supply as the activities of a race club are included in business under Section 2(17)(h).

🛢️ Circular No. 32/06/2018-GST: Cost Petroleum & Profit Petroleum

In oil exploration under Production Sharing Contracts (PSCs) with the Government:

  • Cost Petroleum: The contractor recovers contract costs from total sale proceeds. The contractor carries out exploration for itself, not as a service to Government. Hence, Cost Petroleum is NOT consideration for services and is NOT taxable per se.
  • Profit Petroleum: The remaining profit shared with the Government. The Government’s share of profit petroleum is EXEMPT from GST vide Entry 82A of Notification 12/2017.
ICAI Example (13) • JV Cash Calls: Capital Contribution (No GST)

There are 4 members in an unincorporated Joint Venture (JV) including the operating member. Each contributes ₹100 as part of their share (total ₹400 collected). The operating member purchases machinery for ₹400 for the JV to be used in oil production.
Conclusion: Cash calls paid for purchasing machinery are merely in the nature of capital contribution. This is a transaction in money → NO GST (Circular 35/9/2018).

ICAI Example (14) • JV Cash Calls: Operating Member Equipment (Liable to GST)

There are 4 members in the JV including the operating member and each contributes ₹100 as their share (total ₹400 collected). The operating member thereafter uses its own machinery and performs exploration and production activities on behalf of the JV.
Conclusion: The operating member is recovering the cost appropriated towards machinery & services from other JV members in their participating interest ratio. This constitutes a taxable supply of service (Circular 35/9/2018).

Financial Instruments Transactions in Money, Securities, Derivatives & SLS

The definitions of ‘goods’ and ‘services’ specifically exclude both money and securities. Transactions in financial instruments have specific GST treatments:

Financial Instrument / Transaction Statutory Classification GST Taxability Determination
Commercial Paper (CP) & Certificate of Deposit (CD) Money market instruments / Promissory notes. NOT A SUPPLY (Transaction in money). However, any separate service charge or documentation fee is taxable.
Derivatives (Futures & Options) — Cash Settled Covered under 'Securities' under Section 2(h) of SCRA, 1956. NOT A SUPPLY (Securities are neither goods nor services).
Futures / Forward Contracts with Actual Delivery Settled by actual delivery of underlying commodity or currency. TAXABLE SUPPLY OF GOODS upon delivery! Normal GST applies to the goods supplied.
Interest Rate Swaps & Forex Swaps Contracts for differences / derivatives. NOT A SUPPLY (Classified as securities).
Securities Lending Scheme, 1997 (SLS)
Circular 119/38/2019		 Lending of securities without transfer of title. Lender earns a lending fee. TAXABLE SUPPLY OF SERVICE. The lending fee is consideration for service and attracts GST.
Holding Shares in Subsidiary Company
Circular 196/08/2023		 Holding shares/securities per se. NOT A SUPPLY. Merely owning controlling equity interest is not a supply of service by holding company to subsidiary.

Section 7(1)(b) Importation of Services for Consideration [ICAI Example 15]

Statutory Language: Section 7(1)(b)

"Supply includes importation of services, for a consideration whether or not in the course or furtherance of business."

Section 7(1)(b) is the sole exception to the requirement that supply must be made in the course or furtherance of business. Even if services are imported for purely personal purposes, as long as consideration is paid, it qualifies as a supply:

ICAI Example (15) • Personal Architect Imported from NY

Ramaiyaa, a proprietor, has received architect services for his personal residence from an architect located in New York at an agreed consideration of $5,000.
Conclusion: The import of services by Ramaiyaa qualifies as supply under Section 7(1)(b), though it is not in the course or furtherance of business!

Section 7(1)(c) + Schedule I Activities Treated as Supply Even if Made WITHOUT Consideration [ICAI Examples 16 to 30]

Under Section 7(1)(c), the 4 specific activities in Schedule I are treated as deemed supplies even when made completely free of cost (without consideration):

Para 1: Permanent Transfer or Disposal of Business Assets with ITC

Requires 3 cumulative conditions: (1) Transfer or disposal of business asset, (2) Permanent, (3) Input Tax Credit (ITC) must have been availed on such asset.

ICAI Example (16) • Business Laptops Given Free

Dhruv gives old laptops being used in his business to his friend free of cost.
Conclusion: This qualifies as a supply under Schedule I Para 1 provided input tax credit was availed by Dhruv on such laptops.

ICAI Example (17) • Car with Blocked ITC Given Free

A dealer of air-conditioners permanently transfers a motor vehicle free of cost. ITC on said motor vehicle was blocked under Section 17(5).
Conclusion: The transaction will NOT constitute a supply because the essential condition of availment of ITC on the transferred business asset is not fulfilled.

Para 2: Supplies between Related Persons or Distinct Persons

Supply of goods or services between related persons or between distinct persons (Section 25), when made in the course or furtherance of business, is a supply even without consideration.

ICAI Example (18) • Related Persons by Common Shareholding

Ms. Priya holds 30% shares of ABC Ltd. and 35% shares of XYZ Ltd.
Conclusion: Under explanation to Section 15, since Priya holds more than 25% voting power in both companies, Priya controls both, and ABC Ltd. and XYZ Ltd. are related persons.

ICAI Example (19) • Related Persons by Corporate Control

Q Ltd. has a deciding role in corporate policy, operations management, and quality control of R Ltd.
Conclusion: Q Ltd. controls R Ltd. Therefore, Q Ltd. and R Ltd. are related persons.

ICAI Example (20) • Distinct Persons (Section 25(4))

Mohan, a Chartered Accountant, has a registered head office in Delhi. He has also obtained registration in the State of West Bengal in respect of his newly opened branch office.
Conclusion: Mohan’s registrations under the same PAN in West Bengal and Delhi shall be treated as distinct persons. Any inter-branch services (even without consideration) are supplies.

ICAI Example (21) • Establishments of Distinct Persons (Section 25(5))

Rishabh Enterprises, a registered supplier, owns a restaurant in Virar, Maharashtra. It has opened a liquor shop in Dehradun, Uttarakhand for trading of alcoholic liquor for human consumption (non-taxable, hence unregistered in Uttarakhand).
Conclusion: Restaurant in Maharashtra and liquor shop in Uttarakhand are establishments of distinct persons. Supply of goods/services from Maharashtra restaurant to Uttarakhand shop without consideration qualifies as supply!

ICAI Example (22) • Stock Transfer Between Separate Registrations

Raghubir Fabrics transfers 1,000 shirts from its factory in Lucknow to its retail showroom in Delhi. Both locations are registered.
Conclusion: Although no consideration is charged, supply of goods between distinct persons constitutes supply.

ICAI Example (23) • Stock Transfer Within Same Registration (NO SUPPLY)

Raghubir Fabrics transfers 1,000 shirts from its Lucknow factory to its Kanpur showroom. Both are in Uttar Pradesh under a single registration (Lucknow as principal place and Kanpur as additional place).
Conclusion: Since no consideration is charged and transfer is within the same registration, it does NOT constitute supply.

⚙️ Circular No. 47/21/2018-GST (Moulds & Dies sent FOC to Component Manufacturers): Moulds and dies owned by an Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) sent free of cost to a component manufacturer do NOT constitute supply because OEM and component manufacturer are independent unrelated entities and no consideration is involved.

🎁 Employer-Employee Gifts vs Contractual Perquisites

• Proviso to Schedule I Para 2: Gifts not exceeding ₹50,000 in value in a financial year by an employer to an employee shall not be treated as supply. If the value exceeds ₹50,000, the entire value is taxable!
• Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST: Perquisites provided by employer to employee in terms of the contractual employment agreement (free canteen food, gym, health insurance, free housing) are non-supplies under Schedule III Para 1 and are NOT taxable.

Para 3: Principal & Agent Transactions [ICAI Examples 24 to 28]

Supply of goods by principal to agent (or by agent to principal) without consideration is a deemed supply under Schedule I Para 3.
The Deciding Factor (Circular No. 57/31/2018-GST): Does the agent issue the invoice to the customer in the agent's own name?

ICAI Example (24) • Procurement Facilitator (Not an Agent under Para 3)

Anmol appoints Bholu to procure goods from the market. Bholu identifies suppliers and asks supplier Golu to send goods and invoice directly to Anmol.
Conclusion: Bholu does not issue invoice in his own name. Bholu is NOT an agent under Schedule I Para 3.

ICAI Example (25) • Bank Auctioneer (Not an Agent under Para 3)

Manimani Bank appoints Mandaar (auctioneer) to auction goods. The invoice for supply of goods is issued directly by Manimani Bank to the successful bidder.
Conclusion: Mandaar is merely providing auctioneering services and does not issue the goods invoice. Mandaar is NOT an agent under Schedule I Para 3.

ICAI Example (26) • Auctioneer Invoicing in Own Name (Agent under Para 3!)

Gautam (artist) appoints Gambhir (auctioneer) to auction his painting. The invoice for supply of the painting is issued by Gambhir on behalf of Gautam in Gambhir's own name and the painting is delivered to the bidder.
Conclusion: Gambhir has authority to transfer title on behalf of Gautam. Gambhir is an agent under Schedule I Para 3. The movement of the painting from Gautam to Gambhir without consideration constitutes a deemed supply!

ICAI Example (27) • C&F Commission Agent

A C&F agent takes possession of goods from principal and issues invoice in his own name.
Conclusion: The C&F agent is an agent under Schedule I Para 3. Disclosure or non-disclosure of principal's name is immaterial.

ICAI Example (28) • APMC Commission Agent

Ravi sells agricultural produce through Kavi (APMC commission agent).
Conclusion: If Kavi issues the invoice to the buyer in his own name → Agent under Para 3. If invoice is issued directly by Ravi → Kavi is NOT an agent under Para 3.

🎯 Master Circular: Del Credere Agent (DCA) Rules (Circular No. 73/47/2018-GST)

A Del Credere Agent (DCA) guarantees payment to the principal. If the buyer delays payment, DCA pays the principal and charges interest to the buyer:

Criteria Case 1: DCA is NOT an Agent under Para 3 (Invoice issued by Principal directly) Case 2: DCA IS an Agent under Para 3 (Invoice issued by DCA in own name)
Status Invoice issued by supplier (principal) to customer. Invoice issued by DCA to customer in DCA's name.
Transactions Occurring 1. Supply of goods from Principal to Buyer.
2. Agency services by DCA to Principal.
3. Extension of loan by DCA to Buyer.		 1. Deemed supply of goods: Principal to DCA.
2. Supply of goods: DCA to Buyer.
3. Extension of credit by DCA to Buyer.
Treatment of Interest Charged by DCA Interest on loan is an independent supply of credit on Principal-to-Principal basis. It is EXEMPT from GST under Entry 27 of Notification 12/2017. Does NOT form part of goods value. Credit facility is subsumed into the supply of goods. Under Section 15(2)(d), interest charged for delayed payment must be included in the value of goods and taxed at the goods rate!

Para 4: Import of Services from Related Persons without Consideration [ICAI Examples 29 to 30]

ICAI Example (29) • Branch Receiving Free Legal Advice from HO

Jhumroo Associates (India) received legal consultancy services free of cost from its Head Office located in Malaysia.
Conclusion: Indian branch and Malaysian HO are establishments of distinct/related persons. Service received in the course of business without consideration qualifies as supply under Schedule I Para 4.

ICAI Example (30) • Free Architect Advice from Son in US (Personal vs Business)

Chakmak, a registered proprietor in Delhi, receives free architect services from his son located in US:

  • Case A (Personal Residence): Since the service is for his personal residence, it is NOT in the course or furtherance of business. NOT a supply under Schedule I Para 4!
  • Case B (Commercial Office): If received for his newly constructed office in Delhi → Received in course of business from a related person without consideration → Qualifies as a deemed supply under Schedule I Para 4!

Section 7(1)(aa) Supplies between Clubs/Associations and Members [ICAI Examples 31 & 32]

Statutory Language: Section 7(1)(aa) (Retrospective w.e.f. 01.07.2017)

"The activities or transactions, by a person, other than an individual, to its members or constituents or vice-versa, for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration.
Explanation: For the purposes of this clause, it is hereby clarified that, notwithstanding anything contained in any other law for the time being in force or any judgment, decree or order of any Court, tribunal or authority, the person and its members or constituents shall be deemed to be two separate persons and the supply of activities or transactions inter se shall be deemed to take place from one such person to another."

Legislative Nullification of Calcutta Club: In State of West Bengal v. Calcutta Club Ltd., the Supreme Court held that clubs and their members are one and the same under the Doctrine of Mutuality. Parliament inserted Section 7(1)(aa) with retrospective effect from 1st July 2017 to deem them two separate legal persons.

ICAI Example (31) • RWA Air-Conditioner Supply

Resident Welfare Association (RWA) of Sanskriti Society supplies air-conditioners to its members at a concessional price.
Conclusion: Deemed to be supply between two separate persons → Taxable supply under Section 7(1)(aa).

ICAI Example (32) • RWA Maintenance Charges

A Resident Welfare Association collects monthly maintenance charges from its members for security and common amenities.
Conclusion: Supply takes place from RWA to members → Taxable supply under Section 7(1)(aa) (Subject to RWA threshold exemption of ₹7,500/month/member under Notification 12/2017).

Section 8 Composite and Mixed Supplies — Principles & Taxability

Composite Supply

Sec 2(30) & 8(a)

Consists of two or more taxable supplies of goods or services or both, naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of business, one of which is a principal supply.

Tax Treatment:
Treated as a supply of the Principal Supply.
Entire package taxed at the rate of the Principal Supply!

Classic Examples:

  • Air ticket + in-flight meals + baggage allowance → Taxed as passenger transportation service.
  • Supply of laptop + charger + laptop bag → Laptop is principal supply.
  • Goods packed & transported with transit insurance → Transportation/insurance is ancillary to supply of goods.

Mixed Supply

Sec 2(74) & 8(b)

Consists of two or more individual supplies of goods or services, made together for a single price, which do NOT constitute a composite supply (i.e. artificially bundled; items can be sold independently).

Tax Treatment:
Treated as a supply of that particular supply which attracts the HIGHEST RATE OF TAX!

Classic Examples:

  • Gift hamper containing canned food (12%), dry fruits (12%), chocolates (18%), and aerated drinks (28%) for a single price of ₹2,500 → Entire hamper taxed at 28%!
  • House let out on rent along with furniture and refrigerator for a composite monthly rent → Mixed supply.

⚠️ The "Single Price" Test: If items that could form a mixed supply are billed with separate prices on the invoice (e.g. Chocolates ₹1,000, Dry Fruits ₹500, Aerated Drinks ₹300), the transaction is NEITHER a composite supply NOR a mixed supply. Each item is taxed individually at its own specific tariff rate!

Activity / Contract Circular Composite vs Mixed Supply Determination
Printing of Books, Pamphlets, Brochures Cir 11/11/2017 If the recipient supplies the content/manuscript and printer only prints on paper → Composite Supply of Service (Printing is principal). If printer designs the content himself and sells books → Composite Supply of Goods.
Retreading of Tyres Cir 34/08/2018 Old tyre brought by customer for retreading involving buffing, applying tread rubber, and vulcanising → Composite Supply of Service (Retreading service is principal).

TYK & Case Studies ICAI Test Your Knowledge — Solved Practical Exam Scenarios

Case Scenario 1 • Del Credere Agent & Loan Interest

Case Facts: Smart Tools Ltd. (supplier) engages Varun as a Del Credere Agent (DCA). Varun procures an order for goods worth ₹10,00,000 from Kuber Ltd. Varun issues the tax invoice for the goods in his own name to Kuber Ltd. on 1st April with 30 days credit. Kuber Ltd. fails to pay within 30 days. Varun makes the payment of ₹10,00,000 to Smart Tools Ltd. on the due date. For the additional 60 days delay, Varun charges ₹25,000 as interest from Kuber Ltd. Determine whether GST is payable on ₹25,000 interest and if so, at what rate?

Statutory Solution:

Under Circular No. 73/47/2018-GST, where a DCA issues the invoice for goods in his own name to the customer, the DCA falls within the ambit of an agent under Schedule I Para 3.

In this scenario, the temporary credit provided by Varun no longer retains the character of an independent financial supply. It is subsumed into the supply of goods. Under Section 15(2)(d), any interest or late fee charged for delayed payment must be included in the value of supply of the goods. Therefore, the interest of ₹25,000 is chargeable to GST at the same rate as applicable to Smart Tools' goods, and cannot claim exemption under Entry 27 of Notification 12/2017.

Case Scenario 2 • High Sea Sales & Custom Clearances

Case Facts: Apex Impex Ltd. of Mumbai imported industrial chemicals from Germany. While the ship was en route in international waters, Apex Impex endorsed the Bill of Lading in favour of Bharath Petrochemicals Ltd. for ₹50,00,000. Bharath Petrochemicals subsequently filed the Bill of Entry for Home Consumption at Mumbai port and assessed custom duties on assessable value of ₹52,00,000. The Superintendent of Central Tax demands IGST under GST law from Apex Impex on the ₹50,00,000 transaction. Is the demand sustainable?

Statutory Solution:

Under Paragraph 8(b) of Schedule III of the CGST Act, 2017, the supply of goods by the consignee to any other person, by endorsement of documents of title to the goods, after the goods have been dispatched from the port of origin located outside India but before clearance for home consumption (High Sea Sales) shall be treated as neither a supply of goods nor a supply of services.

Therefore, the transaction between Apex Impex and Bharath Petrochemicals is covered under the negative list (Schedule III) and is NOT subject to GST. IGST is leviable only once under Section 3(7) of the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 when Bharath Petrochemicals clears the goods for home consumption. The demand raised by the Superintendent is legally unsustainable!

Case Scenario 3 • Employer-Employee Gifts vs Contractual Remuneration

Case Facts: Delta Infotech Pvt. Ltd. provides the following perks and gifts to its employees during the financial year:

  1. Gold coin worth ₹45,000 given to Senior Manager Anand on Diwali.
  2. Diamond ring worth ₹75,000 given to Vice President Vikram on achieving sales target.
  3. Free executive lunch in office canteen costing ₹35,000 annually per employee provided as part of the employment agreement.

Statutory Solution:

  • 1. Gold Coin to Anand (₹45,000): Under the proviso to Schedule I Para 2, gifts not exceeding ₹50,000 in value in a financial year by an employer to an employee shall not be treated as supply. Since the value is ₹45,000 (≤ ₹50,000), it is NOT a supply and NO GST is payable.
  • 2. Diamond Ring to Vikram (₹75,000): Since the value exceeds the threshold of ₹50,000, the benefit of the proviso is lost. The entire amount of ₹75,000 is treated as a deemed supply under Schedule I Para 2 between related persons and attracts GST!
  • 3. Canteen Free Lunch (₹35,000): As clarified by Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST, perquisites provided by an employer to employees in terms of the contractual employment agreement are part of cost-to-company (C2C) in relation to employment (Schedule III Para 1). Therefore, contractual canteen facilities do NOT constitute supply and are NOT liable to GST.
Case Scenario 4 • Composite vs Mixed Supply (Gift Packages)

Case Facts: Festive Delights sells a Deepawali Hamper for a composite price of ₹3,000 per box. Each hamper contains: Canned exotic fruits (GST rate 12%), Fine Chocolates (GST rate 18%), Pack of roasted almonds & cashews (GST rate 12%), and Sparkling aerated fruit beverages (GST rate 28%). The items can be purchased individually in the market. Determine the nature of supply and the applicable GST rate.

Statutory Solution:

Under Section 2(74) of the CGST Act, 2017, a mixed supply means two or more individual supplies of goods or services made together for a single price, which do not constitute a composite supply.

Here, canned fruits, chocolates, dry fruits, and aerated beverages are not naturally bundled in the ordinary course of business. Each of these items can be procured and supplied separately. Since they are bundled together artificially for a single price of ₹3,000, the transaction constitutes a Mixed Supply.

Under Section 8(b), a mixed supply is treated as a supply of that particular component which attracts the highest rate of tax. Among the items, sparkling aerated beverages attract 28% GST. Therefore, the entire hamper price of ₹3,000 is taxable at 28% GST!
