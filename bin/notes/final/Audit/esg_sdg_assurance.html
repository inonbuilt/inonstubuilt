Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) & ESG Assurance

Master the paradigm of non-financial reporting: Environment, Social and Governance (ESG), the 17 UN SDGs, Integrated Reporting (<IR> & the 6 Capitals), the statutory evolution of SEBI Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR), the 9 Principles of NGRBC, BRSR Core Reasonable Assurance, Climate-Related Financial Statement Risks (Ind AS 36, 16, 37 & SA 570), and all TYU (1–2) & TYK (1–21) solutions!

🌱 1. Introduction to Sustainability & The ESG Pillars
Triple Bottom Line Focus

Traditional financial reporting focused strictly on single-bottom-line financial profit. Modern corporate governance demands that business performance be measured on the touchstone of the Triple Bottom Line: People, Planet, and Profit.

💡 What is Sustainability & ESG?

Sustainability is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. ESG (Environment, Social, and Governance) represents the three central pillars used by institutional investors, lenders, and stakeholders to evaluate a company's resilience, ethical footprint, and long-term enterprise value creation.

The Three Pillars of ESG

1. Environmental (E)

How an organization impacts and stewards the natural ecosystem:

  • Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Emissions (Scope 1, Scope 2, Scope 3).
  • Energy efficiency & renewable energy transition.
  • Water withdrawal, consumption & recycling.
  • Waste management & Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR).
  • Biodiversity conservation & deforestation risks.
2. Social (S)

How an enterprise manages relationships with workforce, suppliers, customers, and society:

  • Employee health, safety, and workplace well-being.
  • Gender diversity, equity, and equal remuneration.
  • Human rights across supply chain (no child/forced labor).
  • Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) in local communities.
  • Consumer protection, product safety & data privacy.
3. Governance (G)

The internal architecture of leadership, rights, and ethical corporate conduct:

  • Board composition, independence & diversity.
  • Executive compensation linked to ESG KPIs.
  • Anti-bribery, anti-corruption & whistle-blower mechanisms.
  • Transparent financial reporting & shareholder voting.
  • Responsible public policy advocacy and lobbying.
🌐 2. The 17 United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
UN 2030 Global Agenda

Adopted by all 193 United Nations Member States in September 2015, the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development provides a shared global blueprint for peace and prosperity for people and the planet, encompassing 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and 169 associated targets:

Goal UN Sustainable Development Goal Corporate Interconnection & Business Action
SDG 1 No Poverty Creating living-wage jobs, local supply-chain procurement, targeted CSR livelihoods.
SDG 2 Zero Hunger Food waste reduction, agricultural supply-chain support, employee nutrition programs.
SDG 3 Good Health and Well-being Workplace ergonomics, mental healthcare, occupational safety, health insurance coverage.
SDG 4 Quality Education Apprenticeships, technical upskilling, vocational training, supporting local government schools.
SDG 5 Gender Equality Equal pay for equal work, women on corporate boards, maternity protections, anti-harassment (POSH).
SDG 6 Clean Water and Sanitation Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD) plants, rainwater harvesting, reducing water withdrawal in water-stressed zones.
SDG 7 Affordable and Clean Energy Rooftop solar installation, power purchase agreements (PPAs) for wind/solar, green hydrogen adoption.
SDG 8 Decent Work & Economic Growth Eradicating forced and child labor across supply chain, fair working hours, safe working conditions.
SDG 9 Industry, Innovation & Infrastructure R&D in low-carbon materials, circular economy tech, retrofitting industrial plants with clean tech.

SDG 12 Responsible Consumption & Production Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), recyclable product packaging, eliminating single-use plastics, EPR.

📈 3. Global Trends in Sustainability Reporting
International Frameworks

The global ESG landscape has evolved rapidly from fragmented voluntary guidelines to harmonized, mandatory international disclosure standards:

1. Global Reporting Initiative (GRI)

The most widely used voluntary sustainability reporting framework globally. Adopts an "Impact Materiality" lens (how company activities impact the economy, environment, and society for all multi-stakeholder groups).

2. Task Force on Climate-Related Financial Disclosures (TCFD)

Established by the Financial Stability Board (FSB). Structures disclosures around 4 core pillars: Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, and Metrics & Targets, analyzing physical and transition climate risks.

3. ISSB: IFRS S1 & IFRS S2 Standards

Issued in June 2023 by the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) under the IFRS Foundation:
• IFRS S1: General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information.
• IFRS S2: Climate-related Disclosures. Focuses on "Financial Materiality" for capital market investors.

4. European CSRD & ESRS

The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) mandates "Double Materiality" (reporting both financial materiality – outside-in risks, and impact materiality – inside-out impacts) across thousands of EU and non-EU companies.

📝 TYU 1: Meaning & Expected Commercial Benefits of Sustainability Reporting −

Scenario:

Explain the meaning of Sustainability Reporting and elucidate the tangible commercial and strategic benefits derived by a business enterprise from implementing structured sustainability reporting.

✅ ICAI Official Solution & Strategic Value Analysis

Meaning of Sustainability Reporting: It is an organization's practice of publicly measuring, disclosing, and communicating its economic, environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance to internal and external stakeholders in a regular and structured format.

Expected Strategic & Commercial Benefits:

  1. Attracting Global Capital & Lowering Cost of Debt: Institutional investors, sovereign wealth funds, and global banks actively integrate ESG ratings into capital allocation. Strong sustainability reporting unlocks green bonds and sustainability-linked credit at lower interest spreads.
  2. Long-Term Enterprise Resilience & Risk Mitigation: Identifies emerging climate, supply chain, and regulatory compliance risks early, allowing the entity to build adaptive operational resilience.
  3. B2B Customer Acquisition & Supply Chain Retention: Global multinational buyers (e.g., Apple, IKEA, Walmart) require their tier-1 and tier-2 vendors to demonstrate verified sustainability metrics. Robust reporting safeguards lucrative export contracts.
  4. Employee Retention & Talent Attraction: Modern professionals and younger workforce cohorts prefer working for purpose-driven companies that respect human rights, diversity, and environmental stewardship.
  5. Operational Cost Reduction: Energy conservation audits, water recycling, and waste minimization initiatives directly translate into lower manufacturing overheads and higher operating margins.
📊 5. Integrated Reporting Framework (<IR>) & The 6 Capitals
Holistic Value Creation

The International Integrated Reporting Framework (<IR>), developed by the International Integrated Reporting Council (IIRC, now consolidated under the Value Reporting Foundation / IFRS Foundation), shifts corporate reporting from siloed financial statements to a holistic narrative of Value Creation over the Short, Medium, and Long Term.

💡 The 6 Capitals of Integrated Reporting

Integrated Reporting conceives the organization as drawing upon six distinct forms of capital as inputs, transforming them through its business activities, and yielding outputs and outcomes:

1. Financial Capital

The pool of funds available to an organization through equity, debt financing, retained earnings, or grants to support operations and capital investments.

2. Manufactured Capital

Physical human-created infrastructure and objects: buildings, equipment, manufacturing factories, logistics fleet, roads, and port terminals.

3. Intellectual Capital

Organizational knowledge-based intangibles: patents, copyrights, software code, AI algorithms, brand reputation, organizational systems, and R&D capabilities.

4. Human Capital

People's competencies, capabilities, motivation to innovate, leadership skills, safety awareness, and ethical alignment with corporate values.

5. Social & Relationship Capital

Relationships established within and between communities, customers, suppliers, and regulators; shared norms, values, and social license to operate.

6. Natural Capital

All renewable and non-renewable environmental resources and processes: clean air, water, land, forests, minerals, biodiversity, and ecosystem health.

  • This strengthens the company's social license to operate, community goodwill, and shared stakeholder relationships.
  • Capital Referred to: Social and Relationship Capital.
🌐 7. Global ESG Frameworks & International Jurisdictions
Global Harmonization

Across the globe, capital market regulators and standard setters have moved aggressively from voluntary, disparate Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) guidelines to mandatory, legally enforceable climate and sustainability disclosures.

🇺🇸 1. United States: US SEC Climate Disclosure Rules

In March 2022, the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) announced proposed climate-risk disclosure requirements to expand annual reporting (Form 10-K) for publicly traded companies.

  • TCFD-Guided Disclosures: Companies must report financially material climate-related physical and transition risks.
  • Climate Risk Governance: The company's oversight mechanisms and climate risk management processes.
  • Impact on Financials: How identified climate risks impact business strategy, operational performance, and financial estimates.
  • Transition Plans & Scenarios: Details of scenario analysis, decarbonization transition plans, and progress on publicly announced climate targets.
🇬🇧 2. United Kingdom: Net Zero 2050 & SDRs

In 2019, the UK became the first major economy to pass a law legally targeting Net Zero GHG emissions by 2050.

  • Companies Act 2006 (Expanded 2022): Mandates sustainability and climate reporting aligned with TCFD recommendations for large entities (listed, >£500M turnover, or >500 employees).
  • Mandatory Scopes of Reporting: Strategy, due diligence, environmental impacts, workforce well-being, respect for human rights, anti-corruption, and anti-bribery.
  • SECR: Streamlined Energy and Carbon Reporting requires reporting of energy consumption and direct/indirect carbon emissions.
  • SDRs (Sustainability Disclosure Requirements): Introduces an integrated corporate reporting regime for corporates and asset managers.
🇪🇺 3. European Union: The Global ESG Frontier (SFDR, CSRD & CSDDD)

The European Union has enacted the most comprehensive suite of sustainable finance directives:

  • SFDR (Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation - March 2021): Regulates asset managers and financial advisors to prevent greenwashing and classify ESG funds (Article 6, 8 & 9).
  • EU Taxonomy (Delegated Acts Dec 2021): Unified classification system defining environmentally sustainable economic activities.
  • CSDDD (Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive - Feb 2022): Imposes strict duty of care on companies to identify and mitigate human rights and environmental violations across global supply chains.
  • CSRD (Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive): Replaces NFRD and mandates double materiality (impact materiality and financial materiality) based on European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS).

🇮🇳 8. Evolution of ESG in India & The SEBI Mandate
Regulatory Evolution

India's journey towards sustainable corporate governance has transitioned from voluntary philanthropic appeals to rigorous, statutory disclosures backed by mandatory reasonable assurance under SEBI regulations.

📜 The Formative Phase (2009 – 2015)
  • Dec 2009 – MCA CSR Voluntary Guidelines: Earliest policy step by Ministry of Corporate Affairs guiding corporates to integrate social and environmental consciousness into business decisions.
  • 2011 – National Voluntary Guidelines (NVGs): Formulated 9 foundational principles on socio-economic and environmental responsibilities of business.
  • 2012 – SEBI Business Responsibility Report (BRR): Mandated BRR for Top 100 listed entities based on market cap under Listing Agreement.
  • 2014 – Section 135 Companies Act 2013: Mandated statutory Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) spending of 2% net profits for qualifying companies.
  • 2015 – SEBI LODR Regulation 34(2)(f): BRR expanded to Top 500 listed entities.
🚀 The Mandatory Modern Era (2019 – Present)
  • March 2019 – NGRBC Released: MCA revised NVGs into National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC), aligning Indian business ethics with UN SDGs and UNGPs.
  • Dec 2019 – SEBI BRR Expansion: BRR filing extended to Top 1000 listed entities by market capitalization.
  • May 2021 – SEBI BRSR Circular: Replaced BRR with the new, comprehensive Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR). Voluntary for FY 2021-22, mandatory from FY 2022-23 for Top 1000 listed entities.
  • July 2023 – SEBI BRSR Core Circular: Introduced BRSR Core with 9 key ESG attributes requiring Reasonable Assurance and value chain reporting in a phased glide path.
⚠️ Statutory Nexus: Regulation 34(2)(f) of SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015

Under Regulation 34(2)(f) of SEBI LODR, the annual report of the top 1,000 listed entities (calculated based on market capitalization as on 31st March of every financial year) shall contain a Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report describing the initiatives taken by them from an environmental, social, and governance perspective, in the format as specified by SEBI from time to time.

🏛️ 9. SEBI BRSR Architecture: Sections A, B & C
Structure & Format

The BRSR framework is structured into three clearly defined, distinct sections designed to capture institutional transparency, strategic commitment, and quantitative operational outcomes.

BRSR Section Key Coverage & Disclosures Required Statutory Purpose & Critical Parameters
Section A
General Disclosures		 Basic entity profile, reporting boundary, products & services, business locations, employees & workforce diversity, holding, subsidiaries & associates, CSR details, and transparency grievances. Establishes the reporting boundary and corporate demographics. Discloses details of permanent and contract employees, gender diversity ratios, differently-abled staff, and turnover rates.
Section B
Management & Process Disclosures		 Questions on policies, management oversight, governance structure, board review mechanisms, leadership accountability, and stakeholder consultation. Evaluates whether the company has approved, documented policies for all 9 NGRBC principles, whether policies are signed off by the Board, specific goals/targets set, and grievance redressal mechanisms established.
Section C
Principle-Wise Performance Disclosures		 Comprehensive quantitative and qualitative Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) mapped to each of the 9 Principles of NGRBC. Divided strictly into two indicator categories:
1. Essential Indicators (Mandatory): Environmental metrics (Scope 1 & 2 emissions, energy, water, waste), social safety metrics, training hours, minimum wages.
2. Leadership Indicators (Optional): Scope 3 GHG emissions, Life Cycle Assessments (LCA), biodiversity footprint, supply chain audits, employee well-being beyond statutory mandates.
📌 Essential vs. Leadership Indicators: Crucial Exam Distinction

In Section C, Essential Indicators are strictly mandatory for all top 1000 listed entities. Omission of essential indicators constitutes non-compliance with SEBI LODR. In contrast, Leadership Indicators are voluntary/optional; they represent aspirational governance. A company omitting Leadership Indicators (e.g., omitting Scope 3 or LCA) remains fully compliant with mandatory BRSR filing requirements (refer to TYK Question 19).

⚖️ 10. The 9 Principles of NGRBC in Depth & SDG Mapping
NGRBC Principles & Mapping

The National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC), released by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, form the philosophical and operational backbone of BRSR. Every corporate action is tested against these 9 fundamental principles:

Principle 1: Ethics, Transparency & Accountability

Core Theme: Businesses should conduct and govern themselves with integrity, and in a manner that is Ethical, Transparent, and Accountable.

  • Establish governance structures to promote ethical conduct and transparency across all levels.
  • Implement effective anti-corruption and anti-bribery policies.
  • Timely and transparent disclosures on business decisions, financial, and sustainability metrics.
Principle 2: Safe & Sustainable Goods & Services

Core Theme: Businesses should provide goods and services in a manner that is sustainable and safe across their entire life cycle.

  • Design products reducing resource consumption and environmental footprint across life cycle.
  • Conduct Life Cycle Assessments (LCA) for major products and recycle materials.
  • Ensure safe handling, storage, and disposal of hazardous waste and packaging.
Principle 3: Employee Well-being

Core Theme: Businesses should respect and promote the well-being of all employees, including those in their value chains.

  • Equal opportunity, zero discrimination, and fair living wages (no delayed payments).
  • Safe and healthy workplace environments free from occupational hazards.
  • Work-life balance, maternity/paternity leave, skill development, and freedom of association.
Principle 4: Stakeholder Responsiveness

Core Theme: Businesses should respect the interests of and be responsive to all stakeholders, especially disadvantaged and vulnerable groups.

  • Identify all internal and external stakeholders systematically.
  • Understand stakeholder concerns through structured engagement channels.
  • Special responsiveness to marginalized, vulnerable, and underrepresented communities.
Principle 5: Respect Human Rights

Core Theme: Businesses should respect and promote human rights across all operations and relationships.

  • Adhere to the Constitution of India, Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and UNGPs.
  • Zero tolerance for child labour, forced labour, or modern slavery.
  • Effective grievance redressal mechanisms for human rights abuses.
Principle 6: Protection & Restoration of Environment

Core Theme: Businesses should respect and make efforts to protect and restore the environment.

  • Precautionary approach to environmental challenges and climate risks.
  • Reduction of GHG emissions (Scope 1, Scope 2), water stewardship, and energy efficiency.
  • Responsible effluent treatment, circular economy, and conservation of biodiversity.
Principle 7: Responsible Public Policy Advocacy

Core Theme: Businesses, when engaging in influencing public and regulatory policy, should do so in a manner that is responsible and transparent.

  • Advocacy through trade bodies, chambers of commerce, or direct submissions.
  • Promote public interest, fair competition, and sustainable development rather than narrow sectoral gains.
  • Disclose affiliations with industry chambers and public policy submissions.
Principle 8: Inclusive Growth & Equitable Development

Core Theme: Businesses should promote inclusive growth and equitable development.

  • Mitigate adverse impacts on local communities, land acquisition, and involuntary displacement.
  • Align CSR interventions with local developmental priorities and vulnerable groups.
  • Respect traditional knowledge and ensure fair and equitable benefit sharing.
Principle 9: Consumer Value & Responsible Marketing

Core Theme: Businesses should engage with and provide value to their consumers in a responsible manner.

  • Truthful, non-misleading advertisements and complete disclosure of product contents and hazards.
  • Data privacy and protection of customer personal information.
  • Transparent, accessible customer grievance redressal and feedback mechanisms.

🎯 11. BRSR Core & The 9 ESG Assurance Attributes
SEBI Circular July 2023

To combat greenwashing and provide institutional investors with verifiable, high-integrity sustainability data, SEBI introduced BRSR Core in July 2023. BRSR Core is a targeted subset of BRSR consisting of 9 Key ESG Attributes with precise, standardized Key Performance Indicators (KPIs).

📋 SEBI Phased Glide Path for BRSR Core & Reasonable Assurance

SEBI has mandated that listed entities must obtain Reasonable Assurance on BRSR Core indicators according to the following statutory timeline:

  • FY 2023–24: Top 150 listed entities by market capitalization.
  • FY 2024–25: Top 250 listed entities by market capitalization.
  • FY 2025–26: Top 500 listed entities by market capitalization.
  • FY 2026–27: Top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalization.

Value Chain Disclosures: Top 250 listed entities must disclose ESG metrics of their value chain (comprising top upstream and downstream partners representing 75% of purchases/sales) on a comply-or-explain basis from FY 2024-25, with limited assurance starting in FY 2025-26.

The 9 Key ESG Attributes under BRSR Core

Sr. No. ESG Attribute Specific Metrics / Parameter Measured Reasonable Assurance Focus & Verification Objective
1 Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Footprint Scope 1 (Direct emissions from owned/controlled sources) and Scope 2 (Indirect emissions from generation of purchased electricity/heat). GHG Intensity per rupee of turnover. Auditor checks emission factor sources, fuel bills, utility statements, emission meters, and calibration logs to verify quantitative calculation.
2 Water Footprint Total water withdrawn, water consumed, water discharged, and water recycled/reused. Water intensity per rupee of turnover. Inspection of water meter logs, zero liquid discharge (ZLD) certifications, effluent discharge permits, and local municipality water supply contracts.
3 Energy Footprint Total energy consumed from renewable sources vs. non-renewable sources. Energy intensity per unit of revenue. Verify renewable energy purchase obligations (RPOs), on-site solar/wind generation meters, grid bills, and energy audit certificates.
4 Waste Management Total hazardous and non-hazardous waste generated; disposal methods (recycled, re-used, incinerated, landfill). Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) compliance. Verify authorized vendor manifest slips for hazardous waste, EPR compliance filings with State Pollution Control Boards (SPCB), and waste recycling weighbridge receipts.
5 Well-being of Employees & Workers Health insurance, life insurance coverage, annual health check-ups, maternity & paternity benefits, workplace injuries, lost time injury frequency rate (LTIFR). Cross-check payroll data against insurance policies, ESIC returns, incident investigation registers, and safety training attendance records.
6 Gender Diversity Ratio of women employees & workers; percentage of female board directors and Key Managerial Personnel (KMP); median remuneration of female vs. male employees. Examine HR payroll registers, Form MGT-7, appointment letters of independent women directors, and equal pay remuneration audits.
7 Inclusive Development & CSR Input-output impact assessment of CSR projects in aspirational districts; percentage of local procurement from MSMEs / small producers. Audit independent impact assessment reports (under Rule 8(3) of CSR Rules), vendor master for Udyam MSME registration, and local community beneficiary receipts.
8 Fairness in Engaging with Customers & Suppliers Days of accounts payable to MSMEs; percentage of supplier dues settled within statutory timelines (45 days under MSMED Act, 2006); customer grievance redressal rates. Reconcile MSME aging disclosures in financial statements (Schedule III) with BRSR vendor records and verified customer complaint resolution logs.
9 Open-ness & Business Conduct Concentration of purchases/sales with related parties; details of fines, penalties, settlements paid to regulatory bodies (SEBI, RBI, MCA, CCI, NGT). Verify Related Party Disclosures under Ind AS 24, legal representation letters, litigation tracking registers, and public regulatory search databases.
🛡️ 12. Assurance on BRSR, SSAE 3000 & Greenwashing Risks
Audit & Assurance

As sustainability reporting evolves into a mainstream corporate governance requirement, the risk of greenwashing — the practice of making unsubstantiated, exaggerated, or misleading claims about the environmental or social benefits of a product, service, or company — has elevated independent assurance into an absolute necessity.

📜 ICAI SSAE 3000: The Umbrella Assurance Standard

The ICAI issued SSAE 3000: Assurance Engagements on Sustainability Information to establish mandatory ethical, engagement, and performance standards for auditors.

  • Nature of Standard: Umbrella standard applicable to all assurance engagements on non-financial sustainability information, including BRSR and BRSR Core.
  • Scope of Engagement: Applies to engagements providing either Reasonable Assurance (positive opinion: "In our opinion, the sustainability information is prepared, in all material respects...") or Limited Assurance (negative assurance: "Nothing has come to our attention that causes us to believe...").
  • Effective Date:
    • Voluntary: Assurance reports covering financial periods ending on 31st March 2023.
    • Mandatory: Assurance reports covering financial periods ending on or after 31st March 2024.
💨 ICAI SAE 3410: GHG Assurance Engagements

Complementing SSAE 3000, the ICAI issued SAE 3410: Assurance Engagements on Greenhouse Gas Statements.

  • Target Focus: Verification of quantification and reporting of direct emissions (Scope 1), indirect energy emissions (Scope 2), and value chain emissions (Scope 3).
  • Technical Rigor: Auditor assesses emission factors (IPCC guidelines, Central Electricity Authority grid factors), calibration of measurement equipment, and conversion formulas.
  • Uncertainty Evaluation: Evaluates estimation uncertainty and inherent scientific measurement variance in carbon accounting.

Official 5-Step Methodology to Provide Assurance on BRSR

The ICAI framework prescribes a structured, five-stage methodology for assurance practitioners conducting ESG and BRSR audits:

Stage Methodology Phase Key Audit Procedures & Assurance Verification Actions
Phase 1 Preliminary Review of ESG Report & Scope Understand the reporting boundary, materiality assessment, organizational scope (operating entities, plants, subsidiaries), applicable regulatory frameworks, and identify key performance indicators (KPIs) subject to assurance.
Phase 2 On-site Assessment & Document Review Conduct physical site visits to key manufacturing plants, verify meters, examine operational logbooks, interview site engineers and HR personnel, inspect internal controls over non-financial data gathering, and test sample evidence.
Phase 3 Review of Responses & Clarifications Issue audit query sheets, review management responses and clarifications regarding data discrepancies, variances between operational logs and consolidated ERP dashboards, and evaluate corrective actions.
Phase 4 Preparation & Verification of Findings Synthesize audit findings, compute final verified results, perform analytical reviews against industry benchmarks and prior-year metrics, formulate audit recommendations, and draft the Assurance Report.
Phase 5 Issuance of Assessment Report & Statement Obtain signed management representation letter (MRL), conclude on whether sustainability disclosures are materially correct, and issue the formal independent Assurance Report and Assessment Statement for public disclosure.
⚠️ Mitigating Greenwashing: The Auditor's Professional Skepticism

Greenwashing poses severe regulatory, legal, and reputational hazards. Common greenwashing practices include:

  • Hidden Trade-offs: Labelling a product "eco-friendly" based on a single narrow environmental attribute while ignoring massive toxic waste or carbon intensity.
  • No Proof / Vagueness: Making broad claims like "100% natural", "zero-carbon", or "socially conscious" without accessible third-party certifications or verifiable scientific data.
  • Selective Boundary Setting: Excluding high-polluting subsidiaries or outsourced manufacturing plants from the BRSR reporting perimeter.
  • Auditor Safeguard: The assurance provider must exercise heightened professional skepticism, independently test source documentation, inspect meter calibration logs, and review external stakeholder complaints.

🏛️ 13. ICAI Initiatives: SRSB, Social Stock Exchange & SAS 100–1600
Social Audit Standards

The Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) has played a pioneering institutional role in driving sustainability governance, establishing the Sustainability Reporting Standards Board (SRSB), and creating the normative framework for the Social Stock Exchange (SSE) in India.

🌱 Sustainability Reporting Standards Board (SRSB)
  • Formed by ICAI in 2020 to develop sustainability reporting standards, audit guidance, and research papers tailored to the Indian economic landscape.
  • Formulated and issued SSAE 3000, SAE 3410, the Certificate Course on BRSR, and Technical Guides on BRSR Core assurance.
  • Works actively with SEBI, MCA, and international standard setters (ISSB, GRI) to harmonize Indian sustainability practices with global frameworks.
📈 Social Stock Exchange (SSE) & Social Auditors

📉 14. Climate-Related Risks in Financial Statement Audit & Case Study
Ind AS & SA Interplay

The statutory auditor's primary objective under SA 200 is to obtain reasonable assurance whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement. In modern auditing, climate-related risks cannot be relegated to mere narrative footnotes — they have direct, quantifiable financial impacts across the statement of profit and loss and balance sheet.

🌪️ 1. Physical vs. Transition Climate Risks
  • Physical Risks: Arise from climate changes.
    • Acute: Extreme event-driven hazards such as floods, cyclones, severe droughts, and wildfires causing physical destruction of manufacturing assets.
    • Chronic: Longer-term shifts such as rising temperatures, sea-level rise, and chronic water stress affecting agricultural yields or factory cooling capacities.
  • Transition Risks: Arise from society's transition towards a low-carbon, net-zero economy.
    • Policy & Legal: Carbon pricing, emission caps, phasing out of fossil-fuel vehicles or toxic chemicals, and environmental litigation.
    • Technology: Greener, cheaper renewable alternatives making legacy carbon-intensive production lines economically obsolete.
    • Market & Reputation: Shifts in consumer preferences and investor capital away from carbon-intensive entities.
🏭 2. Sectors with Heightened Climate Risk Exposure

While every corporate entity experiences environmental impacts, the auditor must apply intensive scrutiny when auditing entities in the following high-impact sectors:

  • Energy & Utilities: Thermal power, oil & gas exploration, coal mining (stranded asset risks).
  • Transportation: Automotive manufacturing, commercial aviation, marine shipping (stringent emission mandates).
  • Materials & Buildings: Steel, cement, chemicals, construction (intensive carbon footprint and high process emissions).
  • Agriculture, Food & Forestry: Agrochemicals, timber, commercial farming (water stress, pesticide toxicity bans, soil degradation).
  • Banking & Insurance: Underwriting risk for catastrophic weather events and loan defaults in carbon-heavy borrower portfolios.

Interplay Between Climate Risks and Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS)

Accounting Standard Specific Climate Risk Channel Mandatory Auditor Procedures & Scrutiny
Ind AS 36
Impairment of Assets		 Climate policies, emission restrictions, or carbon taxes may reduce future cash flows or cause premature economic obsolescence of carbon-intensive plants, machinery, or Cash Generating Units (CGUs). Assess whether climate risks represent an impairment indicator. Test management's value-in-use assumptions (discount rates, terminal growth rates, and carbon price forecasts) for consistency with declared sustainability targets.
Ind AS 16 / Ind AS 38
PPE & Intangibles		 Regulatory phase-outs of polluting technologies (e.g., combustion engines or toxic chemical plants) or technological obsolescence may dramatically reduce useful economic lives and residual values. Review whether management has revised depreciation periods and amortization schedules. Verify if stranded assets have been decommissioned and written off.
Ind AS 2
Inventories		 Bans on toxic chemicals, non-compliant packaging, or shifts in consumer demand may cause inventory of finished goods or raw materials to become unsellable or obsolete. Verify whether inventory is valued at lower of cost and Net Realizable Value (NRV). Check for slow-moving stocks facing regulatory bans or expiry.
Ind AS 37
Provisions & Contingencies		 Legal or constructive obligations to clean up contaminated industrial land, restore mining sites, pay environmental fines, or settle toxic emission lawsuits. Onerous supply contracts. Inspect environmental inspection notices, pollution control orders, legal correspondence, and determine if reliable estimates exist requiring provision vs. contingent liability disclosure.
Ind AS 109
Financial Instruments		 Lending institutions face increased credit risk and loan defaults from corporate borrowers operating in climate-vulnerable or carbon-intensive industries. Test whether banks and NBFCs have factored forward-looking climate risk scenarios into their Expected Credit Loss (ECL) probability of default (PD) calculations.
🔍 Auditing Standards Interplay: SA 315, SA 570, SA 701 & SA 720
  • SA 315 (Risk Assessment): The auditor must understand the entity's regulatory and environmental environment, identifying whether climate risks create risks of material misstatement.
  • SA 570 (Going Concern): Immediate climate-related events (severe flood destroying sole plant, cancellation of environmental operating licence) or inability to obtain insurance may cast significant doubt on going concern.
  • SA 701 (Key Audit Matters): Climate matters requiring significant management estimation (e.g., valuation of long-lived assets subject to energy transition) should be communicated as KAM in listed company audit reports.
  • SA 720 (Auditor's Responsibilities Relating to Other Information): The auditor is statutorily required to read the Other Information (including BRSR, Sustainability Reports, and Chairman's statement in the Annual Report) to identify any material inconsistency between the non-financial sustainability narratives and the audited financial statements. If a company claims zero carbon emissions by 2030 in its BRSR but reflects zero capital expenditure or R&D provisions in its financials, an inconsistency arises!
📖 Comprehensive ICAI Case Study: Agrochemical Sector – Company A vs. Company B

Industry Background: The Indian agrochemical sector is a $35 billion industry, segmented by product type (fertilizers, pesticides, adjuvants, plant growth regulators) and application. India is a prominent global exporter. The Central Insecticide Board (CIB) categorizes agrochemical toxicity using a 4-color labeling system: Red (Extremely Toxic), Yellow (Highly Toxic), Blue (Moderately Toxic), and Green (Slightly Toxic). Counterfeits account for 25% of sales.

Company A vs. Company B Facts: Both are top 1,000 listed entities.

  • Company A: Proactively aligns with evolving ESG guidelines. In Dec 2025, it eliminated red-labeled products and in 2025 discontinued yellow-labeled products. Increased R&D spending for sustainable alternatives and upgraded backend packaging with interactive barcodes and multilingual instructions. Revenue initially dropped by 8%, but the entity is shielded against impending regulatory bans.
  • Company B: Focuses strictly on short-term revenue and profits. 14% of top-selling products are red- and yellow-labeled. Witnessed 9% YoY revenue growth and plans to expand production of its bestselling insecticide DDT (red-labeled). Company B has now been approached by the regulatory authority for an active investigation requiring extensive tests to prove products have no adverse health effects.

Case Study Questions & ICAI Master Solutions:

Sub-Question 1: What are the reporting requirements for each of the two companies? ▼

Statutory Reporting Requirements under SEBI & Ind AS:

  • Both Company A and Company B fall within the top 1,000 listed entities in India. Consequently, under Regulation 34(2)(f) of SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015, both companies must mandatorily furnish a Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) as part of their Annual Report.
  • Principle-Wise Performance Disclosures (Section C): Both entities must report on all 9 NGRBC principles, with special emphasis on Principle 2 (safe and sustainable products across life cycle), Principle 6 (environmental protection and hazardous waste discharge), and Principle 9 (consumer safety, safe usage instructions, and non-misleading labeling).
  • BRSR Core: Depending on whether they fall within the top 150/250/500 thresholds, they must obtain Reasonable Assurance on BRSR Core attributes.
  • Financial Statement Disclosures (Ind AS): Both entities must disclose environmental expenditures, R&D investments, and any legal contingencies or provisions in compliance with Ind AS 37 and Ind AS 38.
Sub-Question 2: Which company has absorbed the impacts of future regulatory changes and what steps were taken? ▼

Company A has successfully absorbed the impacts:

  • Absorption of Regulatory Transition: By taking an initial revenue decline of 8% to systematically phase out toxic chemicals, Company A has eliminated structural dependency on products that face imminent legal bans under global treaties and tightening Indian environmental standards.
  • Concrete Steps Taken by Company A:
    • Eliminated red-labeled products in Dec 2025 and discontinued yellow-labeled products in 2025.
    • Substantially increased Research and Development (R&D) spending to innovate green, non-toxic bio-agrochemical alternatives.
    • Upgraded backend digital infrastructure to implement unique interactive barcodes on packaging, enabling farmers to verify product authenticity and prevent counterfeit usage.
    • Published multilingual usage instructions to overcome language barriers and prevent farmer health hazards.
Sub-Question 3: What would be the consideration by the auditors of Company A and Company B in the audit of financial statements? ▼

Considerations for Auditors of Company A:

  • Ind AS 36 (Impairment): Verify if the discontinuation of red and yellow products resulted in idle manufacturing plant and machinery requiring an impairment loss write-down.
  • Ind AS 2 (Inventories): Check whether any remaining stocks of red/yellow chemicals or packaging materials have been written down to Net Realizable Value (NRV).
  • Ind AS 38 (Intangibles / R&D): Audit increased R&D expenditure to determine whether costs are properly classified as research expenses (charged to P&L) or development assets (capitalized under Ind AS 38 criteria).
  • SA 720: Review BRSR disclosures regarding product greening for consistency with financial line items.

Considerations for Auditors of Company B:

  • Ind AS 37 (Provisions & Contingencies): Since Company B faces an active regulatory investigation, the auditor must assess whether an outflow of economic resources is probable (requiring a provision for fines/recalls) or possible (requiring disclosure as a Contingent Liability).
  • Ind AS 36 (Impairment of DDT Plant): If DDT and red-labeled chemicals face impending bans, dedicated production lines may suffer sudden obsolescence and require substantial impairment write-offs.
  • SA 570 (Going Concern): Since red/yellow products constitute 14% of revenue and include bestselling items, an adverse regulatory outcome could threaten business viability. The auditor must assess going concern assumptions.
  • SA 701 (KAM) & SA 720: Consider communicating the regulatory investigation as a Key Audit Matter (KAM). Reconcile BRSR environmental claims against the regulatory scrutiny.

Q3: What are the prominent global trends in sustainable reporting? ▼

Key Global Trends Shaping Sustainability Disclosures:

  • Transition from Voluntary to Mandatory: Global regulators (EU, US SEC, UK FCA, SEBI) have made non-financial ESG reporting compulsory for large and listed companies.
  • Consolidation of Global Standard Setters: Establishment of the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) by the IFRS Foundation to formulate a comprehensive global baseline (IFRS S1 and IFRS S2).
  • Double Materiality Doctrine: Championed by the EU CSRD, requiring entities to report both how climate/ESG issues impact their business (financial materiality) and how their operations impact the planet and society (impact materiality).
  • Value Chain Diligence: Legislative mandates (e.g., EU CSDDD and SEBI BRSR Core) extending due diligence and disclosure obligations across upstream and downstream supply chains.
  • Independent Assurance Imperative: Increasing regulatory mandates requiring third-party limited and reasonable assurance (under SSAE 3000 / IAASB ISSA 5000) to eradicate greenwashing.
Q4: What is the methodology of providing assurance on BRSR? ▼

5-Step Methodology under ICAI Guidelines & SSAE 3000:

  1. Phase 1 – Preliminary Review: Review the entity's ESG report, determine engagement scope, understand materiality assessment, and identify assurance criteria.
  2. Phase 2 – On-site Assessment & Document Review: Conduct physical site inspections, review data measurement systems, test calibration records, inspect operational logs, and evaluate internal controls over sustainability reporting.
  3. Phase 3 – Review of Responses & Clarifications: Issue observations/queries to management and evaluate written clarifications and corrective evidence.
  4. Phase 4 – Verification & Assessment Report Preparation: Verify synthesized metrics against source documents, evaluate benchmark deviations, formulate audit findings, and draft recommendations.
  5. Phase 5 – Issuance of Final Report & Statement: Secure management representation letter and issue the formal independent Assurance Report / Statement.

  • 4. Environmental Opportunities: Transition to clean technology, green building standards, and adoption of renewable energy.
Q21: Omega Technologies (AI R&D patents, employee AI training, community digital literacy, tech volunteering). Classify capitals. ▼

Classification under Integrated Reporting (<IR>) Framework:

  • (i) Investment in R&D for AI products & Patents Registered: Primary contribution is to Intellectual Capital (patents, software IP, algorithms, and technical know-how).
  • (ii) Advanced AI Training Conducted for Employees: Primary contribution is to Human Capital (enhancing workforce skills, capabilities, and innovative competencies), which in turn feeds Intellectual Capital.
  • (iii) Community Digital Literacy Programs in Rural Areas: Primary contribution is to Social and Relationship Capital (building community goodwill, rural trust, and societal development).
  • (iv) Employee Volunteering for Non-Profit Technology Solutions: Primary contribution is to Social and Relationship Capital (external social impact) and secondary benefit to Human Capital (employee engagement and purpose).

2. Limited Assurance vs. Reasonable Assurance (SSAE 3000 / SAE 3410)

Comparison Dimension Limited Assurance Engagement Reasonable Assurance Engagement
Engagement Objective Reduction in engagement risk to an acceptable level (higher risk than reasonable assurance). Reduction in engagement risk to an acceptably low level as the basis for a positive form of conclusion.
Nature of Procedures Primarily limited to inquiries of management, analytical procedures, and high-level evaluation of data consistency. Comprehensive audit procedures including tests of operating controls, physical on-site inspections, third-party recalculations, and meter calibration tests.
Opinion Formulation Negative Form: "Nothing has come to our attention that causes us to believe that the sustainability report is not prepared, in all material respects..." Positive Form: "In our opinion, the sustainability information is prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable reporting criteria."
BRSR Core Mandate Permitted for value chain disclosures for top 250 listed entities in initial phases (FY 2025-26). Mandatorily required by SEBI for BRSR Core indicators across Top 150 → 250 → 500 → 1000 entities.

3. Paradigm Shift: Financial vs. Integrated vs. BRSR Reporting

Feature Traditional Financial Report Integrated Report (<IR>) SEBI BRSR Reporting
Primary Audience Shareholders, banks, and creditors. Providers of financial capital and strategic stakeholders. Regulators (SEBI), investors, employees, society, and consumers.
Information Focus Historical financial performance (profit, assets, liabilities). Multi-capital value creation (6 Capitals) across short, medium, and long term. Operational performance across 9 NGRBC principles and 9 BRSR Core attributes.
Regulatory Mandate Companies Act 2013, Ind AS / AS, SEBI LODR. Voluntary globally (encouraged by SEBI circular 2017 for top 500). Mandatory for Top 1000 listed entities under SEBI LODR Regulation 34(2)(f).
