Indian Accounting Standard 115: Revenue from Contracts with Customers

Every business exists to earn revenue — whether it sells a samosa, builds a highway, streams movies, or installs enterprise software. Ind AS 115 replaces old piecemeal rules (Ind AS 11 & Ind AS 18) with one crystal-clear 5-Step Model. Explained below so clearly that even a 10th-class student can picture every concept, alongside every rule, formula, all 75 Illustrations, all 8 Examples, 7 TYKs, HUL Extract, AS 7/9 comparison, and 16 interactive MCQs.

Big Picture • Sections 3 & Core Principle

What Is Ind AS 115 Really About? (The 10th-Class Story)

Before memorising paragraphs, imagine you run a coaching + book + tablet bundle shop. When exactly can you write "I earned this money as Revenue" in your notebook?

Think Like a 10th-Class Student — The Mobile Recharge & Cricket Kit Story

Suppose your friend gives you ₹10,000 today for a cricket bat, helmet, and 12 months of weekend coaching. Can you boast today that you "earned ₹10,000"? No! Today you only hold his advance cash (a liability because you still owe him the kit and coaching).

Core Principle of Ind AS 115: Record revenue only when (and as) you hand over control of the promised goods or services to the customer, at the amount you genuinely expect to keep! Every single page of Ind AS 115 answers one of just five questions in order:

Step 1 • Identify the Contract(s) with a Customer: Is this a real, enforceable deal where both sides mean business and you'll actually get paid?
↓
Step 2 • Identify the Performance Obligations (POs): List every separate ("distinct") item/service you promised inside that deal (e.g., Bat + Coaching = 2 promises).
↓
Step 3 • Determine the Transaction Price: How much net cash/value do you truly expect to earn after bonuses, returns, late penalties, interest, or coupons?
↓
Step 4 • Allocate the Transaction Price to POs: Split that total bill fairly among the separate items based on their standalone shop prices.
↓
Step 5 • Recognize Revenue When (or As) Control Transfers: Book each piece of revenue only when control passes — either at a point in time (handing over the bat) or over time (each month of coaching).
Overview & Convergence (Section 3): Issued by MCA (converged with IFRS 15 / FASB ASU 2014-09) effective for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1st April, 2018 (noted in curriculum examples as 20X2). Ind AS 115 completely supersedes and replaces Ind AS 11 (Construction Contracts) and Ind AS 18 (Revenue). Practical expedient: an entity may apply Ind AS 115 to a portfolio of contracts with similar characteristics if results wouldn't differ materially from individual contract accounting.

1. Scope — Who Is In and Who Is Excluded?

Section 1
10th-Class Analogy — Your Shop vs. Partnering with a Friend

Ind AS 115 applies when you sell your ordinary business output to a Customer (a customer buys what your shop normally makes/does). It does not cover bank interest/dividends, renting out buildings, insurance policies, or teaming up with another factory to jointly share profits & risks of inventing a vaccine (a collaboration, not a customer!). And if two oil companies swap petrol in two different cities so both can serve nearby drivers faster, that swap is outside Ind AS 115.

Included: All contracts with a customer to provide goods or services that are outputs of the entity's ordinary activities in exchange for consideration (plus incremental contract acquisition & fulfilment costs relating to in-scope contracts).

4 Explicit Exclusions (Ind AS 115.5):

  • (a) Leases within the scope of Ind AS 116.
  • (b) Insurance Contracts within the scope of Ind AS 117 (note: entity may choose to apply Ind AS 115 to fixed-fee service insurance contracts meeting Ind AS 117 conditions).
  • (c) Financial Instruments & Group Investments within Ind AS 109, 110, 111, 27, 28 (e.g., dividends, interest income on bonds).
  • (d) Non-monetary exchanges between entities in the same line of business to facilitate sales to customers/potential customers (e.g., two oil companies swapping oil barrels in different regions to save transport time). Careful: swapping dissimilar goods/services (like TV ad spots for newspaper ad space — see TYK Q1, or talk-time for electricity — see TYK Q2) IS covered by Ind AS 115!
Partially In-Scope Contracts (Split Rule): Suppose a single contract includes both an equipment lease (Ind AS 116) and maintenance service (Ind AS 115):
• Step (i): If the other Ind AS specifies how to separate/measure its piece (e.g. Ind AS 109 fair value or Ind AS 116 lease split), apply that Standard first and deduct that amount from the transaction price.
• Step (ii): If the other Ind AS gives no separation/measurement rule, apply Ind AS 115 (Step 4 relative SSP) to split the contract.

2. Nine Core Definitions & 4. Transition Options

Sections 2 & 4
10th-Class Analogy — Contract Asset vs. Receivable vs. Contract Liability

Suppose you build a customer's website in 2 parts: Homepage (₹5,000) + Payment Page (₹5,000), and the contract says "Customer pays ₹10,000 ONLY after BOTH pages are finished." After you finish the Homepage, you earned ₹5,000 revenue, right? Can you demand cash today? No — you still have to build the Payment Page first! So today it is a Contract Asset (conditional right). Once both pages are built and you only wait 30 days for the cheque to clear, it becomes a Receivable (unconditional right — only time needs to pass!). What if the customer paid ₹3,000 advance before you started? That's a Contract Liability!

TermStandard Definition & Key Distinction
1. ContractAn agreement between two or more parties that creates enforceable rights and obligations.
2. Contract AssetEntity's right to consideration for goods/services already transferred when that right is conditioned on something other than the passage of time (e.g., future performance).
3. Contract LiabilityEntity's obligation to transfer goods/services for which it has received consideration (or the amount is due) from the customer.
4. CustomerA party that has contracted with an entity to obtain goods/services that are an output of the entity's ordinary activities for consideration.
5. IncomeIncreases in economic benefits (asset inflows/enhancements or liability decreases) increasing equity, other than owner contributions.
6. Performance ObligationA promise in a contract to transfer either: (a) a distinct good/service (or bundle), or (b) a series of distinct goods/services that are substantially the same with the same pattern of transfer.
7. RevenueIncome arising in the course of an entity's ordinary activities.
8. Stand-alone Selling Price (SSP)The price at which an entity would sell a promised good or service separately to a customer.
9. Transaction PriceThe amount of consideration an entity expects to be entitled to in exchange for transferring promised goods/services, excluding amounts collected on behalf of third parties (e.g., GST / sales taxes).

4. Transition Methods (When First Adopting Ind AS 115):

  • (a) Full Retrospective Approach: Restate all prior comparative periods presented as per Ind AS 8 (subject to permitted practical expedients).
  • (b) Modified Retrospective Approach: Do not restate comparative prior years; record cumulative catch-up effect directly in opening retained earnings of the year of initial application.

MCQ 1 • Scope Exclusions
Which of the following non-monetary exchanges IS covered within the scope of Ind AS 115?
A) Two oil companies swapping crude oil in different cities to deliver quickly to nearby retail customers
B) A TV channel exchanging an ad spot for advertisement space in a daily newspaper
C) Two pharma firms sharing R&D risks and future profits equally under a collaboration deal
D) An equipment manufacturer leasing out cranes for 5 years under an operating lease
Correct: B. Ind AS 115.5(d) excludes non-monetary exchanges between entities in the same line of business to facilitate sales to customers (Option A). Swapping dissimilar media services (TV vs Print) falls within Ind AS 115 (TYK Q1). Option C is a collaboration (not a customer), and D is scoped under Ind AS 116.
MCQ 2 • Contract Asset vs Receivable
Ravi Builders finishes the foundation of a factory (Performance Obligation A worth ₹4 lakh) on 31 March. The contract states payment of ₹10 lakh becomes billable only when the roof (PO B) is also installed in June. How should ₹4 lakh be shown on 31 March?
A) Trade Receivable of ₹4 lakh
B) Contract Asset of ₹4 lakh
C) Contract Liability of ₹4 lakh
D) Work-in-progress inventory only; no revenue yet
Correct: B. PO A is already satisfied, so ₹4 lakh revenue is recognised. Because the right to bill/cash depends on completing PO B (something other than mere passage of time), it is a Contract Asset, not an unconditional Receivable.
MCQ 3 • Transaction Price Exclusions
A laptop shop sells a laptop for ₹50,000 + ₹9,000 GST (18%). What is the Transaction Price under Ind AS 115?
A) ₹59,000
B) ₹50,000
C) ₹41,000
D) ₹54,500
Correct: B. Transaction price explicitly excludes amounts collected on behalf of third parties (such as GST / sales taxes collected for the government).

Step 1: Identifying the Contract & Collectability

Para 9–16: Legal enforceability, the 5 contract gateway criteria, cash received on failed contracts, reassessment & contract term

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: Can You Count Pocket Money Before It’s Promised?

Suppose your friend casually says, "Maybe I'll buy your old cycle someday." Can you go spend ₹5,000 today thinking you already sold it? No! Under Ind AS 115, a deal only becomes an accounting "contract" when it is legally binding and passes 5 strict security checks (especially: is the buyer actually willing and able to pay?). And if you sign a gym plan where you can walk away penalty-free after 3 months, the accounting contract is only 3 months, not 12 months!

5.1 Enforceability & The 5 Mandatory Contract Criteria (Para 9–10)

A contract is an agreement between two or more parties that creates enforceable rights and obligations (determined by legal law, not accounting choice). It can be written, oral, or implied by an entity’s customary business practices. However, Step 1 revenue accounting ONLY begins when ALL FIVE conditions are satisfied:

Step 1 Gateway — All 5 Criteria Must Be Met Simultaneously
  1. Approved & Committed: Parties have approved the contract (in writing, orally, or by customary business practice) and are committed to perform their respective obligations.
  2. Identifiable Rights: Entity can identify each party’s rights regarding the goods or services to be transferred.
  3. Identifiable Payment Terms: Entity can identify the payment terms for the goods or services to be transferred.
  4. Commercial Substance: The contract has commercial substance (the risk, timing, or amount of the entity’s future cash flows is expected to change as a result).
  5. Probable Collection: It is probable that the entity will collect the consideration to which it will be entitled in exchange for the goods/services transferred.
Key Rule on Criterion 5 — "Probable" & Price Concessions

Under Ind AS 115, "probable" means "more likely than not" (> 50% likelihood). Assess ONLY the customer’s ability and intention to pay that amount when due. Crucially, if consideration is variable (say you intend to give a price concession), you test collectability of the lower estimated transaction price, NOT the sticker contract price!

Illustration 1 Collectability Assessment with Expected Price Concession (New Way Ltd)

Scenario: New Way Ltd enters into a contract with a customer to sell networking products for ₹1,250,000. Based on past experience with customers in this region (experiencing an economic downturn), New Way Ltd expects it will have to grant a price concession so the customer can make payment, and intends to offer it rather than force full payment. Does Criterion 5 fail?

Answer: Evaluate collectability against the transaction price expected after considering any implicit price concession—NOT against the stated contract price of ₹1,250,000. If New Way Ltd expects to offer a concession (making the expected transaction price lower, e.g., ₹1,000,000) and it is probable the customer has the ability and intent to pay that reduced amount, Criterion 5 is satisfied and the contract enters Ind AS 115 at the lower transaction price.

What If a Contract Fails Step 1 & You Already Received Cash? (Para 15–16)

Check Step 1 Fails

Do not recognise revenue yet; keep assessing if the 5 criteria get met later.

Cash In Contract Liability / Deposit

Recognise any non-refundable cash received as a Liability (NOT revenue).

Exit A No Remaining Obligations

Recognise cash as revenue ONLY when you have no remaining obligations AND substantially all consideration is received & is non-refundable.

Exit B Contract Terminated

Recognise cash as revenue when the contract has been terminated AND the consideration received is non-refundable.

5.2 Reassessment of Step 1 Criteria & 5.3 Contract Term (Para 11–14)

  • Asymmetric Reassessment (Para 13–14): If a contract passes Step 1 at inception, do NOT reassess unless there is an indication of a significant change in facts and circumstances (e.g., severe deterioration in customer’s financial condition). Any stop in revenue recognition is prospective (do not reverse past valid revenue; test existing receivables/contract assets for impairment under Ind AS 109). Conversely, if a contract fails Step 1 at inception, keep checking every reporting date!
  • 5.3 Contract Term & Termination Penalties (Para 11–12): Ind AS 115 applies only to the duration in which parties have present enforceable rights and obligations. If each party has a unilateral enforceable right to terminate a wholly unperformed contract without compensating the other party, no contract exists. If one party can cancel early without a substantive penalty, the contract term ends on that cancellation date!
Illustration 2 Penalty-Free Early Cancellation (Gym Membership)

Scenario: A gymnasium enters into a contract with a new member for 1 year of access at ₹4,500 per month. The member can cancel the contract after 3 months without paying any penalty. What is the contract term?

Answer: Enforceable rights and obligations exist only for the initial 3 months. After month 3, the member can walk away without compensating the gym. Therefore, the contract term under Ind AS 115 is 3 months, followed by month-to-month contracts (or customer renewal options) for months 4–12.

Illustration 3 Right to Cancel Remaining Units (Contractor P — 100 CCTV Cameras)

Scenario: Contractor P contracts to deliver 100 CCTV cameras at ₹1,000 per camera. The customer has a contractual right to cancel the contract at any time after receiving 10 cameras without any penalty. What is the contract scope?

Answer: Enforceable obligations exist only for the first 10 CCTV cameras (Contract = ₹10,000). The remaining 90 cameras are treated as a customer option to purchase additional goods (evaluated to see if there is any material discount/right), not part of the initial enforceable contract.

Quick Check — Step 1 Gateway & Contract Term

1. Entity X signs a 24-month service contract (₹10,000/month), allowing the customer to cancel anytime after 6 months without paying any termination penalty. Also, Entity X received ₹50,000 non-refundable advance from another customer whose contract fails Criterion 5 (collectability) while Entity X has 40% performance left. What is correct?

A) 24-month term for first customer; ₹50,000 non-refundable cash is immediate revenue.
B) 6-month term for first customer; ₹50,000 non-refundable cash is a Contract Liability (deposit) until Criterion 5 is met or Exit A/B occurs.
C) 24-month term for first customer; ₹50,000 is split 60% revenue and 40% liability.
D) 6-month term for first customer; ₹50,000 is immediate revenue because it is non-refundable.
Correct: B. Penalty-free unilateral termination after 6 months caps the enforceable term at 6 months (Para 11). Non-refundable cash received on a contract failing Step 1 cannot be recognised as revenue while performance remains unfulfilled and the contract isn't terminated (Para 15).

Combining Contracts & Contract Modifications

Para 17–21: Bundling linked contracts & the 3 accounting treatments for contract modifications

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: Two Receipts for One Burger Combo & Mid-Way Menu Changes

Combining Contracts: If a shop bills you ₹1 for a burger on Slip 1 and ₹199 for fries on Slip 2 at the same counter just to confuse people, accountants say: "Nice try—combine both slips!"
Modifications: Suppose a tutor agrees to teach 10 hours at ₹500/hr. After 5 hours, you ask for 5 extra hours. Three possibilities happen: (1) Extra hours are charged at full ₹500/hr = Separate brand-new contract; (2) Extra hours are cheaper at ₹400/hr for future standalone classes = Cancel old future & start fresh prospectively; (3) Everything is part of one big unfinished science project = Mix old + new prices and do a Cumulative Catch-Up adjustment today!

5.4 Combining Contracts (Para 17)

Combine two or more contracts entered into at or near the same time with the same customer (or related parties of the customer) and account for them as a single contract if ONE OR MORE of these criteria are met:

  1. Negotiated as a package with a single commercial objective;
  2. Consideration paid in one contract depends on the price or performance of the other contract; OR
  3. Goods or services promised in the contracts (or some in each) form a single performance obligation.
Illustration 4 Do NOT Combine Unrelated Contracts (Fighter Planes vs Spare Parts)

Scenario: An aircraft manufacturer sells 5 fighter planes to a government agency, and on the same day enters a separate contract to supply routine spare parts priced independently at market rates with independent negotiation and performance. Should they be combined?

Answer: No. Entering contracts near the same time with the same customer is not enough by itself. Since neither pricing, commercial objective, nor performance obligation linkage exists among the three Para 17 criteria, account for them separately.

Illustration 5 Combine Linked Software + Customisation (Software Company S)

Scenario: Software company S enters into a contract to license its CRM software to Customer A. Three days later, in a separate contract, S agrees to perform significant customisation of that CRM software for Customer A because the base software won't function in A’s environment without it.

Answer: Combine both contracts. They were signed near the same time with the same customer, and the software licence plus significant customisation form a single performance obligation (Para 17(c)).

Illustration 6 Combine Related-Party Price-Linked Contracts (Manufacturer M)

Scenario: Manufacturer M signs a contract with Entity P and another near the same time with Entity Q (both controlled by the same Government, i.e., related parties under Ind AS 24). P gets a deep volume discount ONLY if Q also buys a specified quantity from M.

Answer: Combine both contracts. Entities P and Q are related parties, the contracts were entered near the same time, and the consideration payable under P’s contract depends on performance/pricing in Q’s contract (Para 17(b)).

5.5 Contract Modifications (Para 18–21)

A modification is an approved change in scope, price, or both. If parties approve a scope change but have not yet determined the price change, estimate the change in transaction price under variable consideration rules. Once approved, apply this 3-path decision framework:

Accounting Treatment Exact Condition (Para 20–21) Impact on Past & Future Revenue
1. Separate Contract (Para 20) Scope increases by adding distinct goods/services AND price increases by their Standalone Selling Price (SSP) (adjusted for contract-specific circumstances like saved selling costs). Original contract untouched. New scope accounted for completely separately.
2. Prospective — Termination of Old + New Contract (Para 21(a)) Does NOT qualify as Separate Contract (e.g., price ≠ SSP), AND remaining goods/services ARE distinct from those already transferred. Blend unrecognised consideration from old contract + new consideration, and allocate prospectively over remaining distinct POs. Past revenue unchanged.
3. Cumulative Catch-up (Para 21(b)) Remaining goods/services are NOT distinct (part of a single partially satisfied PO on the modification date). Update Transaction Price and measure of progress; recognise an immediate cumulative catch-up adjustment (increase or decrease) to revenue on modification date!
Note on Hybrid Modifications (Para 21(c))

If remaining goods/services are a mix of distinct and non-distinct items, apply Para 21(a) and 21(b) consistently: catch-up on the partially satisfied non-distinct PO and allocate remaining consideration prospectively to future distinct POs.

Illustration 7 Modification as Separate Contract (Adjusted SSP)

Scenario: An entity promises to sell 120 products for ₹1,000 each over 6 months. After 60 products are delivered, the contract is modified to add 30 distinct products at ₹950 each (reflecting SSP less ₹50 because no new sales commission/selling costs are incurred).

Answer: Account for the 30 additional products as a Separate Contract (Para 20). Why? (1) The 30 products are distinct, and (2) ₹950 reflects their SSP appropriately adjusted for avoided selling costs. Original remaining 60 units stay at ₹1,000 each (₹60,000); new contract = 30 × ₹950 = ₹28,500.

Illustration 8 Modification as Prospective Termination & New Contract (KLC Ltd)

Scenario: KLC Ltd sells a machine for ₹25,00,000 and 1 year of monthly maintenance services (distinct series) from 1 April at ₹55,000/month. On 1 October (after 6 months completed = ₹3,30,000 recognised), the parties reduce maintenance for the remaining 6 months to ₹45,000/month. How should KLC account on 1 October?

Answer: Maintenance services for the remaining 6 months are distinct from the 6 months already delivered, and the contract price dropped (not priced at SSP for added scope). Apply Para 21(a) Prospective Treatment: terminate old future maintenance and treat remaining 6 months as a new contract for ₹2,70,000 (6 × ₹45,000), recognising ₹45,000/month prospectively. Do NOT adjust the ₹3,30,000 already booked.

Illustration 9 Modification with Cumulative Catch-Up (Growth Ltd — Customised Consulting Hours)

Scenario: Growth Ltd contracts to deliver a single customised project requiring 200 hours at ₹150/hour = ₹30,000. After completing 100 hours (recognising ₹15,000 revenue, 50% complete), the customer adds 50 hours (non-distinct part of the same project) for an extra ₹5,000 (₹100/hour). Calculate the modification-date adjustment.

Answer — Cumulative Catch-Up (Para 21(b)):

  • Revised Total Transaction Price = ₹30,000 + ₹5,000 = ₹35,000
  • Revised Total Hours = 200 + 50 = 250 hours (Blended rate = ₹140/hour)
  • Revised Progress = \( \frac{100}{250} = 40\% \)
  • Cumulative Revenue earned to date = \( 40\% \times 35,000 = \mathbf{₹14,000} \)
  • Revenue already recognised = ₹15,000
  • Modification-Date Catch-Up Adjustment: \( ₹14,000 - ₹15,000 = \mathbf{₹(1,000)} \) (reduce revenue immediately by ₹1,000!). Remaining 150 hours will be recognised at ₹140/hr = ₹21,000.
Quick Check — Contract Modifications

2. An architect contracts to design one custom building for ₹10,00,000. When design is 60% complete (₹6,00,000 revenue recognised), the client modifies the design to add an atrium for an extra ₹2,00,000, making the project only 50% complete under the new total effort. Step 2: Identifying Performance Obligations (POs)

Para 22–30: Explicit & implicit promises, two-step "Distinct" test, Series requirement & Multiple-Element arrangements

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: Flour + Sugar + Eggs vs Cake vs School Uniform + Tie

If you hire a baker to bake your birthday cake, did you buy "separate flour, eggs, and baking effort"? No! The ingredients and baking work together as ONE combined output (a cake = 1 PO). On the other hand, if a school store sells you a blazer, a shirt, and a belt in a combo pack, you can wear each separately and they don't transform each other—so that's 3 separate POs! Always check TWO questions: (1) Can the customer use it on its own? AND (2) Is the seller actually promising a bundle rather than separate bricks?

6.1 Promises in Contracts & Activities That Are NOT POs

  • Explicit vs Implied Promises (Para 24): A promise to transfer a good or service can be written in the contract OR implied by customary business practices, published policies, or specific statements that create a valid expectation in the customer (even if called "free", e.g., free maintenance offered in ads, or free manufacturer warranty/support promised via distributors).
  • What is NOT a Performance Obligation: Administrative tasks to set up a contract (like registering a customer profile or internal onboarding paperwork that transfers no good/service to the customer) are NOT POs.

The 2-Step "Distinct" Test (Para 27–29)

A Good or Service Is DISTINCT Only If BOTH Criteria Pass
  1. Step 6.1.1 — Capable of Being Distinct (Customer Level, Para 27(a)): Can the customer benefit from the good/service either on its own OR together with other readily available resources (sold separately by the entity/others or already obtained)? Selling regularly on a standalone basis proves capability; being useful only as scrap fails!
  2. Step 6.1.2 — Distinct Within the Context of the Contract / Separately Identifiable (Contract Level, Para 27(b) & Para 29): Is the promise to transfer the item separately identifiable from other promises in the contract? Indicators that items are NOT separately identifiable (Bundle into 1 PO):
    • Indicator 1 (Significant Integration): Entity provides a significant service of integrating items into a combined output (e.g., construction materials + engineering + construction = 1 building/plant).
    • Indicator 2 (Significant Modification / Customisation): One item significantly modifies or customises another item (Example 1: modifying accounting software significantly for a customer’s legacy ERP).
    • Indicator 3 (Highly Interdependent / Interrelated — Two-Way Dependency): Items significantly affect each other such that the entity could not fulfil one promise independently without affecting the other. (Beware: One-way dependency alone—e.g., machine + standard consumables or installation that anyone can do—does NOT merge POs!)
Illustrations 10–13 & Ex 1–2 Applying Distinct Test & Mandatory Series Guidance (Water Plant, Telco, Power Plant, IT & Hotel Management)
  • Illustration 10 (Water Purification Plant): Contractor constructs a building and supplies/installs specialised purification equipment with complex testing/integration into a single functioning plant.
  • Illustration 11 (Telecom Bundle): Telco sells 12-month broadband + voice service + standard modem (usable with other ISPs or standard network).
  • Illustration 12 (Power Plant EPC): Entity agrees to design, procure boilers/turbines, construct civil works, and test-commission a power plant (engineering + procurement + construction).
  • Example 1 & 2 + Illustration 13 (Series Requirement — Para 22(b) & 23): (Ex 2) 5-year daily IT transaction processing service; (Illus 13) Multi-year hotel management contract (hiring staff, marketing, accounting, procurement daily).

  • Illus 11 (Broadband + Voice + Modem): 3 POs. Customer can benefit from each on its own or with readily available resources, and none significantly modifies/integrates the others inside the contract.
  • Illus 12 (Power Plant EPC): 1 PO. High interdependence and significant integration of engineering, specialised equipment, and civil construction into one combined output (a working power plant).
  • Ex 1 (Significant Customisation): Base software + significant customisation = 1 PO because customisation modifies the software significantly to create the promised combined output.
  • Ex 2 (IT Processing) & Illus 13 (Hotel Management — Series Rule): Under Para 22(b) & 23, a series of distinct goods/services that are substantially the same and have the same pattern of transfer MUST be treated as a Single PO if: (a) each distinct daily/monthly increment is satisfied over time, AND (b) the same method would measure progress for each increment. Even though daily tasks vary (housekeeping vs marketing vs payroll), the customer receives the same overall daily management/processing service. Result: 1 Series PO.

6.6 Principal vs Agent Considerations (Para B34–B38)

7.1 Transaction Price Overview & Variable Consideration (Para 46–59)

Transaction Price is the amount of consideration an entity expects to be entitled to in exchange for transferring promised goods/services, excluding amounts collected on behalf of third parties (e.g., GST / sales tax). Four elements shape Transaction Price: (1) Variable consideration, (2) Significant financing component, (3) Non-cash consideration, and (4) Consideration payable to a customer.

Sources of Variability: Discounts, rebates, refunds, credits, price concessions, incentives, performance bonuses, penalties (Example 3: ₹1,00,000 if delivered within 30 days, else ₹95,000 → ₹95,000 fixed + ₹5,000 variable!), or contingent events.

Estimation Method (Para 53) When to Use It How to Compute
1. Expected Value (EV) Large number of contracts with similar characteristics OR multiple possible outcomes (sliding scales / daily penalties). Sum of probability-weighted amounts: \( \sum (P_i \times X_i) \).
2. Most Likely Amount (MLA) Contract has only two possible outcomes (binary: win bonus or lose bonus). Single most likely amount in the range of possible consideration.

Include variable consideration in the Transaction Price ONLY to the extent that it is "highly probable" that a significant reversal in cumulative revenue will NOT occur when the uncertainty is later resolved.

5 Factors Indicating High Likelihood of Revenue Reversal (Para 57)
  1. Consideration is highly susceptible to outside factors (market volatility, third-party actions, weather, stock market/commodity prices).
  2. Uncertainty will not be resolved for a long period of time.
  3. Entity has limited experience (or limited predictive value) with similar contracts.
  4. Entity customarily offers a broad range of price concessions or changes payment terms.
  5. Contract has a large number and broad range of possible amounts.

Step 4: Allocating Transaction Price to Performance Obligations

Para 73–90: Standalone Selling Price (SSP), 3 estimation methods, Discount-before-Residual rule, Variable allocation & Changes in TP

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: The School Bag + Pencil Box Combo Discount!

Suppose a School Bag sells alone for ₹800 and a Pencil Box sells alone for ₹200 (Total ₹1,000), but the shop sells the combo for ₹900 (₹100 discount). If you take the bag home today and pick up the pencil box next week, how much revenue does the shop record today? 80% of ₹900 = ₹720 for the bag, and ₹180 next week for the box! Even the ₹100 discount gets shared 80:20—UNLESS the shop has hard proof that only two items in the bundle caused the discount!

8.1 Relative Standalone Selling Price (SSP) & Estimation Methods (Para 76–80)

Default Rule: Allocate the Transaction Price to each distinct PO in proportion to its Standalone Selling Price (SSP) — the price at which the entity would sell that good/service separately to a similar customer. Best evidence = an observable price in standalone sales. If not directly observable, estimate SSP using maximum observable inputs via 3 suitable methods:

Method 1 Adjusted Market Assessment

Look at competitors’ prices for similar goods/services and adjust for your entity’s costs & margins.

Method 2 Expected Cost Plus a Margin

Forecast expected costs of satisfying the PO and add an appropriate margin for that good/service.

Method 3 Residual Approach (Strictly Restricted!)

Total TP minus sum of observable SSPs of other POs. Allowed ONLY if the item’s SSP is highly variable OR uncertain (never previously sold standalone)!

8.1.1 Allocating a Discount (Para 81–83) & Golden Rule on Residual Order

Default: Proportionally across all POs in the contract. Exception (Allocate ENTIRE discount to 1 or a subset of POs) ONLY if ALL THREE conditions hold: (1) Entity regularly sells each distinct item on a standalone basis; (2) Entity regularly sells a bundle of some of those distinct items at a discount to their SSPs; AND (3) The bundle discount is substantially the same as the contract discount (giving observable evidence which POs generated the discount!).

Exam-Crucial Sequence Rule (Para 83)

ALWAYS allocate a qualifying bundle discount BEFORE using the Residual Approach to estimate the SSP of an item with uncertain/highly variable pricing! Furthermore, check if the resulting residual price is zero or unrealistically below the observable price range—if so, the residual approach is invalid and you must switch to Market or Cost+Margin!

Step 5 (Part A): Transfer of Control — Over Time vs Point in Time

Para 31–45: What "control" means, the 3 Over-Time criteria, measuring progress (Uninstalled Materials!) & 5 Point-in-Time indicators

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: Painting Your Own Bedroom Wall vs Ordering a Custom Trophy

When does a seller earn revenue? When the customer gets Control (meaning the customer holds the steering wheel—they can use the item and stop everyone else from using it).
Over Time (3 Doors): You earn revenue bit-by-bit during work if ANY ONE of 3 doors opens:
Door A: Customer enjoys it as you do it (like cleaning a house or daily school bus service);
Door B: You’re building right inside the customer’s land/property (like painting their own house wall);
Door C: The item is so custom-made nobody else could ever use it (like an engraved trophy with their school name)—PLUS you have a legal right to get paid cost + fair profit even if they cancel mid-way! If they only pay back raw material without profit, Door C is SLAMMED SHUT → wait until Point in Time!

9.1 Definition of Control & 9.2.1 Over-Time Revenue Criteria (Para 31–37)

Control = Ability to direct the use of, and obtain substantially all of the remaining benefits from, the asset (including the ability to prevent others from doing so).

Satisfied OVER TIME If ANY ONE Criteria Is Met at Contract Inception (Para 35)
  1. Criterion (a) — Simultaneously Receives & Consumes Benefits: Customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits as the entity performs (e.g., routine cleaning, payroll processing, freight transport). Practical Non-Reperformance Test: Another entity would not need to substantially re-perform the work already completed to date if it took over!
  2. Criterion (b) — Customer Controls Asset as Created/Enhanced: Entity’s performance creates or enhances an asset (e.g., building on customer’s land, upgrading customer’s existing factory) that the customer controls as WIP.
  3. Criterion (c) — No Alternative Use + Enforceable Right to Payment (BOTH Required!):
    • Part 1 (No Alternative Use): Contractual restriction OR practical limitation (rework would cause huge loss) prevents directing the asset elsewhere; AND
    • Part 2 (Enforceable Right to Payment for Performance Completed to Date): At all times throughout the contract, entity has a legally enforceable right to an amount that covers cost incurred + a reasonable profit margin if the customer cancels for reasons other than entity’s non-performance! (Merely keeping deposits/instalments or cost without margin FAILS!).

  • Output Methods: Surveys of performance, appraisals of results, milestones, units produced/delivered. (Only use "right to invoice" practical expedient B16 if invoiced amount directly corresponds to value transferred!).

No, not distinct, customer gets control before installation; (ii) cost is significant relative to total expected costs; (iii) entity procures goods from 3rd party without significant involvement in designing/manufacturing; AND (iv) excluding them prevents distorting progress.

9.2.2 Point in Time Control Indicators (Para 38)

If none of the 3 Over-Time criteria pass, control transfers at a Point in Time. Assess ALL 5 indicators (none individually conclusive):

  1. Entity has a present right to payment;

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: "Pawn Shop Loan", Warehouse Storage & Mickey Mouse vs One Song

9.4 Bill-and-Hold Arrangements (Para B79–B82)

For a customer to obtain control of a product they asked the seller to keep storing physically, BOTH normal Point-in-Time control transfer AND ALL FOUR extra criteria must be met:

  1. Substantive Reason: E.g., the customer specifically requested bill-and-hold (warehouse full / factory delayed).
  2. Identified Separately: Product is clearly tagged/segregated as belonging to the customer.
  3. Ready for Physical Transfer: Product is complete and currently ready for immediate shipment to the customer.
  4. No Ability to Redirect: Entity cannot use the product or direct it to another customer.

Illustration 68 (Machine + Spare Parts + Custodial Care): Entity sells Machine (delivered Year 1) + Custom Spare Parts (paid & held in entity’s warehouse for 2–4 years at buyer’s request, separately tagged & ready). → Identify 3 Distinct POs: (1) Machine, (2) Spare Parts (recognise Point in Time at transfer/segregation!), and (3) Custodial / Storage Services (recognise Over Time over 2–4 years!).

9.5 Licensing of Intellectual Property (Para B52–B63B)

First check if the IP licence is distinct from other goods/services in the contract (e.g., if bundled with critical anti-virus updates or non-separable service, treat together!). For a distinct IP licence, determine the nature of the entity’s promise:

Feature Right to ACCESS IP (Over Time) Right to USE IP (Point in Time)
Nature of IP Dynamic IP — entity’s ongoing activities continuously change the form/functionality or value of the IP. Static IP — customer can use the IP as it exists at the moment the licence is granted (e.g., completed film, 1975 song, standalone software).
3 Cumulative Criteria (Para B58 — ALL 3 Required for Over Time!) 1. Contract requires (or customer reasonably expects) entity will undertake activities that significantly affect the IP;
2. Rights granted directly expose the customer to positive/negative effects of those activities; AND
3. Those activities do NOT transfer a separate good or service to the customer as they occur!
Timing Recognise Over Time across licence period. Recognise Point in Time when customer can first use & benefit from the licence (NOT before licence period begins!).

Para 91–104: Which selling/setup costs get capitalised as Assets vs expensed immediately, amortisation horizon & impairment reversal

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: "Only Winner’s Commission" vs "Travel Costs Even If You Lose!"

Suppose a salesperson pays ₹5,000 for train tickets and hotel to pitch a 5-year deal, plus earns a ₹1,00,000 commission ONLY IF the client signs!
• The train/hotel cost happens win or lose (not incremental!) → Expense immediately!
• The ₹1,00,000 signing commission happens ONLY because the contract was won (incremental!) → Capitalise as a Contract Asset and slowly expense it over the 5 years (plus likely renewal years—say 7 years total!). Shortcut: if the whole amortisation period is 1 year or less, Ind AS 115 lets you expense it upfront to save paperwork!

10.1 Incremental Costs of Obtaining a Contract (Para 91–94)

Rule: Capitalise incremental costs of obtaining a contract ONLY if the entity would NOT have incurred them if the contract had not been obtained AND expects to recover them. (Practical Expedient, Para 94: May expense immediately if amortisation period would be ≤ 1 year.)
Example 8 & AS 9 Contrast: Company pays 1% sales commission on obtaining a 3-year contract (e.g., ₹1 crore upfront acquisition cost → amortised ₹33.33 lakh/year over 3 years under Ind AS 115 vs often expensed upfront under AS 9).

10.2 Costs to Fulfil a Contract (Para 95–98) & 10.3 Amortisation & Impairment (Para 99–104)

Order of Standards: First check if another standard applies (Inventory → Ind AS 2; PPE → Ind AS 16; Intangible Assets → Ind AS 38). Capitalise fulfilment costs under Ind AS 115 ONLY IF NOT covered elsewhere AND ALL 3 pass: (1) Relate directly to an existing or specific anticipated contract; (2) Generate/enhance resources that will be used to satisfy POs in the future; AND (3) Expected to be recovered.
Always Expense Immediately (Para 98): General & administrative costs (unless explicitly chargeable), abnormal wasted materials/labour, costs relating to already-satisfied POs, and costs indistinguishable between satisfied and unsatisfied POs.

🧒 10th-Class Intuition: Building a Government Toll Highway ("BOT Model")

A private company builds a ₹100 crore highway for the Government and runs it for 10 years before handing it back. The builder isn't doing charity—how does it get paid?
Door 1 (Financial Asset — Guaranteed Cash): If the Government promises: "Don't worry about traffic—we guarantee to pay you ₹200 crore fixed annuities over 10 years!" → The builder has an unconditional right to cash → book a Financial Asset (Loan/Receivable) and earn Construction Revenue + Finance Income + Operating Revenue!
Door 2 (Intangible Asset — Toll Risk): If the Government says: "Good luck! Collect tolls directly from passing cars—you keep whatever drivers pay!" → Traffic is risky (no guaranteed cash!) → book a Toll Licence Intangible Asset and amortise it over the concession years as toll cash flows in!

TYK Q2 Software Royalty Cap vs Sales Threshold (A Ltd vs B Ltd — ₹0.50 & ₹2.50 Royalties)

Question: Company X grants an identical 1-year static software licence (Right to Use, transferred on Day 1) to two customers:

Solution: Even though the coupon passes through retailers to end consumers, the ₹10 reimbursement is consideration payable to a customer / variable price concession reducing Entity K’s revenue (Para 70 & Para 50): \[ \text{Expected Reimbursement per Unit} = 25\% \times ₹10 = ₹2.50 \] \[ \text{Transaction Price per Razor} = ₹50.00 - ₹2.50 = \mathbf{₹47.50} \] (Record Bank/Receivable ₹50, Revenue ₹47.50, and Refund/Coupon Liability ₹2.50 per razor!)
