Ind AS 2: Valuation of Inventories

Governs the accounting treatment for inventories: determination of cost, subsequent recognition as an expense (cost of goods sold), any write-down to Net Realisable Value (NRV), and subsequent reversals.

1. Scope and Exclusions

Mandatory Boundaries

Ind AS 2 applies to all inventories, except:

  • Financial instruments covered under Ind AS 109.
  • Biological assets related to agricultural activity and agricultural produce at the point of harvest covered under Ind AS 41 Agriculture.

Measurement Exemptions (Scope excludes only measurement, not disclosure):

  • Producers of agricultural and forest products, agricultural produce after harvest, and minerals / mineral products, to the extent they are measured at Net Realisable Value in accordance with well-established industry practices. When such inventories are measured at NRV, changes in that value are recognized in profit or loss in the period of the change.
  • Commodity broker-traders who measure their inventories at Fair Value less costs to sell (in accordance with Ind AS 113). When such inventories are measured at fair value less costs to sell, changes in fair value less costs to sell are recognized in profit or loss in the period of the change.

2. Definition of Inventories

Three Core Limbs

Under paragraph 6 of Ind AS 2, inventories are assets:

Classification Limb Practical Form Key Exam Recognition Rules
(a) Held for sale in the ordinary course of business Finished goods, trading merchandise Includes primary packing materials necessary to make the product marketable (e.g., bottles, blister packs). Excludes secondary advertising publicity materials.
(b) In the process of production for such sale Work-in-progress (WIP) Includes cost of raw material, direct labour, and systematic allocation of production overheads incurred up to the reporting date.
(c) In the form of materials or supplies to be consumed Raw materials, components, consumables, stores & spares Held for consumption in production or service delivery. Machinery spares that meet the definition of PPE under Ind AS 16 are excluded from inventory!
Core Measurement Axiom (Paragraph 9)
$$\text{Carrying Amount of Inventory} = \min\Big(\text{Cost}, \; \text{Net Realisable Value (NRV)}\Big)$$
Cost is determined using FIFO or Weighted Average. NRV is the estimated selling price less costs of completion and selling expenses.

3. Components of Inventory Cost

Paragraphs 10–18

The cost of inventories comprises all costs of purchase, costs of conversion, and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition.

1
Costs of Purchase (Paragraph 11) Includes purchase price, import duties and other non-recoverable taxes, transport (freight inward), handling and other costs directly attributable to the acquisition of finished goods, materials and services.
Deductions: Trade discounts, rebates and other similar items are deducted in determining the costs of purchase.
Settlement / Cash Discounts: Cash discounts for early settlement do NOT reduce inventory cost under Ind AS; they are classified as financing income (or adjustment to finance cost).
2
Costs of Conversion (Paragraphs 12–14) Includes costs directly related to the units of production (direct labour, direct expenses).
Includes a systematic allocation of fixed and variable production overheads that are incurred in converting materials into finished goods.
3
Other Costs (Paragraph 15) Included in inventory cost only to the extent that they are incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition.
Examples: Non-production design costs for specific customers, borrowing costs for qualifying inventories under Ind AS 23 (e.g. maturing wine or cheese taking over a year).
Rigorous Rules for Production Overheads Allocation (Paragraph 13):
  • Variable Production Overheads: Allocated to each unit of production based on the actual use of the production facilities.
  • Fixed Production Overheads: Allocated based on the normal capacity of the production facilities: $$\text{Allocated Fixed Overhead per unit} = \frac{\text{Budgeted Fixed Overheads}}{\text{Normal Capacity}}$$
  • Low Production or Idle Plant: The amount of fixed overhead allocated to each unit of production is NOT increased as a consequence of low production or idle plant. Unallocated overheads are recognized as an expense in profit or loss in the period in which they are incurred!
  • Abnormally High Production: In periods of abnormally high production, the amount of fixed overhead allocated to each unit of production is decreased so that inventories are not measured above cost.
Strictly Excluded Costs & Expensed in Profit & Loss (Paragraph 16):
  1. Abnormal amounts of wasted materials, labour, or other production costs: Always expensed immediately in P&L.
  2. Storage costs: Expensed unless those costs are necessary in the production process before a further production stage (e.g., storage during wine fermentation or timber maturation is capitalized; storage of finished goods is expensed).
  3. Administrative overheads: That do not contribute to bringing inventories to their present location and condition.
  4. Selling and distribution costs: Always expensed in the period incurred.

Allocation between Joint Products and By-Products (Paragraph 14)

When the costs of conversion of each product are not separately identifiable, total conversion costs are allocated between the products on a rational and consistent basis:

  • Joint Products: Allocated based on relative sales value of each product either at the stage in the production process when the products become separately identifiable (split-off point), or at the completion of production.
  • By-Products: Most by-products are immaterial by nature. When this is the case, they are measured at Net Realisable Value (NRV) and this value is deducted from the cost of the main product. Consequently, the carrying amount of the main product is not materially different from its cost.
Inventories Purchased on Deferred Settlement Terms (Paragraph 18): When an inventory purchase arrangement effectively contains a financing element (e.g., deferred payment beyond normal commercial credit terms), the difference between the purchase price for normal credit terms (cash price equivalent) and the amount paid is recognized as interest expense over the period of financing under Ind AS 109/Ind AS 23, and is NOT included in inventory cost.

4. Cost Formulas and Measurement Techniques

FIFO vs WAC • LIFO Prohibited
Cost Formula Applicability Key Principles & Restrictions
Specific Identification Items not ordinarily interchangeable; goods/services segregated for specific projects. Mandatory for bespoke or custom items. Inappropriate when there are large numbers of items that are ordinarily interchangeable (prevents arbitrary profit manipulation).
FIFO (First-In, First-Out) Ordinarily interchangeable inventories. Assumes that items of inventory that were purchased or produced first are sold first, and consequently items remaining at the end of the period are those most recently purchased or produced.
Weighted Average Cost (WAC) Ordinarily interchangeable inventories. The cost of each item is determined from the weighted average of the cost of similar items at the beginning of a period and the cost of similar items purchased/produced during the period (periodic or moving average basis).
LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) STRICTLY PROHIBITED Ind AS 2 does NOT permit the use of the LIFO formula because it does not reflect modern physical inventory flows and leads to outdated balance sheet values.
Consistency Rule Across Geographical Locations!
Paragraph 25 mandates that an entity must use the SAME cost formula for all inventories having a similar nature and use to the entity! A difference in geographical location of inventories (or in respective tax rules), by itself, is NOT sufficient to justify the use of different cost formulas!

Accepted Measurement Techniques:

  • Standard Cost Method: Takes into account normal levels of materials and supplies, labour, efficiency and capacity utilisation. Standards must be regularly reviewed and, if necessary, revised in the light of current conditions.
  • Retail Method: Widely used in the retail industry for measuring inventories of large numbers of rapidly changing items with similar margins for which it is impracticable to use other costing methods. Inventory is measured by reducing the sales value of the inventory by the appropriate percentage gross margin.

5. Net Realisable Value (NRV) & The Raw Material Exception

Crucial ICAI Exam Focus

Definition (Paragraph 6): Net Realisable Value is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less the estimated costs of completion and the estimated costs necessary to make the sale.

Net Realisable Value Calculation
$$\text{NRV} = \text{Estimated Selling Price} - \Big(\text{Estimated Costs of Completion} + \text{Estimated Costs Necessary to Make Sale}\Big)$$

Key Principles in Determining NRV:

  • NRV is Entity-Specific: NRV refers to the net amount that an entity expects to realise from the sale of inventory in the ordinary course of business. Fair value (Ind AS 113) reflects the price at which an orderly transaction between market participants would take place. NRV may not equal fair value less costs to sell!
  • Firm Sales Contracts: If an entity holds inventory to satisfy firm sales contracts, the NRV of that specific quantity is based on the contract price (less selling costs). If sales contracts are for less than the inventory quantities held, the NRV of the excess is based on general selling prices.
  • Item-by-Item Valuation: Inventories are usually written down to net realisable value item by item. It is not appropriate to write down inventories based on a classification of inventory, for example, finished goods, or all inventories in a particular operating segment.
The Sacred Raw Materials Rule (Paragraph 32):

Materials and other supplies held for use in the production of inventories are NOT written down below cost if the finished products in which they will be incorporated are expected to be sold at or above cost!

However, when there has been a decline in the price of materials and it is estimated that the cost of the finished products will exceed net realisable value, the materials are written down to net realisable value. In such circumstances, the replacement cost of the materials is the best available measure of their net realisable value.

Scenario Finished Goods Selling Price Finished Goods Cost vs NRV Valuation of Raw Material
Case 1 Finished goods expected to sell at or above cost FG Cost $\le$ FG NRV (No loss on FG) Value Raw Material at COST, even if replacement cost is lower than cost!
Case 2 Finished goods expected to sell at a loss FG Cost > FG NRV (Loss anticipated on FG) Write down Raw Material to NRV (measured at its Replacement Cost).

Reversal of Write-Down (Paragraph 33): A new assessment of NRV is made in each subsequent period. When the circumstances that previously caused inventories to be written down below cost no longer exist, or when there is clear evidence of an increase in NRV because of changed economic circumstances, the amount of the write-down is reversed (the reversal is limited to the amount of the original write-down) so that the new carrying amount is the lower of the cost and the revised NRV. The reversal is recognized in profit or loss as a reduction in inventory expense.

Exhaustive Exam Solutions
Illustration 1 • Scope Packing vs Publicity Material
Question: As per Ind AS 2, inventories include "materials and supplies awaiting use in the production process". Examine whether packing material and publicity material are covered by the term "materials and supplies awaiting use in the production process".
Solution:
  1. Packing Materials: Packing materials can be primary or secondary. Primary packing materials are essential to bring the product to its marketable state (e.g., bottles for medicine/liquids, wrappers). These are part of "materials and supplies awaiting use in the production process" and are included in the cost of inventory. Secondary packing materials used solely for delivery/distribution are selling/distribution costs and are expensed.
  2. Publicity Material: Publicity and promotional materials (brochures, posters, banners) are incurred to advertise and promote sales. Paragraph 16(d) specifically provides that selling costs are excluded from the cost of inventories and recognized as an expense. Hence, publicity materials are NOT covered under inventories and must be expensed in P&L when incurred.
Illustration 2 • Purchase Cost Import Duties & Cash Discounts
Question: ABC Ltd. buys 1,000 units of component X from an overseas supplier. Quoted price is ₹1,200 per unit. ABC Ltd. negotiated a trade discount of 5%. The supplier offers an early settlement discount of 2% for payment within 30 days and ABC Ltd. intends to achieve this. Import duties (basic custom duties) of ₹60 per unit must be paid before release through customs. Once released, ABC Ltd. pays delivery cost of ₹5,000 to have components taken to its warehouse. Compute the cost of inventory.
Solution:

Under Ind AS 2, cost of purchase includes purchase price, non-recoverable import duties, and directly attributable transport costs, less trade discounts. Early settlement / cash discount is NOT deducted from inventory cost; it is recognized as finance income upon payment.

Quoted price (1,000 units × ₹1,200)₹12,00,000
Less: Trade discount (5% of ₹12,00,000)(₹60,000)
Net Purchase Price₹11,40,000
Add: Import duties (1,000 units × ₹60)₹60,000

Total Cost of Inventory₹12,05,000
Cost per unit (₹12,05,000 / 1,000 units)₹1,205 per unit

Note: The 2% settlement discount (₹22,800) will be credited to Profit and Loss as other income / finance income when payment is made within 30 days.

Illustration 3 • Overheads Normal Capacity Overhead Allocation
Question: A business plans for production overheads of ₹10,00,000 per annum. The normal level of production is 1,00,000 units per annum. Due to supply difficulties the business was only able to make 75,000 units in the current year. Other costs per unit were ₹126. Calculate the per unit cost and amount of overheads to be expensed during the year.
Solution:
  1. Fixed overhead allocation rate based on normal capacity: $$\text{Rate} = \frac{₹10,00,000}{1,00,000 \text{ units}} = ₹10 \text{ per unit}$$
  2. Allocated overheads to 75,000 units produced: $$75,00,00 \text{ units} \times ₹10 = ₹7,50,000$$
  3. Cost per unit: ₹126 (other costs) + ₹10 (allocated fixed overhead) = ₹136 per unit.
  4. Unallocated fixed overheads expensed in P&L: $$₹10,00,000 - ₹7,50,000 = \mathbf{₹2,50,000}$$ (Paragraph 13 mandates that low production shall not increase the allocated overhead per unit).
Illustration 4 • Conversion Costs Manufacturing Cost Per Unit
Question: ABC Ltd. manufactures control units for air conditioning systems. Each control unit requires: 1 component X @ ₹1,205, 1 component Y @ ₹800, sundry materials @ ₹150. Monthly expenses: Assembly line workers wages ₹2,50,000; Factory supervisors salaries ₹45,000; Depreciation of factory equipment ₹30,000; General administration costs ₹15,000; Factory rent ₹20,000. In the current month, 500 units were manufactured against normal monthly capacity of 500 units. Compute the cost of one completed control unit.
Solution:

Direct materials per unit = ₹1,205 + ₹800 + ₹150 = ₹2,155.

Direct labour per unit = ₹2,50,000 / 500 units = ₹500.

Fixed production overheads = Factory supervisors (₹45,000) + Depreciation (₹30,000) + Factory rent (₹20,000) = ₹95,000.

Fixed overhead per unit = ₹95,000 / 500 = ₹190.

General administration costs of ₹15,000 are excluded from inventory cost and expensed in P&L.

Total Cost per unit: ₹2,155 + ₹500 + ₹190 = ₹2,845 per unit.

Question: A dealer purchased 1,000 cars costing ₹2,80,000 each on deferred payment basis as ₹25,000 per month per car to be paid in 12 equal instalments. Total payment per car = ₹3,00,000. At year end 31 March 20X1, twenty cars are in stock. Compute the cost of inventory, finance cost and cost of goods sold.

  3. Closing Inventory (20 cars): Measured at cash price equivalent = $20 \times ₹2,80,000 = \mathbf{₹56,00,000}$.
  4. Cost of Goods Sold (980 cars sold): $980 \times ₹2,80,000 = \mathbf{₹27,44,00,000}$.

Illustration 14 • NRV & Firm Contract Contracted vs Uncommitted Units
Question: Company P has 100 units of inventory on hand with a carrying amount of ₹10 per unit. Current market price is ₹8 per unit. Company P has a firm sales contract with Company Q to sell 60 units at ₹11 per unit. Incremental selling costs are ₹1 per unit. Compute the Net Realisable Value and final inventory value.
Solution:
  1. For 60 contracted units:
    NRV = Contract price (₹11) - Selling cost (₹1) = ₹10 per unit.
    Cost = ₹10 per unit.
    Value of 60 contracted units = $\min(₹10, ₹10) \times 60 = \mathbf{₹600}$.
  2. For 40 uncommitted units:
    NRV = Market price (₹8) - Selling cost (₹1) = ₹7 per unit.
    Cost = ₹10 per unit.
    Value of 40 uncommitted units = $\min(₹10, ₹7) \times 40 = \mathbf{₹280}$.
  3. Total Value of Inventory: ₹600 + ₹280 = ₹880.
    Inventory write-down charged to P&L = $100 \times ₹10 - ₹880 = \mathbf{₹120}$.
Illustration 16 • Raw Material Exception Finished Goods Selling at a Loss
Question: Raw material inventory consists of 1,000 kg purchased at ₹10 per kg. Replacement cost of raw material on 31 March is ₹9.50 per kg. Further processing costs to convert 1 kg of raw material into finished goods are ₹11. Due to market slump, finished goods can be sold at only ₹20 per kg. Calculate closing inventory of raw material. What if finished goods could be sold at ₹22 per kg?
Solution:
  1. Case 1: Finished goods selling price = ₹20 per kg:
    Estimated cost of finished goods = Raw material cost (₹10) + Conversion cost (₹11) = ₹21 per kg.
    NRV of finished goods = ₹20 per kg.
    Since cost of finished goods (₹21) exceeds NRV (₹20), finished goods will sell at a loss!
    Therefore, applying Paragraph 32, raw materials MUST be written down to their NRV (Replacement Cost).
    Raw material inventory value = 1,000 kg × ₹9.50 = ₹9,500. (Write-down of ₹500 charged to P&L).
  2. Case 2: Finished goods selling price = ₹22 per kg:
    Estimated cost of finished goods = ₹21 per kg.
    NRV of finished goods = ₹22 per kg.
    Since finished goods will be sold at or above cost (₹22 ≥ ₹21), the raw material is NOT written down below cost!
    Raw material inventory value = 1,000 kg × ₹10 = ₹10,000.
Unit 2 • Tangible Non-Current Assets

Ind AS 16: Property, Plant and Equipment

Prescribes the accounting treatment for property, plant and equipment (PPE), covering initial recognition, component accounting, capitalisation of decommissioning liabilities (Appendix A), subsequent measurement models (Cost vs Revaluation), depreciation, and derecognition.

1. Scope and Core Definitions

Paragraphs 2–6

Property, plant and equipment are tangible items that:

  • Are held for use in the production or supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes; and
  • Are expected to be used during more than one period.
Subject Matter Standard Applicable Key Distinctions
Bearer Plants Ind AS 16 A living plant that: (a) is used in production/supply of agricultural produce; (b) is expected to bear produce for more than one period; and (c) has a remote likelihood of being sold as agricultural produce, except for incidental scrap sales (e.g., tea bushes, grape vines, rubber trees). Accounted for under Ind AS 16 using cost model or revaluation model.
Produce growing on Bearer Plants Ind AS 41 Agricultural produce growing on bearer plants (e.g. tea leaves, grapes, latex) remains within the scope of Ind AS 41 (measured at fair value less costs to sell).
Investment Property Ind AS 40 Property held to earn rentals or for capital appreciation (or both) rather than for owner-occupation.
Assets Held for Sale Ind AS 105 Non-current assets classified as held for sale under Ind AS 105 are derecognized from PPE and measured under Ind AS 105.

2. Initial Recognition and Measurement Elements

Paragraphs 7–28

Recognition Criteria (Paragraph 7): An item of PPE is recognized as an asset if, and only if:

  1. It is probable that future economic benefits associated with the item will flow to the entity; and
  2. The cost of the item can be measured reliably.

Initial Measurement: An item of PPE that qualifies for recognition shall be measured at its Cost.

A
Purchase Price Includes purchase price, import duties, and non-refundable purchase taxes, after deducting trade discounts and rebates.
B
Directly Attributable Costs Any costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management:
  • Costs of employee benefits arising directly from construction/acquisition.
  • Costs of site preparation and initial delivery/handling costs.
  • Installation and assembly costs, professional fees (architects, legal).
  • Costs of testing whether the asset is functioning properly.
C
Decommissioning, Restoration and Dismantling Costs (Ind AS 37) The initial estimate of the costs of dismantling and removing the item and restoring the site on which it is located. The entity recognizes an obligation under Ind AS 37 measured at the Present Value of estimated future restoration cash flows, and capitalizes an equal amount into the initial cost of the PPE.
Significant Carve-out: Testing Proceeds Treatment (MCA Notification March 2022):

Under Ind AS 16, net proceeds from selling any items produced while bringing an item of PPE to the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management (e.g. samples produced when testing equipment) are deducted from the directly attributable costs of the asset.

International Carve-out Contrast: IAS 16 was amended internationally to prohibit deducting testing proceeds from asset cost (requiring proceeds and related production costs to be recognized in P&L). India's MCA notified an amendment maintaining that net testing proceeds continue to be deducted from the cost of the PPE. This distinction has consequential impacts on depreciation, impairment, and deferred tax calculations in CA Final examinations!

Costs Excluded from PPE and Expensed in P&L:
  • Costs of opening a new facility or introducing a new product/service (advertising, promotional).
  • Costs of conducting business in a new location or with a new class of customer (staff training).
  • Administration and other general overheads.
  • Costs incurred while an item capable of operating in intended manner has yet to be brought into use or is operated at less than full capacity.
  • Initial operating losses, such as those incurred while demand for the asset's output builds up.
  • Costs of relocating or reorganising part or all of an entity's operations.
  • Incidental operations during construction (e.g., income earned using a building site as a car park prior to construction is recognized in P&L, not capitalized).
Deferred Settlement Credit Calculation
$$\text{Initial Cost of Asset} = \text{Cash Price Equivalent at Transaction Date}$$ $$\text{Total Payments} - \text{Cash Price Equivalent} = \text{Total Interest Expense over Credit Period}$$
Interest is recognized in P&L over the credit period unless capitalized under Ind AS 23.

3. Subsequent Costs and Component Accounting

Mandatory Componentization

Subsequent Costs Framework:

  • Day-to-day Servicing (Repairs & Maintenance): Costs of day-to-day servicing (labour, consumables, minor spare parts) are recognized in profit or loss as incurred.
  • Replacement of Parts: Parts of some items of PPE may require replacement at regular intervals (e.g. furnace lining, aircraft seats). An entity capitalizes the cost of the replacement part if recognition criteria are met, and derecognizes the carrying amount of the replaced part. If the carrying amount of the replaced part is not determinable, the cost of the replacement may be used as an indication of what the cost of the replaced part was at the time it was acquired/constructed (discounted back).
  • Major Inspections: A condition of continuing to operate an item of PPE may be performing regular major inspections for faults (e.g. aircraft overhaul). When each major inspection is performed, its cost is recognized in the carrying amount of the PPE as a replacement if recognition criteria are met. Any remaining carrying amount of the cost of the previous inspection is derecognized.
Mandatory Component Accounting (Paragraphs 43–47):

Each part of an item of property, plant and equipment with a cost that is significant in relation to the total cost of the item shall be depreciated separately.

Example: An airframe and engines of an aircraft must be treated as separate depreciable components if their useful lives or depreciation methods differ. A significant part of an item of PPE may have a useful life and a depreciation method that are the same as the useful life and the depreciation method of another significant part of that same item; such parts may be grouped in determining the depreciation charge.

4. Measurement After Recognition: Cost Model vs Revaluation Model

Paragraphs 29–42

An entity shall choose either the Cost Model or the Revaluation Model as its accounting policy and shall apply that policy to an entire class of property, plant and equipment.

Feature Cost Model (Para 30) Revaluation Model (Para 31)
Subsequent Carrying Amount Cost less accumulated depreciation less accumulated impairment losses. Fair value at the date of revaluation less subsequent accumulated depreciation and subsequent accumulated impairment losses.
Frequency of Revaluation Not applicable. Must be made with sufficient regularity so that the carrying amount does not differ materially from fair value. Annual for volatile assets; every 3–5 years for stable assets.
Entire Class Requirement Applies to entire class. If an item of PPE is revalued, the entire class of PPE to which that asset belongs shall be revalued simultaneously to avoid selective revaluations.

Methods of Adjusting Accumulated Depreciation on Revaluation Date:

1
Gross-up / Proportionate Restatement Method: The gross carrying amount is adjusted in a manner that is consistent with the revaluation of the carrying amount of the asset. The accumulated depreciation at the date of the revaluation is restated proportionately with the change in the gross carrying amount so that the carrying amount of the asset after revaluation equals its revalued amount.
2
Net Elimination Method: The accumulated depreciation is eliminated against the gross carrying amount of the asset and the net amount restated to the revalued amount of the asset.
Accounting for Revaluation Increases and Decreases (Paragraphs 39–42):
  • Revaluation Increase:
    • Recognized in Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) and accumulated in equity under the heading Revaluation Surplus.
    • Exception: To the extent that it reverses a revaluation decrease of the same asset previously recognized in profit or loss, the increase is recognized in Profit or Loss.
  • Revaluation Decrease:
    • Recognized in Profit or Loss.
    • Exception: To the extent of any existing credit balance in the revaluation surplus in respect of that same asset, the decrease is recognized in Other Comprehensive Income (reducing Revaluation Surplus).
  • Transfer to Retained Earnings: The revaluation surplus included in equity may be transferred directly to Retained Earnings when the asset is derecognized, OR on an ongoing basis as the asset is used (the amount transferred being the difference between depreciation based on the revalued carrying amount and depreciation based on the asset's original cost).
    Crucial Rule: Transfers from revaluation surplus to retained earnings are made directly through equity and are NEVER reclassified through Profit or Loss!

6. Depreciation, Useful Life Revision and Derecognition

Paragraphs 43–72
Depreciable Amount Formula
$$\text{Depreciable Amount} = \text{Cost (or Revalued Amount)} - \text{Residual Value}$$
Residual value is the estimated amount an entity would currently obtain from disposal after deducting disposal costs, assuming asset is already of age and in condition expected at the end of its useful life.

Depreciation Rules:

  • Commencement: Depreciation begins when the asset is available for use (i.e. when it is in the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management).
  • Cessation: Depreciation does NOT cease when the asset becomes idle or is retired from active use unless the asset is fully depreciated or is classified as held for sale under Ind AS 105.
  • Annual Review: The residual value, useful life, and depreciation method (SLM, WDV, Units of Production) must be reviewed at least at each financial year-end. If expectations differ from previous estimates, any change is accounted for as a change in accounting estimate in accordance with Ind AS 8 (prospectively adjusted over remaining useful life).

Derecognition (Paragraphs 67–72):

  • The carrying amount of an item of PPE is derecognized on disposal, or when no future economic benefits are expected from its use or disposal.
  • The gain or loss arising from derecognition is the difference between net disposal proceeds and carrying amount, and is recognized in Profit or Loss (cannot be classified as Revenue from operations).
  • Compensation for Impairment: Compensation from third parties (e.g. insurance claims) for items of PPE that were impaired, lost or given up is recognized in profit or loss when the compensation becomes receivable as a separate economic event from the impairment/disposal.

Unit 3 • Interest & Financing Capitalisation

Ind AS 23: Borrowing Costs

Prescribes the mandatory accounting criteria for capitalising interest and other borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition, construction, or production of a qualifying asset, including the capitalisation rate algorithm, foreign exchange adjustments, and group consolidation mechanics.

1. Core Principle and Qualifying Asset Criteria

Paragraphs 1–7

Core Axiom: Borrowing costs that are directly attributable to the acquisition, construction, or production of a qualifying asset form part of the cost of that asset. Other borrowing costs are recognized as an expense in profit or loss in the period in which they are incurred.

Definition of Qualifying Asset (Paragraph 5): A qualifying asset is an asset that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale.

Qualifying Assets (Eligible for Capitalisation) Non-Qualifying Assets (Immediate P&L Expense)
  • Manufacturing plants, factories, and refineries.
  • Power generation facilities and utility infrastructure.
  • Investment properties under construction (Ind AS 40).
  • Intangible assets under development (e.g. software taking years to code).
  • Inventories that require a substantial period of aging/maturation (e.g. wine, cheese, timber, ships).
  • Financial assets covered under Ind AS 109.
  • Inventories manufactured routinely or in large quantities on a repetitive basis over a short period of time.
  • Assets that are ready for their intended use or sale when acquired (e.g. off-the-shelf equipment, ready buildings).
  • Bearer plants that take a short time to mature.

Substantial Period of Time: What constitutes a "substantial period of time" depends on the facts and circumstances of each case. Ordinarily, a period of twelve months is considered as a substantial period of time unless a shorter or longer period can be justified based on technical requirements.

2. Components of Borrowing Costs & Foreign Currency Rule

Paragraph 6(e) • Critical ICAI Calculation

Under paragraph 6, borrowing costs may include:

  1. Interest expense calculated using the effective interest method as described in Ind AS 109.
  2. Finance charges in respect of finance leases recognized in accordance with Ind AS 116.
  3. Exchange differences arising from foreign currency borrowings to the extent that they are regarded as an adjustment to interest costs.
Foreign Currency Borrowing Cost Adjustment (Paragraph 6(e))
$$\text{Adjusted Borrowing Cost} = \min\Big(\text{Actual Exchange Loss}, \; (\text{Interest on Equivalent Local Loan} - \text{Interest on Foreign Loan})\Big)$$
Any remaining exchange loss beyond this cap is treated as an exchange difference under Ind AS 21 and expensed in P&L.
1
Step 1: Compute Foreign Currency Interest Incurred Convert foreign currency interest paid/payable at the applicable exchange rate. This is always a borrowing cost.
2
Step 2: Determine Equivalent Domestic Borrowing Cost Compute what interest would have been payable if the entity had borrowed the principal amount in functional currency (e.g. INR) at domestic market rates.
3
Step 3: Allocate Exchange Loss The exchange loss on repayment/restatement of foreign debt is capitalized as borrowing cost up to the interest differential (Domestic Interest - Foreign Interest). Any surplus exchange loss is expensed under Ind AS 21.

3. Specific vs General Borrowings and Capitalisation Rate

Paragraphs 12–15
Borrowing Type Measurement Rule Investment Income Treatment
Specific Borrowings (Para 12) Funds borrowed specifically for the purpose of obtaining a qualifying asset. $$\text{Capitalised Cost} = \text{Actual Borrowing Costs Incurred} - \text{Temporary Investment Income}$$ Any investment income earned on the temporary investment of specific borrowings pending their expenditure on the qualifying asset is deducted from borrowing costs eligible for capitalisation.
General Borrowings (Para 14) Funds borrowed generally and used for the purpose of obtaining a qualifying asset. $$\text{Capitalised Cost} = \text{Expenditure on Asset} \times \text{Capitalisation Rate}$$ Temporary investment income on general funds cannot be deducted. Capitalisation rate applies to weighted expenditure.
Capitalisation Rate Algorithm (Paragraph 14)
$$\text{Capitalisation Rate} = \frac{\sum \text{Borrowing Costs on General Borrowings Outstanding}}{\sum \text{Principal Amount of General Borrowings Outstanding}}$$
Borrowings made specifically for obtaining another qualifying asset are excluded from this pool until substantially all activities to prepare that qualifying asset are complete!
Capitalisation Cap: The amount of borrowing costs that an entity capitalises during a period shall NOT exceed the total amount of borrowing costs incurred by it during that period!

4. Lifecycle: Commencement, Suspension and Cessation

Paragraphs 17–25
→
Commencement of Capitalisation (All 3 Criteria Cumulative):
An entity shall begin capitalising borrowing costs on the commencement date when it first satisfies all of the following conditions:
  1. It incurs expenditures for the asset;
  2. It incurs borrowing costs; and
  3. It undertakes activities that are necessary to prepare the asset for its intended use or sale (includes technical and administrative work prior to physical construction, e.g., obtaining building permits).
‖
Suspension of Capitalisation (Paragraphs 20–21):
Capitalisation must be suspended during extended periods in which active development is interrupted (e.g. work halted for months due to legal disputes or labour strikes).
Exception: Capitalisation is NOT suspended during periods when substantial technical and administrative work is being carried out, or when a temporary delay is a necessary part of the process of getting an asset ready (e.g., waiting for concrete to cure, high water levels during bridge construction).
✓
Cessation of Capitalisation (Paragraphs 22–25):
Capitalisation shall cease when substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the qualifying asset for its intended use or sale are complete (physical construction finished, even if minor administrative/decorative work remains).
Parts Capable of Independent Use: When the construction of a qualifying asset is completed in parts and each part is capable of being used while construction continues on other parts (e.g., a business park consisting of several buildings), capitalisation of borrowing costs ceases for each part as it is completed.

2. Value in Use (VIU) and Cash Flow Projections

Paragraphs 30–57

Value in Use (VIU): The present value of the future cash flows expected to be derived from an asset or cash-generating unit.

Strict Rules on Cash Flow Forecasts for VIU (Paragraphs 33–53):
  1. Time Horizon: Cash flow projections shall be based on reasonable and supportable assumptions representing management's best estimate over a maximum period of 5 years, unless a longer period can be justified.
  2. Terminal Value: Cash flows beyond 5 years are estimated by extrapolating projections using a steady or declining growth rate (cannot exceed long-term average growth rate for products/industry).
  3. EXCLUDED CASH FLOWS (Strictly Prohibited):
    • Cash flows from future restructurings to which the entity is not yet committed.
    • Cash flows from improving or enhancing the asset's performance (only maintain current standard of performance).
    • Cash inflows or outflows from financing activities (interest/principal).
    • Income tax receipts or payments (cash flows must be pre-tax).
  4. Discount Rate: Must be a pre-tax discount rate reflecting current market assessments of the time value of money and the risks specific to the asset (e.g., WACC, CAPM).
Foreign Currency Future Cash Flows (Paragraph 54): Future cash flows are estimated in the currency in which they will be generated and then discounted using a discount rate appropriate for that currency. The present value is then translated into functional currency using the spot exchange rate at the date of the value in use calculation.

3. Cash-Generating Units (CGUs) and Corporate Assets

Paragraphs 66–103

Definition of CGU (Paragraph 6): A Cash-Generating Unit is the smallest identifiable group of assets that generates cash inflows that are largely independent of the cash inflows from other assets or groups of assets.

Corporate Assets (Paragraph 100): Assets other than goodwill that contribute to the future cash flows of both the CGU under review and other CGUs (e.g. head office building, IT infrastructure, shared research facility). Corporate assets do not generate separate cash inflows.

1
Case A: Corporate Asset CAN be Allocated on Reasonable & Consistent Basis Allocate carrying amount of corporate asset to each individual CGU (pro-rata based on carrying amounts weighted by remaining useful lives). Compare each CGU's revised carrying amount with its recoverable amount.
2
Case B: Corporate Asset CANNOT be Allocated on Reasonable Basis (Two-Step Test):
Step 1 (Bottom-up Test): Test each individual CGU for impairment excluding the corporate asset. Recognize any impairment loss.
Step 2 (Top-down Test): Aggregate the carrying amounts of all CGUs plus the unallocated corporate asset, and compare this combined group carrying amount with the combined recoverable amount of the entire entity/group.

4. Impairment Loss Allocation and Reversal Ceilings

Paragraphs 104–125
Order of Allocation of Impairment Loss to a CGU (Paragraph 104):
  1. First: Reduce the carrying amount of any Goodwill allocated to the CGU to zero.
  2. Second: Allocate the remaining loss pro-rata to the other assets of the unit based on the relative carrying amount of each asset in the unit.
The Floor Rule for Individual Assets in a CGU (Paragraph 105)
$$\text{Floor for Asset Carrying Amount} = \max\Big(\text{FVLCD}, \; \text{VIU}, \; 0\Big)$$
The carrying amount of an asset shall not be reduced below the highest of its FVLCD, VIU, or zero. Any unallocated loss is reallocated pro-rata to other assets of the CGU.
Strict Reversal Ceilings (Paragraphs 114–124)
1. Goodwill Impairment Can NEVER Be Reversed! Paragraph 124 strictly prohibits reversing an impairment loss recognized for goodwill in a subsequent period under any circumstances.
2. Ceiling for Other Assets: The increased carrying amount of an asset attributable to a reversal of an impairment loss shall NOT exceed the carrying amount that would have been determined (net of amortisation or depreciation) had NO impairment loss been recognized in prior years!
3. Unwinding of Discount: An impairment loss is NOT reversed merely due to the passage of time (unwinding of discount)!

Unit 5 • Non-Physical & Intellectual Assets

Ind AS 38: Intangible Assets

Prescribes the criteria for recognizing and measuring intangible assets, distinguishing research from development (PIRAC criteria), strictly prohibiting the capitalization of internally generated brands and customer lists, and governing finite vs indefinite amortisation rules.

1. Definition, Identifiability and Control

Paragraphs 8–17

Definition (Paragraph 8): An intangible asset is an identifiable non-monetary asset without physical substance.

1
Identifiability Criterion (Paragraph 12) An asset is identifiable if it either:
  • Is Separable: Is capable of being separated or divided from the entity and sold, transferred, licensed, rented or exchanged, either individually or together with a related contract; OR
  • Arises from Contractual or Other Legal Rights: Regardless of whether those rights are transferable or separable from the entity or from other rights and obligations (e.g. patents, trademarks, licenses, franchises).
2
Control Criterion (Paragraphs 13–16) An entity controls an asset if it has the power to obtain the future economic benefits flowing from the underlying resource and to restrict the access of others to those benefits.
Staff & Skilled Workforce: An entity usually cannot demonstrate sufficient control over the expected future economic benefits arising from a team of skilled staff or employee training, because employees may leave. Therefore, a skilled workforce or trained human resources cannot be recognized as an intangible asset!

2. Initial Recognition Across Acquisition Modes

Paragraphs 24–47

An intangible asset is initially measured at Cost:

Acquisition Mode Initial Measurement Basis Key Exam Accounting Rules
Separate Acquisition Cost = Purchase price + import duties + non-refundable taxes - trade discounts + directly attributable testing/preparation costs. Operating losses and staff training costs are expensed. Multi-year maintenance contracts are separated and expensed over the service period.
Acquisition in Business Combination (Ind AS 103) Fair Value at acquisition date. Recognized separately from goodwill if identifiable. Even in-process research and development (IPR&D) of the acquiree is recognized as an asset at fair value!
Government Grant (Ind AS 20) Fair value OR nominal amount plus direct acquisition expenses. E.g. airport landing slots, radio broadcast spectrum, import licenses.
Exchange of Assets Fair value of asset given up (or asset received), unless transaction lacks commercial substance. If fair value not reliably measurable, measured at carrying amount of asset given up.

3. Internally Generated Intangibles: Research vs Development (PIRAC)

Paragraphs 51–67

To assess whether an internally generated intangible asset meets the criteria for recognition, an entity classifies the generation of the asset into:

Phase Core Activities Mandatory Accounting Treatment
Research Phase (Para 54–56) Original and planned investigation undertaken with the prospect of gaining new scientific or technical knowledge and understanding (e.g. searching for alternatives, formulating designs). ALL RESEARCH EXPENDITURE IS EXPENSED IN P&L AS INCURRED! No intangible asset arising from research shall be recognized.
Development Phase (Para 57) Application of research findings or other knowledge to a plan or design for the production of new or substantially improved materials, devices, products, processes, systems or services before commercial production. CAPITALIZED AS INTANGIBLE ASSET if, and only if, the entity can demonstrate ALL 6 PIRAC Criteria!
The 6 Mandatory Development Criteria (PIRAC)
\begin{aligned} \mathbf{P} &\quad \text{Probable future economic benefits generated by the asset} \\ \mathbf{I} &\quad \text{Intention of management to complete and use or sell it} \\ \mathbf{R} &\quad \text{Resources (technical, financial, and other) available to complete and use/sell} \\ \mathbf{A} &\quad \text{Ability to use or sell the intangible asset} \\ \mathbf{C} &\quad \text{Technical Feasibility of Completing the asset so it will be available for use/sale} \\ \mathbf{M} &\quad \text{Expenditure attributable to the asset during development can be reliably Measured} \end{aligned}
Strict Prohibition Against Retrospective Reinstatement (Paragraph 71): Expenditure on an intangible item that was initially recognized as an expense in previous interim or annual financial statements shall NOT be recognized as part of the cost of an intangible asset at a later date! Once expensed, always expensed.

4. Prohibited Capitalisations and Website Costs

Paragraph 63 • SIC 32 / Appendix A
Strictly Prohibited from Recognition as Intangible Assets (Paragraph 63):
  • Internally Generated Goodwill: Shall never be recognized as an asset (not identifiable or controllable).
  • Internally generated brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists, and items similar in substance: SHALL NOT BE RECOGNIZED AS INTANGIBLE ASSETS! Expenditures on these cannot be distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole.
  • Start-up, pre-opening, and pre-operating costs: Expensed in P&L as incurred.
  • Training costs: Expensed in P&L as incurred.
  • Advertising and promotional activities: Expensed in P&L when the entity has a right to access the goods or receives the services.
  • Relocation or reorganization expenses: Expensed in P&L.

Accounting for Website Costs (Appendix A to Ind AS 38)

  • Planning Stage: Similar to research phase → Expensed in P&L.
  • Application and Infrastructure Development Stage: Purchasing hardware/software → Capitalized as PPE / Intangibles if PIRAC met.
  • Graphical Design & Content Development:
    • If developed solely to advertise and promote an entity's own products and services → EXPENSED IN P&L as incurred (cannot demonstrate how it will generate probable future economic benefits).
    • If website is capable of generating direct revenue (e.g. taking e-commerce orders, processing online transactions) → Capitalized during development phase if PIRAC met.
  • Operating Stage: Ongoing maintenance, hosting, minor content updates → Expensed in P&L.

5. Useful Life: Finite vs Indefinite Amortisation

Paragraphs 88–108
Parameter Finite Useful Life Indefinite Useful Life
Definition A limited period of benefit or number of production units. No foreseeable limit to the period over which the asset is expected to generate net cash inflows (e.g. perpetual broadcast license).
Amortisation Amortized systematically over useful life on SLM, WDV, or unit of production basis. NOT AMORTIZED! Amortisation charge is zero.
Impairment Testing Tested only when an indication exists under Ind AS 36. Mandatory annual impairment test under Ind AS 36 (plus whenever indication exists).

Ind AS 40: Investment Property

Governs the recognition, measurement, transfers, and disclosures of land and buildings held to earn rentals or for capital appreciation, emphasizing the mandatory Cost Model carve-out in India and extensive Fair Value disclosure notes.

1. Definition, Classification and Scope Boundaries

Paragraphs 5–15

Definition (Paragraph 5): Investment property is property (land or a building—or part of a building—or both) held (by the owner or by the lessee as a right-of-use asset) to earn rentals or for capital appreciation or both, rather than for:

  • Use in the production or supply of goods or services or for administrative purposes (owner-occupied property covered by Ind AS 16); or
  • Sale in the ordinary course of business (inventory covered by Ind AS 2).
Investment Property (Ind AS 40) Owner-Occupied (Ind AS 16) Inventory (Ind AS 2)
  • Land held for long-term capital appreciation.
  • Land held for currently undetermined future use.
  • Building leased out under operating leases.
  • Vacant building held to be leased out under operating leases.
  • Property being constructed/developed for future use as investment property.
  • Property held for use in production or supply of goods/services.
  • Property held for administrative purposes.
  • Property occupied by employees (whether or not employees pay rent at market rates).
  • Owner-occupied property awaiting disposal.
  • Property intended for sale in the ordinary course of business.
  • Property in the process of construction or development for such sale (e.g. real estate developer flats).

Dual-Use Properties (Paragraph 10):

  • If portions could be sold separately (or leased out separately under a finance lease), an entity accounts for the portions separately (part as Ind AS 40 and part as Ind AS 16).
  • If portions cannot be sold separately, the property is investment property only if an insignificant portion is held for use in production or administrative purposes.

Ancillary Services to Occupants (Paragraphs 11–13):

  • If ancillary services provided to occupants are insignificant to the arrangement as a whole (e.g. security and maintenance services in an office tower), the property is Investment Property.
  • If services provided are significant (e.g. hotels, serviced apartments where substantial guest amenities/catering are provided), the property is Owner-Occupied (Ind AS 16).
Inter-Company Leases (Standalone vs Consolidated Financial Statements - Paragraph 15):

In standalone financial statements, an entity leasing a building to its parent or fellow subsidiary classifies it as Investment Property.

However, in Consolidated Financial Statements (CFS), the property is owner-occupied from the group's perspective because it is occupied by a group member. Therefore, it is reclassified to PPE under Ind AS 16 in the consolidated balance sheet!

2. Measurement & The Critical Cost Model Carve-Out

Paragraphs 20–56

Initial Measurement: An investment property is measured initially at its Cost, including transaction costs (legal fees, transfer taxes, title registration). Start-up costs and initial operating losses are expensed.

Crucial Carve-out: Cost Model vs Fair Value Model:

Under international standard IAS 40, entities have an accounting policy choice between the Cost Model and the Fair Value Model (where changes in fair value are recognized in P&L).

Under Ind AS 40, an entity shall measure all of its investment properties in accordance with Ind AS 16's requirements for the COST MODEL ONLY!

The Fair Value Model is NOT PERMITTED for measurement in India. All investment properties must be depreciated and tested for impairment under Ind AS 36.

Mandatory Note Disclosure: Despite the prohibition of the fair value model for balance sheet measurement, Paragraph 79(e) mandates that the FAIR VALUE OF INVESTMENT PROPERTY MUST BE DISCLOSED IN THE NOTES TO FINANCIAL STATEMENTS! The disclosure must include valuation techniques (e.g. DCF method), significant unobservable inputs, and whether valuation was performed by an independent registered valuer.

3. Transfers to and from Investment Property

Paragraphs 57–65

Transfers to, or from, investment property shall be made when, and only when, there is a change in use, evidenced by:

1
Commencement of owner-occupation: Transfer from Investment Property (Ind AS 40) to PPE (Ind AS 16).
2
Commencement of development with a view to sale: Transfer from Investment Property (Ind AS 40) to Inventories (Ind AS 2).
3
End of owner-occupation: Transfer from PPE (Ind AS 16) to Investment Property (Ind AS 40).
4
Inception of an operating lease to another party: Transfer from Inventories (Ind AS 2) to Investment Property (Ind AS 40).
Measurement on Transfer under Ind AS 40: Since Ind AS 40 permits only the Cost Model, transfers between investment property, owner-occupied property, and inventories DO NOT change the carrying amount of the property transferred, and they do not change the cost of that property for measurement or disclosure purposes!

Unit 7 • Disposal Groups & Discontinued Operations

Ind AS 105: Non-current Assets Held for Sale & Discontinued Operations

Prescribes the criteria for classifying non-current assets and disposal groups as held for sale, the measurement mandate at the lower of carrying amount and Fair Value Less Costs to Sell (FVLCD), immediate cessation of depreciation, and separate single-line presentation of discontinued operations.

1. Criteria for Classification as Held for Sale

Paragraphs 6–14

Core Axiom: An entity shall classify a non-current asset (or disposal group) as held for sale if its carrying amount will be recovered principally through a sale transaction rather than through continuing use.

Two Mandatory Cumulative Conditions (Paragraphs 7–8):
  1. Available for Immediate Sale: The asset (or disposal group) must be available for immediate sale in its present condition subject only to terms that are usual and customary for sales of such assets.
    Exam Rule: If an entity plans renovations or refurbishment prior to selling to enhance value, the asset is NOT available for immediate sale in its present condition and CANNOT be classified as held for sale!
  2. Sale Must Be Highly Probable:
    • The appropriate level of management must be committed to a plan to sell the asset (or disposal group).
    • An active programme to locate a buyer and complete the plan must have been initiated.
    • The asset must be actively marketed for sale at a price that is reasonable in relation to its current fair value.
    • The sale should be expected to qualify for recognition as a completed sale within ONE YEAR from the date of classification.
    • Actions required to complete the plan should indicate that it is unlikely that significant changes to the plan will be made or that the plan will be withdrawn.
Non-current Assets to Be Abandoned (Paragraph 13):

An entity shall NOT classify as held for sale a non-current asset (or disposal group) that is to be abandoned! This is because its carrying amount will be recovered principally through continuing use to the end of its economic life rather than through a sale transaction.

2. Measurement Mandate & Depreciation Cessation

Paragraphs 15–25
1
Step 1: Measurement Immediately Prior to Classification (Paragraph 18) Immediately before the initial classification as held for sale, the carrying amounts of the asset (or all assets and liabilities in a disposal group) shall be measured in accordance with applicable Ind AS (e.g. depreciation charged up to classification date under Ind AS 16).
2
Step 2: Measurement at Classification Date (Paragraph 15) An entity shall measure a non-current asset (or disposal group) classified as held for sale at the lower of its Carrying Amount and Fair Value Less Costs to Sell (FVLCD).
3
Step 3: Recognition of Impairment Loss & Subsequent Gains (Paragraphs 20–22) An impairment loss is recognized in profit or loss for any initial or subsequent write-down of the asset to FVLCD.
A subsequent increase in FVLCD is recognized as a gain in P&L, but NOT in excess of the cumulative impairment loss that has been recognized either under Ind AS 105 or previously under Ind AS 36!
Immediate Cessation of Depreciation (Paragraph 25):

An entity shall NOT depreciate (or amortise) a non-current asset while it is classified as held for sale or while it is part of a disposal group classified as held for sale! Interest and other expenses attributable to the liabilities of a disposal group shall continue to be recognized.

Allocation of Impairment Loss to a Disposal Group (Paragraph 23)
\text{Total Impairment Loss} = \text{Carrying Amount of Group} - \text{FVLCD of Group}
1. Allocated first to reduce the carrying amount of any Goodwill.
2. Remaining loss allocated pro-rata to other non-current assets within scope of Ind AS 105 measurement provisions (current assets like inventory and debtors are measured under Ind AS 2 & Ind AS 109 and are NOT written down by this allocation).

3. Changes to a Plan of Sale

Paragraphs 26–29

If an entity has classified an asset as held for sale, but the criteria are no longer met, the entity shall cease to classify the asset as held for sale.

Measurement When Asset Ceases to be Held for Sale (Paragraph 27)
$$\text{Measurement} = \min\Big(\text{Carrying Amount had asset never been classified as HFS}, \; \text{Recoverable Amount at decision date}\Big)$$
Carrying amount before classification is adjusted for any depreciation, amortisation or revaluations that would have been recognized had the asset not been classified as held for sale. Any adjustment is recognized immediately in P&L from continuing operations.

4. Discontinued Operations Presentation

Paragraphs 31–40

Definition (Paragraph 32): A discontinued operation is a component of an entity that either has been disposed of or is classified as held for sale, and:

  • Represents a separate major line of business or geographical area of operations;
  • Is part of a single coordinated plan to dispose of a separate major line of business or geographical area of operations; OR
  • Is a subsidiary acquired exclusively with a view to resale.
Presentation in Statement of Profit and Loss (Paragraph 33):

An entity shall present a single amount on the face of the Statement of Profit and Loss comprising the total of:

  1. The post-tax profit or loss of discontinued operations; and
  2. The post-tax gain or loss recognized on the measurement to fair value less costs to sell or on the disposal of the assets or disposal group(s) constituting the discontinued operation.

A detailed breakdown of revenue, expenses, pre-tax profit, and related income tax expense must be disclosed either in the notes or on the face of the statement of profit and loss.

Unit 8 • Comprehensive Lease Architecture

Ind AS 116: Leases

Introduces the single on-balance sheet lessee accounting model (Right-of-Use Asset and Lease Liability), substantive substitution rights testing, lease term economic incentives, remeasurement and modification mechanics, dual lessor accounting, and sale and leaseback frameworks under Ind AS 115.

1. Identifying a Lease: The Two Core Pillars

Paragraphs 9–17 • Appendix B

Definition (Paragraph 9): A contract is, or contains, a lease if the contract conveys the right to control the use of an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for consideration.

1
Pillar 1: Identified Asset (Explicit or Implicit) An asset can be identified by being explicitly specified in a contract or implicitly specified at the time it is made available for use.
Substantive Substitution Rights (Paragraph B14): Even if an asset is specified, a customer does NOT have the right to use an identified asset if the supplier has a substantive right to substitute the asset throughout the period of use. A substitution right is substantive only if BOTH conditions exist:
  • The supplier has the practical ability to substitute alternative assets throughout the period of use; and
  • The supplier would benefit economically from the exercise of its right to substitute the asset.
Exam Rule: If supplier stores equipment at its own premises, has a large pool of identical units, and substitution costs are minimal → Substitution rights are substantive → No identified asset → NOT A LEASE!
2
Pillar 2: Right to Control the Use To convey the right to control the use of an identified asset, the customer must have BOTH of the following throughout the period of use:
  1. The right to obtain substantially all of the economic benefits from use of the identified asset (exclusive capacity or output); and
  2. The right to direct the use of the identified asset (how and for what purpose the asset is used).
Protective Rights: Contractual terms designed to protect the supplier's interest in the asset or its personnel (e.g. maximum operating speed, safety protocols, prohibiting transport of explosives) are merely protective rights and do NOT prevent the customer from having the right to direct the use!

2. Separating Components & Determining Lease Term

Paragraphs 12–19

Separating Lease and Non-Lease Components (Paragraphs 12–17):

  • Contracts often contain a combination of lease components (e.g. right to use equipment) and non-lease service components (e.g. maintenance, security, insurance).
  • The consideration is allocated between lease and non-lease components based on their relative stand-alone selling prices.
  • Practical Expedient for Lessees: As a practical expedient, a lessee may elect, by class of underlying asset, not to separate non-lease components from lease components, and instead account for each lease component and any associated non-lease components as a single lease component. (This expedient is not available to lessors!).

Determining the Lease Term (Paragraphs 18–21):

Lease Term Formulation
\text{Lease Term} = \text{Non-Cancellable Period} + \text{Extension Periods (if reasonably certain)} + \text{Termination Periods (if reasonably certain NOT to exercise)}
Economic incentives considered: significant leasehold improvements, specialised asset customization, termination penalties, and relocation disruption.

3. Lessee Accounting Model: ROU Asset & Lease Liability

Paragraphs 22–35
Recognition Exemptions (Paragraphs 5–8):

A lessee may elect NOT to apply the on-balance sheet model to:

  1. Short-term Leases: Leases that, at commencement date, have a lease term of 12 months or less and do not contain a purchase option. (Election made by class of underlying asset).
  2. Leases of Low-Value Assets: Underlying asset has a low value when brand new (e.g. laptops, tablets, personal computers, small office furniture). Evaluated on an absolute basis, lease-by-lease, regardless of lessee's size or wealth.

For exempt leases, the lessee recognizes lease payments as an expense on a straight-line basis over the lease term.

Initial Measurement of Lease Liability (Paragraph 26):

Initial Lease Liability Formula
$$\text{Lease Liability} = \text{Present Value of Lease Payments not paid at Commencement Date}$$
Discounted using the interest rate implicit in the lease (if readily determinable) or the lessee's Incremental Borrowing Rate (IBR).
+
Lease Payments Included in Liability:
  • Fixed Payments: (including in-substance fixed payments) less any lease incentives receivable.
  • Variable Lease Payments that Depend on an Index or Rate: (e.g., CPI, benchmark interest rates), initially measured using the index/rate at commencement date.
  • Amounts Expected to be Payable under Residual Value Guarantees (RVG).
  • Exercise Price of Purchase Option: if the lessee is reasonably certain to exercise that option.
  • Payments of Penalties for Terminating the Lease: if the lease term reflects the lessee exercising an option to terminate.
Strictly Excluded: Variable payments based on sales or usage (e.g. % of store revenue) are EXCLUDED from lease liability and expensed in P&L when incurred!

Initial Measurement of Right-of-Use (ROU) Asset (Paragraph 24):

Initial ROU Asset Valuation
\begin{aligned} \text{ROU Asset} &= \text{Initial Measurement of Lease Liability} \\ &\quad + \text{Lease payments made at or before commencement date} \\ &\quad - \text{Lease incentives received} \\ &\quad + \text{Initial direct costs incurred by lessee} \\ &\quad + \text{Estimated dismantling and site restoration costs (Ind AS 37)} \end{aligned}

4. Subsequent Measurement, Remeasurement & Modifications

Paragraphs 29–46
Component Subsequent Accounting Treatment P&L / Balance Sheet Impact
ROU Asset Measured at Cost Model: Cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment losses (Ind AS 36).
Depreciation Period: Depreciated over shorter of lease term and useful life. (Depreciated over useful life if ownership transfers or purchase option is reasonably certain). 		Depreciation charged to Profit or Loss as an operating expense.
Lease Liability Measured at amortised cost:
1. Carrying amount increased by interest expense (effective rate).
2. Carrying amount reduced by lease payments made. 		Interest expense charged to Profit or Loss as finance cost. Cash payments split between financing principal and interest under Ind AS 7.
Remeasurement Discount Rate Rules (Paragraphs 39–43):
  • Remeasure using REVISED Discount Rate when:
    • There is a change in the lease term (extension or termination option assessment changes).
    • There is a change in the assessment of an option to purchase the underlying asset.
  • Remeasure using UNCHANGED Discount Rate when:
    • There is a change in the amounts expected to be payable under a residual value guarantee.
    • There is a change in future lease payments resulting from a change in an index or rate (e.g. CPI change).
Remeasurement Adjustment: The remeasurement of the lease liability is recognized as an adjustment to the ROU Asset. If the ROU asset is reduced to zero, any remaining reduction is recognized in Profit or Loss.

Lease Modifications (Paragraphs 44–46):

  • Accounted for as a Separate Lease: If the modification increases the scope by adding the right to use one or more underlying assets AND the consideration increases by an amount commensurate with the stand-alone price.
  • Not a Separate Lease:
    • Decrease in Scope (e.g. space reduced or term cut): Decrease the carrying amount of ROU asset to reflect partial/full termination, and recognize the resulting gain or loss in Profit or Loss. Remeasure lease liability.
    • All Other Modifications: Remeasure lease liability using a revised discount rate and adjust the carrying amount of the ROU asset.

5. Lessor Accounting: Finance Lease vs Operating Lease

Paragraphs 61–97

Ind AS 116 maintains the dual classification model for lessors:

Classification Criteria (Paragraphs 62–65) Balance Sheet & P&L Mechanics
Finance Lease Transfers substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership of an underlying asset.
Indicators: Ownership transfers at end of term; bargain purchase option; lease term for major part of economic life; PV of lease payments substantially all of fair value; specialized nature. 		1. Derecognize underlying asset.
2. Recognize Net Investment in the Lease (Lease Receivable) = PV of lease payments + PV of Unguaranteed Residual Value (UGRV) discounted at rate implicit in lease.
3. Recognize finance income over lease term to produce constant periodic rate of return.
Operating Lease A lease that does NOT transfer substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership. 1. Retain underlying asset in Balance Sheet and depreciate under Ind AS 16/38.
2. Recognize lease rental income on a straight-line basis over the lease term (or other systematic basis).

6. Sale and Leaseback Transactions

Paragraphs 98–103 • Critical ICAI Model

To determine whether the transfer of an asset is accounted for as a sale, the entity applies the requirements for determining when a performance obligation is satisfied in Ind AS 115 Revenue from Contracts with Customers.

Ind AS 115 Assessment Seller-Lessee Accounting Buyer-Lessor Accounting
Transfer IS a Sale 1. Derecognize underlying asset.
2. Measure ROU Asset at proportion of previous carrying amount relating to right of use retained.
3. Recognize ONLY GAIN/LOSS relating to rights transferred to buyer-lessor. 		1. Recognize purchase of underlying asset under Ind AS 16.
2. Account for lease under lessor rules (finance or operating).
Transfer is NOT a Sale 1. Continue to recognize transferred asset on Balance Sheet.
2. Recognize a Financial Liability equal to transfer proceeds (Ind AS 109). 		1. Do NOT recognize transferred asset.
2. Recognize a Financial Asset equal to transfer proceeds (Ind AS 109).
Sale and Leaseback Calculations (When Transfer is a Sale)
$$\text{ROU Asset} = \text{Carrying Amount of Asset} \times \left( \frac{\text{PV of Lease Payments}}{\text{Fair Value of Asset}} \right)$$ $$\text{Gain Recognized in P\&L} = (\text{Fair Value} - \text{Carrying Amount}) \times \left( \frac{\text{Fair Value} - \text{PV of Lease Payments}}{\text{Fair Value}} \right)$$
If sales price or lease payments are not at market value, adjustments are made: below-market terms are treated as prepayments of lease payments; above-market terms are treated as additional financing provided by buyer-lessor.

TYK Question 1 Rail Wagon Fleet Substitution Rights
Question: A lessee enters into a 10-year contract with a freight carrier to transport goods. Carrier uses rail wagons of a particular specification and has a large pool of similar rail wagons stored at its own premises. Costs of substituting rail wagons are minimal. Does the carrier have substantive substitution rights and does the arrangement contain a lease?
Solution:

Under Paragraph B14 of Ind AS 116, a supplier's substitution rights are substantive if the supplier has both the practical ability to substitute alternative assets throughout the period of use and would benefit economically from doing so:

  • The rail wagons are stored at the lessor's premises, the lessor has a large pool of similar rail wagons, and the costs associated with substituting them are minimal.
  • Thus, the lessor has the practical ability to substitute wagons at any time.
  • Because substitution costs are minimal and wagons are pooled at lessor's yard, the lessor can optimize fleet utilization and achieve economic benefits from substitution.

Therefore, the lessor's substitution rights are substantive. Consequently, there is NO identified asset and the contract DOES NOT contain a lease (it is a service contract for freight transport).

TYK Question 2 Customer-Designed Solar Energy Plant
Question: Customer M enters into a 20-year contract with Supplier S to install and operate a solar plant for M's energy supply. M designed the solar plant before construction (determined location and engineering specifications). M has the exclusive right to receive all energy produced. Therefore, Customer M has the right to control the use of the identified asset, and the contract contains a lease.

TYK Question 4 Separating Lease & Non-Lease Components

  3. Therefore, ₹8,000 per year is allocated to the lease component (used in measuring the lease liability and ROU asset), and ₹2,000 per year is recognized as an operating maintenance expense.
TYK Question 7 In-Substance Fixed Payments vs Usage-Based Variable Rents
Question: An entity leases mining equipment. Lease payments are ₹2,000 per machine hour used, with a contractual mandatory minimum payment of ₹2,00,00,000 per year (equivalent to 10,000 hours). Expected actual usage is 20,000 hours (expected total payment ₹4,00,00,000). Are these payments included in the initial lease liability?
Solution:
  1. The mandatory minimum payment of ₹2,00,00,000 per year is an in-substance fixed payment because it is unavoidable regardless of whether the equipment is used. Therefore, the present value of ₹2,00,00,000 per year MUST be included in the initial measurement of the Lease Liability.
  2. The additional expected payment of ₹2,00,00,000 per year (for hours exceeding 10,000) represents a variable lease payment linked to usage. Under Paragraph 27(b), variable lease payments that depend on usage (and not on an index or rate) are EXCLUDED from the lease liability and are recognized as an expense in Profit or Loss in the period in which the usage occurs.
