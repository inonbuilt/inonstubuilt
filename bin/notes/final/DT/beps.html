25.1

Background, Genesis & Economic Impact of BEPS

From Double Taxation (1920s League of Nations) to Double Non-Taxation in Globalized Digital Commerce
Historical Evolution

1. The Impact of Globalization & The Evolution of International Tax Rules

Economic globalization has transformed international trade, accelerated capital mobility, and enabled multinational enterprises (MNEs) to establish highly integrated global supply chains. Key structural developments include:

  • Cross-Border Mobility: Seamless movement of capital, skilled labor, and production facilities from high-cost to low-cost developing countries.
  • Ascendance of Intangibles: Modern enterprise value is driven primarily by intellectual property (IP), software, algorithms, trademarks, and patents rather than physical factories.
  • Digitalization of the Economy: Businesses can now engage with millions of customers and generate immense economic value in market jurisdictions without maintaining any physical brick-and-mortar presence.

Historically, international tax principles were forged in the 1920s by the League of Nations. The primary objective at that time was the elimination of double taxation (i.e., preventing the same income from being taxed simultaneously in the source country and the residence country) through bilateral Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs).

What is Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS)? Core Definition

Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) refers to tax planning strategies that exploit gaps and mismatches in tax rules across sovereign nations to artificially shift profits to locations where there is little or no real economic activity, but where taxes are low or zero, resulting in little or no overall corporate income tax being paid (Double Non-Taxation or long-term tax deferral).

2. Four Severe Adverse Effects of BEPS [Tested in TYK Q1]

1. Revenue Loss to Governments

Governments worldwide suffer massive fiscal revenue loss, estimated by the OECD at USD 100 to 240 Billion annually (equivalent to 4% to 10% of global corporate income tax revenues), alongside higher administrative enforcement costs.

2. Under-Funding in Developing Countries

Developing and emerging economies rely more heavily on corporate income tax revenues than developed nations. BEPS leads to acute under-funding of vital public infrastructure, healthcare, education, and development projects.

3. Tax Inequity & Fairness Distortions

When large multinational corporations legally pay negligible taxes, the fiscal burden inevitably shifts onto individual wage earners and purely domestic businesses. This undermines public trust and voluntary compliance in the tax system.

4. Harm to Fair Domestic Competition

Domestic enterprises (including family businesses, SMEs, and local startups) cannot shift profits across borders. They face higher effective tax rates than multinational conglomerates, distorting resource allocation and competitive neutrality.

Minimum Standards

The 4 BEPS Minimum Standards & Peer Reviews

Mandatory Commitments for Inclusive Framework Jurisdictions & Global Monitoring
Mandatory for 140+ Countries

Overview of the 4 Mandatory Minimum Standards

While some BEPS recommendations provide flexible best-practice guidance or model legislation, four Action Plans are categorized as Minimum Standards. All members of the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework on BEPS (including India) commit to implementing them to protect against cross-border negative spillovers:

1. Action 5: Countering Harmful Tax Practices Substance & Transparency

Requires jurisdictions to eliminate harmful preferential tax regimes by requiring substantial economic activity (the Nexus Approach) for intellectual property (patent box) regimes. Mandates spontaneous exchange of information on cross-border tax rulings (APAs, PE rulings, conduit rulings).

2. Action 6: Preventing Treaty Abuse Anti-Treaty Shopping

Requires treaties to include an express preamble stating treaties are not intended to create opportunities for non-taxation or reduced taxation through tax evasion or avoidance. Must include the Principal Purpose Test (PPT) or a Limitation on Benefits (LOB) rule.

3. Action 13: Country-by-Country Reporting Global Transparency

Mandates large MNE groups with consolidated group revenue exceeding EUR 750 Million (₹6,400 Crores in India) to file an annual Country-by-Country (CbC) report detailing global allocation of income, taxes paid, economic activity, and headcount across each jurisdiction.

4. Action 14: Effective Dispute Resolution MAP Strengthening

Requires countries to resolve treaty-related disputes through the Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) under Article 25 in an effective and timely manner. Implementation is monitored via a rigorous, published peer review process.

Peer Review Mechanism

Compliance with these 4 Minimum Standards is evaluated through an objective peer review process conducted by the OECD. India has successfully undergone peer reviews for Actions 5, 6, 13, and 14, consistently receiving positive evaluations for its statutory alignment and robust administrative framework.

Action 1

Action 1: Tax Challenges Arising from Digitalisation

Lack of Physical Presence, Significant Economic Presence (SEP), Equalisation Levy & The Two-Pillar Solution
Horizontal Action

1. The Core Direct Tax Challenges of the Digital Economy

The digital economy does not create a separate, isolated sector; rather, the entire global economy is rapidly becoming digitalized. The key challenges identified by the OECD include:

  • Scale without Mass: Businesses can operate extensively in a market jurisdiction, generating substantial revenues from millions of active users without any local physical footprint (employees, offices, or servers).
  • Heavy Reliance on Intangibles: Value is driven by proprietary algorithms, intellectual property, and user data rather than tangible assets.
  • Value Creation via User Participation: Network effects and user-generated data (content, browsing patterns, purchase behavior) actively contribute to value creation in the market country.

2. OECD Recommended Direct Tax Options [Tested in TYK Q2]

In the 2015 Action 1 Final Report, the OECD evaluated three primary unilateral mechanisms for sovereign tax systems:

OECD Option Operational Mechanism Indian Statutory Adoption
1. Significant Economic Presence (SEP) Modifying traditional Permanent Establishment (PE) rules to establish a nexus based on revenue generated and sustained digital engagement with local users, regardless of physical presence. Enacted in Section 9(9) of Income-tax Act, 2025 (formerly Section 9(1)(i) Explanation 2A). Core business connection nexus!
2. Withholding Tax on Digital Transactions Levying a standalone final withholding tax on payments made by residents to foreign e-commerce providers for digital goods and services. Section 194-O (1% TDS on gross e-commerce sales facilitated by digital operators).
3. Equalisation Levy An indirect/unilateral levy on consideration received by foreign tech enterprises from local advertising or e-commerce supply, outside income tax treaties. Equalisation Levy introduced in Finance Act 2016 (6% on B2B digital advertising) and expanded in 2020 (2% on e-commerce operators).

Significant Economic Presence (SEP) in India [Section 9(9) of 2025 Act] Statutory Nexus

Under Section 9(9) of the Income-tax Act, 2025, a non-resident shall have a Significant Economic Presence (SEP) in India (which constitutes a statutory business connection in India) if either of the following objective quantitative thresholds is satisfied:

Nature of Activity Statutory Threshold (Previous Year)
(a) Transaction-Based Threshold
Transaction in respect of any goods, services, or property carried out by a non-resident with any person in India, including provision of download of data or software in India.		 Aggregate payments arising from such transaction(s) during the previous year exceeds ₹2 Crores.
(b) User-Based Threshold
Systematic and continuous soliciting of business activities or engaging in interaction with users in India.		 Number of active users in India is at least 3 Lakhs (300,000 users).

Crucial Non-Physical Caveats: The above transactions/activities constitute SEP in India whether or not —

  1. the agreement for such transactions or activities is entered in India;
  2. the non-resident has a residence or place of business in India; or
  3. the non-resident renders services in India.

Income Attributable: Only so much of the income as is reasonably attributable to the transactions or activities referred to above shall be deemed to accrue or arise in India.

3. The OECD Inclusive Framework Two-Pillar Solution (Pillar 1 & Pillar 2)

In October 2021, over 135 countries of the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework joined a historic agreement to reform international tax rules for the digital era:

Pillar One: Reallocation of Profit

  • Amount A: Reallocates 25% of residual profits (profit exceeding a 10% profitability margin) of the largest MNEs (global turnover > EUR 20 Billion) to market jurisdictions where goods/services are used or consumed.
  • Amount B: Standardizes and simplifies the application of the arm's length principle to baseline in-country marketing and distribution activities.
  • Trade-Off: Requires withdrawal of unilateral digital services taxes (Equalisation Levy).

Pillar Two: Global Minimum Tax (GloBE)

  • Global Minimum Rate: Establishes a 15% global minimum corporate tax for MNE groups with consolidated annual revenues ≥ EUR 750 Million.
  • Income Inclusion Rule (IIR): Parent jurisdiction imposes top-up tax on low-taxed foreign subsidiaries.
  • Undertaxed Profits Rule (UTPR): Backstop rule allowing source states to deny deductions if parent fails to apply IIR.
  • Subject to Tax Rule (STTR): Treaty-based rule allowing source states to impose a 9% minimum tax on related-party cross-border interest, royalties, and fees.
Action 2

Action 2: Neutralise the Effects of Hybrid Mismatch Arrangements

Eliminating Deduction / No Inclusion (D/NI), Double Deductions (DD) & Transparent Entities
Coherence Pillar

1. What are Hybrid Mismatch Arrangements? [Tested in TYK Q6]

A Hybrid Mismatch Arrangement is a cross-border structure that exploits differences in the tax treatment of an entity or financial instrument under the domestic laws of two or more sovereign tax jurisdictions to achieve unintended double non-taxation or long-term tax deferral.

Common ways hybrid arrangements are used to exploit international tax rules:

Mismatch Outcome Nature of Exploitation Illustrative Structure
1. Deduction / No Inclusion (D/NI) Payment is allowed as a deductible business expense in the payer jurisdiction, but is not included in the taxable income of the recipient payee jurisdiction. Hybrid Financial Instrument: Characterized as deductible debt/interest in Country A (payer), but treated as tax-exempt equity/dividend under a participation exemption in Country B (payee).
2. Double Deduction (DD) A single economic expenditure gives rise to two separate deductions against different income bases in two different jurisdictions. Hybrid Entity / Dual Resident: Entity treated as a taxable corporate entity in Country A, but treated as a fiscally transparent pass-through partnership in Country B.
3. Indirect D/NI
(Imported Mismatch)		 A deductible payment is made to an ordinary entity, but that entity sets off the income against a hybrid mismatch payment made to a third jurisdiction. Funds routed through a conduit entity to import hybrid deductions into an otherwise normal commercial transaction.
4. Foreign Tax Credit & Participation Misuse Generating duplicate foreign tax credits or exploiting participation exemptions where underlying profits were never taxed. Structured hybrid asset sales or repo arrangements.
OECD Recommendations & India's Position

OECD Linking Rules: Recommends a Primary Rule (the payer country denies deduction) and a Defensive Rule (if payer country does not deny, the payee country taxes the income).
India's Position: Under Article 3 of the MLI (Transparent Entities), India opted out because its domestic corporate and partnership tax provisions already provide sufficient clarity to prevent hybrid mismatches.

Action 3

Action 3: Strengthen Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC) Rules

Taxing Passive Offshore Shelters, Place of Effective Management (POEM) & CFC Building Blocks
Coherence Pillar

1. Concept and Building Blocks of CFC Rules

Controlled Foreign Corporation (CFC) rules prevent resident corporations and high-net-worth individuals from establishing shell companies in zero-tax havens to park passive investment income (dividends, interest, royalties, capital gains) and defer domestic taxation indefinitely.

1. Definition of CFC: Legal control threshold (e.g. > 50% voting power or economic value held by resident shareholders).
2. Low-Tax Threshold: CFC rules trigger only if foreign subsidiary is subject to an effective tax rate below a specified benchmark.
3. Income Attribution: Attributes tainted/passive income directly to resident parent shareholders on a current-year basis.

Indian Tax Context: India does not have explicit standalone CFC regulations. However, India achieves equivalent anti-deferral protection through:

  • Place of Effective Management (POEM) [Section 6(3) / Section 2(73)]: A foreign company whose POEM is in India in that year is treated as a resident Indian company and taxed on its worldwide income.
  • Taxation of Foreign Dividends: Foreign dividends received by Indian resident companies are taxed at normal corporate rates without preferential concessions.
Action 4

Action 4: Limit Interest Deductions (Thin Capitalization)

The 10%-30% EBITDA Fixed Ratio Rule & Indian Statutory Adoption under Section 177 [TYK Q3]
Mandatory statutory code

1. The BEPS Action 4 Recommendations on Interest Stripping

MNEs frequently use internal debt financing (excessive shareholder loans) to strip profits out of high-tax operating entities. The OECD recommended a Fixed Ratio Rule capping net interest expense between 10% and 30% of EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization), complemented by a worldwide Group Ratio Rule.

Section 177 of Income-tax Act, 2025 (Corresponds to Sec 94B) 30% EBITDA Cap

India enacted Action 4 recommendations through Section 94B (now Section 177 of the 2025 Act):

  • Cap on Interest: Total interest paid/payable to a non-resident Associated Enterprise in excess of 30% of EBITDA (or actual interest to AE, whichever is lower) is disallowed as a business deduction.
  • De Minimis Threshold: Applies only where the total interest expenditure paid/payable to foreign AE exceeds ₹1 Crore.
  • Deemed Loan Rule: Applies equally to third-party borrowings where a non-resident AE provides an explicit/implicit guarantee or deposits a matching amount with the lender.
  • 8-Year Carry Forward: Disallowed interest can be carried forward for up to 8 assessment years and set off against business profits subject to the 30% EBITDA ceiling in future years.
  • Carve-Outs: Does not apply to banking, insurance, finance companies in an IFSC, or notified NBFCs under Section 2(71).
Action 5

Action 5: Counter Harmful Tax Practices More Effectively

Substantial Activity & The Nexus Approach for IP/Patent Box Regimes [TYK Q5]
Minimum Standard

1. The Nexus Approach for Intellectual Property (IP) Regimes

Action 5 is one of the four BEPS Minimum Standards. It revamps preferential tax regimes (such as "Patent Boxes") to prevent jurisdictions from offering low tax rates to attract portable IP income without real R&D activity.

The Nexus Approach: A preferential tax regime is compatible with international standards only if there is a direct nexus between the income qualifying for preferential taxation and the qualifying research & development (R&D) expenditures incurred by the taxpayer.

OECD Nexus Formula for Eligible IP Profits
$$\text{Income Eligible for Concessional Rate} = \frac{\text{Qualifying R&D Expenditure Incurred by Taxpayer}}{\text{Total R&D Expenditure Incurred to Develop IP}} \times \text{Overall IP Income}$$

Indian Patent Box: Section 194(1) Table Sl. No. 2 (Formerly Sec 115BBF) 10% Concessional Rate

In line with the Action 5 Nexus Approach, India introduced a preferential patent regime:

  • Concessional Tax Rate: Royalty income in respect of a patent developed and registered in India is taxed at a flat rate of 10% (plus surcharge and cess).
  • Eligible Assessee: Resident in India who is the true and first inventor of the invention and registered as patentee under the Patents Act, 1970.
  • Strict 75% Domestic Expenditure Nexus: "Developed" means at least 75% of the total expenditure was incurred in India by the eligible assessee for the invention for which the patent is granted!
Action 6

Action 6: Prevent Treaty Abuse (Treaty Shopping)

Anti-Abuse Preamble, The Principal Purpose Test (PPT) & Limitation on Benefits (LOB)
Minimum Standard

1. Tackling Treaty Shopping & Conduit Structures

Treaty Shopping involves individuals or corporations resident in a third country routing investments through a conduit shell company located in a favorable treaty jurisdiction to claim reduced withholding tax rates or capital gains exemptions to which they are not directly entitled.

Under the Action 6 Minimum Standard, countries commit to implementing a three-tiered defense:

Anti-Abuse Layer Statutory Mechanism Legal Effect
1. Treaty Preamble Clarification Treaty text explicitly states that treaties are intended to eliminate double taxation without creating opportunities for non-taxation or reduced taxation through tax evasion or avoidance (including treaty-shopping). Establishes contextual interpretation under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT).
2. Principal Purpose Test (PPT) A general anti-abuse rule denying treaty benefits if it is reasonable to conclude that obtaining that benefit was one of the principal purposes of any arrangement or transaction. Shifts burden to taxpayer to demonstrate genuine economic substance and business purpose.
3. Limitation on Benefits (LOB) Specific, objective criteria (e.g. active trade or business test, publicly traded company test, ownership and base erosion test) that an entity must satisfy to qualify as a resident entitled to treaty relief. Eliminates letter-box companies lacking operational reality.
Action 7

Action 7: Prevent Artificial Avoidance of Permanent Establishment (PE) Status

Broadened Agency PE, Commissionaire Arrangements & The Anti-Fragmentation Rule
Substance Pillar

1. Major Revisions to Article 5 of the OECD Model Convention

Action 7 closes loopholes used by MNEs to maintain sales and distribution networks in market countries while avoiding creating a taxable Permanent Establishment (PE):

Commissionaire Structures

Amends Article 5(5) and 5(6). An Agency PE is created if a person habitually concludes contracts, or habitually plays the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts that are routinely approved without material modification by the foreign enterprise.

Anti-Fragmentation Rule

Prevents MNEs from artificially fragmenting a cohesive business operation across several group entities into multiple small activities (e.g. warehousing + display + delivery) to claim the "preparatory or auxiliary" exemption under Article 5(4).

Splitting of Contracts

Prevents contractors from splitting building, construction, or installation contracts into multiple agreements of less than 6 or 12 months awarded to related enterprises to circumvent the construction PE threshold.

Actions 8 - 10

Actions 8–10: Aligning Transfer Pricing Outcomes with Value Creation

DEMPE Functions for Intangibles, Risk & Capital Control, "Cash Boxes" & High-Risk Transactions
Substance Pillar

1. Action 8: Intangibles & The DEMPE Framework

Under traditional transfer pricing, MNEs often transferred legal ownership of high-value patents and software to shell subsidiaries in low-tax jurisdictions, contractually allocating all residual licensing profits to the shell. Action 8 overhauls this by decoupling legal ownership from economic entitlement:

Core Rule: Legal Ownership Alone is Insufficient!

Legal ownership of an intangible asset does not, by itself, confer any right to retain the economic returns generated by that intangible. Profits must be allocated to the group entities that perform the DEMPE functions, contribute assets, and manage risks related to the intangible.

DEMPE Component Substantive Economic Activity Profit Allocation Standard
D — Development Conceptualization, design, core coding, scientific experimentation, and clinical trials. Major share of economic intangible returns.
E — Enhancement Upgrades, software patches, feature additions, adapting to new markets. Proportional share of enhanced value.
M — Maintenance Quality control, bug fixing, technical support, server maintenance. Routine cost-plus service fee if low risk.
P — Protection Filing patent applications, defending against IP infringement lawsuits. Legal costs plus arm's length administrative return.
E — Exploitation Licensing, commercial marketing, customer deployment, monetization. Commercial operating return based on sales effort.

Hard-to-Value Intangibles (HTVI): For intangibles with no reliable comparables at the time of transfer (e.g. early-stage biotech molecules), tax authorities may consider *ex post* outcomes (actual commercial sales) as presumptive evidence for assessing the reasonableness of *ex ante* pricing.

2. Action 9: Risks & "Cash Boxes"

Action 9 addresses contractual risk-shifting and capital-rich, low-substance entities ("cash boxes"):

  • Control over Risk: Contractual allocation of risk is respected only if the enterprise exercises active decision-making control over the risk and has the financial capacity to bear consequences.
  • Risk-Free Return for Cash Boxes: A capital-rich affiliate that provides funding but performs no active financial management functions is entitled to no more than a risk-free return (routine benchmarked interest rate).

3. Action 10: High-Risk Transactions

Action 10 targets transactions between AEs that rarely occur between independent parties:

  • Management Fees & Head Office Costs: Requires proof of actual service rendering and economic benefit (the "benefit test").
  • Non-Recognition: Tax authorities are empowered to re-characterize or disregard transactions that lack commercial rationality and cannot be priced under arm's length principles.
Actions 11 & 12

Action 11 & Action 12: Measuring BEPS & Mandatory Disclosure Rules

Economic Indicators, Data Analysis & Early Warning Mechanisms for Aggressive Tax Planning
Transparency Pillar

Action 11: Measuring & Monitoring BEPS

Action 11 focuses on gathering data and developing analytical tools to track the scale of BEPS:

  • Identifies indicators of BEPS activity using corporate tax statistics, foreign direct investment (FDI) data, and CbCR filings.
  • Recommends that jurisdictions publish aggregated tax data to evaluate the fiscal impact and effectiveness of anti-BEPS countermeasures over time.

Action 12: Mandatory Disclosure Rules (MDR)

Action 12 provides a modular framework for countries to design domestic disclosure rules:

  • Requires taxpayers and tax promoters/advisors to mandatorily disclose potentially aggressive or abusive tax planning schemes at an early stage.
  • Provides tax administrations with early warnings regarding emerging tax avoidance structures before they become widespread.

Action 13: 3-Tier Transfer Pricing Documentation & CbCR

Local File, Master File (Form 3CEAA) & Country-by-Country Reporting (Form 3CEAD) [TYK Q4]
Minimum Standard

The Standardized 3-Tier BEPS Documentation Architecture

Action 13 establishes a globally standardized, three-tiered transfer pricing documentation framework, replacing fragmented domestic reporting requirements:

Tier Scope & Content Filing & Exchange Mechanism Indian Statutory Provision
Tier 1:
Master File		 High-level strategic overview of global business operations, MNE organizational structure, IP ownership (DEMPE), financing arrangements, and global tax positions. Filed by constituent entities directly with local tax administrations. Section 171 of 2025 Act (Sec 92D) read with Rule 10DA. Action 14

Action 14: Making Dispute Resolution Mechanisms More Effective

Strengthening Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP), Article 25 & Peer Review Compliance
Minimum Standard

1. Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) Optimization

Recognizing that aggressive anti-avoidance measures could create double taxation, Action 14 commits countries to ensure that treaty disputes are resolved in a timely and effective manner:

  • Treaty Access: Taxpayers who believe actions of one or both states result in taxation not in accordance with the treaty can access MAP under Article 25 within a minimum period of 3 years.
  • Peer Review Process: Peer reviews evaluate a jurisdiction's legal and administrative framework across four key areas:
    (i) Preventing disputes; (ii) Availability and access to MAP; (iii) Resolution of MAP cases within an average of 24 months; and (iv) Implementation of MAP agreements.
  • India's Compliance: India has demonstrated significant progress in resolving double tax disputes, expanding its Competent Authority teams, and establishing clear administrative guidelines for MAP.
Action 15

Action 15: Developing a Multilateral Instrument (MLI)

Multilateral Convention to Implement Tax Treaty Related Measures, Covered Tax Agreements & Ratification
Horizontal Action

1. The Legal Rationale & Mechanics of the MLI

With over 3,000 bilateral tax treaties in force worldwide, amending each treaty individually through traditional bilateral negotiations would have taken decades. Action 15 created the Multilateral Convention to Implement Tax Treaty Related Measures to Prevent BEPS (the MLI), adopted in Paris in November 2016.

Modifies, Does Not Replace

The MLI does not replace existing bilateral DTAAs. It operates alongside them, amending and supplementing their provisions based on the joint choices of both Contracting Jurisdictions.

Covered Tax Agreements (CTAs)

A DTAA becomes a Covered Tax Agreement (CTA) under Article 2 only if both treaty partners explicitly notify the DTAA to the OECD depositary as an agreement to be covered by the MLI.

The Matching Principle

An MLI provision applies to a CTA only if both countries adopt the same option (opt-in) or neither country makes a reservation (opt-out). Minimum standards (Actions 6 and 14) are mandatory for all CTAs!

Concept Withholding Taxes (TDS) Other Taxes (Capital Gains / Business Profits)
Entry into Force
[Article 34]		 Enters into force on the 1st day of the month following the expiration of 3 months after the deposit of the instrument of ratification by the country with the OECD.
Entry into Effect
[Article 35]		 Takes effect for events occurring on or after the first day of the next calendar/tax year that begins on or after the latest date on which the MLI enters into force for both treaty partners. Takes effect for taxable periods beginning on or after the expiration of 6 calendar months (or as notified) from the latest date on which the MLI enters into force for both treaty partners.

Board Cases

Illustration 1: Withholding Tax & Ratification Status of Treaty Partner (India & Italy)

Question: An Indian company intends to withhold taxes on a payment to be made to an Italian Company on 10th April 2026.
In this regard, the Indian company wants to understand whether the provisions of the Multilateral Instrument (MLI) are applicable to read it along with the India-Italy DTAA.

Legal Analysis & Solution:

  1. Entry into Force for India: The MLI entered into force for India on 1st October 2019 following the deposit of its instrument of ratification on 25th June 2019.
  2. Bilateral Status Requirement: Under Article 34 and Article 35 of the MLI, the MLI enters into effect for a particular bilateral tax treaty only when both Contracting Jurisdictions have deposited their instruments of ratification, acceptance, or approval, and the stipulated timelines have elapsed.
  3. Application to Facts: As Italy had not deposited its instrument of ratification as on 10th April 2026, the MLI had not entered into force for Italy and did not modify the India-Italy DTAA.
  4. Conclusion: The provisions of the MLI are not applicable to the payment made on 10th April 2026. The Indian company must apply the provisions of the existing India-Italy DTAA in its unamended form without any MLI modifications!

Illustration 2: Scenarios on Covered Tax Agreements (CTAs) & Domestic Ratification

Question: Examine whether the MLI provisions will apply in the following independent scenarios:
(a) Country A and Country B are signatories to the MLI. Both countries have ratified the MLI agreement as per their domestic legislative process. Country A has notified the DTAA with Country B as a Covered Tax Agreement (CTA); however, Country B has not notified the DTAA with Country A.
(b) Country A - Country B DTAA is a CTA under Article 2 of the MLI, as both countries have notified the concerned DTAA. Country A has ratified the MLI under its domestic laws and deposited the ratification instrument with the OECD. Country B has not ratified the MLI owing to political instability in their country. Country A wishes to invoke MLI provisions while applying the Country A - Country B DTAA. Is the action of Country A valid?

Analysis & Statutory Solutions:

Scenario MLI Legal Principle Application & Validity
Scenario (a)
Unilateral CTA Notification		 Under Article 2(1)(a) of the MLI, a bilateral agreement qualifies as a Covered Tax Agreement (CTA) only if both Contracting Parties have notified the agreement to the Depositary. Since Country B has not notified the DTAA with Country A as a CTA, there is no mutual notification. Therefore, the MLI does not apply to the Country A - Country B DTAA.
Scenario (b)
Failure to Ratify by Treaty Partner		 Under Article 34 (Entry into Force), the MLI enters into force for a particular CTA only when both countries deposit their instruments of ratification and the timeline expires. Country B has not ratified the MLI; hence, the MLI has not entered into force with respect to Country B. Consequently, Country A cannot invoke MLI provisions against Country B. Action of Country A is INVALID!

TYK Question 1: Concept of BEPS & Its Adverse Economic Effects

Question: What do you understand by Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS)? Describe briefly its adverse effects on governments and domestic economies.

1. Meaning of BEPS:

Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) refers to tax planning strategies that exploit gaps and mismatches in tax rules across sovereign nations to make profits "disappear" for tax purposes or to artificially shift profits to locations where there is little or no real economic activity, but where taxes are low or zero, resulting in little or no overall corporate income tax being paid.

2. Four Primary Adverse Effects of BEPS:

  1. Fiscal Revenue Loss: Governments suffer significant corporate income tax losses, restricting public revenues and increasing the administrative and enforcement costs necessary to ensure compliance.
  2. Acute Under-Funding in Developing Countries: Developing economies are heavily reliant on corporate income tax. Revenue leakages from BEPS lead to severe under-funding of essential public infrastructure, education, and healthcare that foster long-term economic growth.
  3. Undermining Integrity & Fairness of the Tax System: When multinational enterprises pay negligible taxes on substantial real profits, individual wage earners and purely domestic businesses must bear a disproportionately higher share of the tax burden. This perceived unfairness degrades voluntary tax compliance across the broader economy.
  4. Distortion of Fair Competition: Domestic businesses, family-owned firms, and local startups that operate exclusively within domestic borders cannot shift profits across frontiers. They are placed at a significant competitive disadvantage compared to MNEs that exploit international tax arbitrage, harming market efficiency and fair competition.

TYK Question 2: OECD Action 1 Recommendations & Indian Adoption

Question: What are the significant OECD Recommendations under Action Plan 1 of BEPS to address the direct tax challenges of the digital economy? Which recommendation has been adopted in Indian tax laws?

Part I — Significant OECD Recommendations under Action Plan 1:

  • Modifying the Permanent Establishment (PE) Rule: Establishing a taxable nexus based on an enterprise maintaining a Significant Economic Presence (SEP) in another country's economy, even in fully dematerialized digital activities without physical presence.
  • Virtual Fixed Place of Business PE: Creating a PE when a foreign enterprise maintains a website on a server located in a jurisdiction or carries on systematic digital commercial activity through that website.
  • Withholding Tax on Digital Payments: Imposing a final standalone withholding tax on payments made for digital goods or services provided by foreign e-commerce providers.
  • Equalisation Levy: Imposing an equalisation levy on consideration for digital advertising and e-commerce transactions received by non-residents from domestic residents or local PEs.

Part II — Adoption in Indian Tax Laws: Significant Economic Presence [Section 9(9)]

India adopted the virtual fixed place / SEP recommendation by enacting Significant Economic Presence (SEP) under Section 9(9) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (which constitutes a statutory business connection in India):

Nature of Transaction / Activity Statutory Threshold (Previous Year)
(a) Transaction in goods, services, or property with any person in India (including download of data/software). Aggregate payments exceed ₹2 Crores.
(b) Systematic and continuous soliciting of business activities or engaging in interaction with users in India. Number of users in India is at least 3 Lakhs (300,000).

Statutory Caveats: SEP is constituted whether or not the agreement is entered in India, whether or not the non-resident has a residence or place of business in India, or renders services in India. Only income reasonably attributable to such transactions is deemed to accrue or arise in India.

TYK Question 3: Thin Capitalization in Income-tax Act, 2025 [Action 4 / Sec 177]

Question: Discuss the provisions contained in the Income-tax Act, 2025 in line with the OECD recommendations under Action Plan 4 of BEPS (Limitation on Interest Deductibility).

Provisions of Section 177 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (Corresponds to Sec 94B):

  1. Statutory Cap on Interest Deduction [Section 177(1)]: Where an Indian company, or a permanent establishment of a foreign company in India, being the borrower, incurs any expenditure by way of interest in respect of any debt issued by a non-resident Associated Enterprise (AE), the interest deductible under PGBP is capped at: $$\text{Maximum Deduction} = \min(\text{Actual Interest Paid to AE}, 30\% \text{ of EBITDA})$$ Total interest paid/payable in excess of 30% of EBITDA is disallowed.
  2. Deemed Debt from AE [Section 177(2)]: Where debt is issued by an unrelated third-party lender, but an AE provides an explicit or implicit guarantee to the lender or deposits a corresponding matching amount of funds with the lender, the debt is deemed to be issued by an AE, triggering Section 177.
  3. De Minimis Threshold: Section 177 is attracted only if the total interest expenditure paid/payable to non-resident AEs exceeds ₹1 Crore during the previous year.
  4. 8-Year Carry Forward [Section 177(3)]: Disallowed interest can be carried forward for up to 8 assessment years immediately succeeding the year of disallowance and set off against future business profits, subject to the 30% EBITDA ceiling in each subsequent year.
  5. Exemptions [Section 177(4)]: The provision does not apply to an Indian company or PE engaged in banking, insurance, a finance company in an IFSC, or a notified NBFC under Section 2(71).

TYK Question 4: Three-Tier Transfer Pricing Documentation [Action 13]

Question: Describe the three-tier structure for transfer pricing documentation mandated by BEPS Action Plan 13.

The 3-Tier BEPS Action 13 Structure:

Documentation Tier Core Contents & Standardized Purpose Delivery & Exchange Mechanism
1. Master File Provides tax administrations with a high-level strategic "blueprint" of the MNE group's global business operations, organizational structure, profit drivers, intangible assets (DEMPE), intercompany financing, and global financial/tax positions. Delivered by MNE constituent entities directly to local tax administrations (in India: Form 3CEAA by 30th November).
2. Local File Contains detailed, country-specific transactional information on related-party cross-border transactions, amounts involved, functional analysis (FAR), and economic benchmarking demonstrating selection of the Most Appropriate Method (MAM). Maintained by local entity and delivered directly to local tax authorities upon audit notice (in India: Section 171 / Form 3CEB audit report by 31st October).
3. Country-by-Country Report (CbCR) Comprehensive annual report showing key economic indicators across all tax jurisdictions where the group operates: revenues (unrelated vs related), PBT, corporate income-tax paid and accrued, stated capital, accumulated earnings, employee headcount, and tangible assets. Filed by the Ultimate Parent Entity (UPE) in its residence state (in India: Form 3CEAD u/s 511 for groups > ₹6,400 Cr); shared globally via automatic government-to-government exchange.

TYK Question 5: Nexus Approach in BEPS Action 5 & Indian Patent Box

Question: Explain the Nexus Approach recommended by OECD in BEPS Action Plan 5 which has been adopted in the Income-tax Act, 2025.

1. The Nexus Approach under Action Plan 5:

The Nexus Approach requires that the tax benefits of a preferential intellectual property (IP) regime (such as a "Patent Box") must be directly linked to the proportion of actual research and development (R&D) expenditures incurred by the taxpayer to develop the IP.
Income arising from exploiting IP must be attributed and taxed in the jurisdiction where substantial real R&D activities are undertaken, rather than where legal ownership or capital is located.

2. Adoption in Indian Tax Laws [Section 194(1) Table Sl. No. 2 / Sec 115BBF]:

India enacted the patent box regime to encourage indigenous innovation, commercialization, and retain high-value scientific jobs:

  • Concessional Tax Rate: Royalty income in respect of a patent developed and registered in India is taxed at a preferential flat rate of 10% (plus applicable surcharge and cess).
  • Eligible Assessee: A person resident in India who is the true and first inventor of the invention and registered as patentee under the Patents Act, 1970.
  • Mandatory 75% Domestic R&D Expenditure Threshold: Under the statutory definition, "developed" requires that at least 75% of the total expenditure was incurred in India by the eligible assessee for the invention in respect of which the patent was granted!

TYK Question 6: Ways Hybrid Mismatch Arrangements Achieve Tax Avoidance

Question: What are the ways in which hybrid mismatch arrangements are used to achieve unintended double non-taxation or long-term tax deferral?

Four Primary Mechanisms of Hybrid Mismatches:

  1. Double Deduction (DD) for a Single Economic Borrowal:
    Exploiting hybrid entities or dual-resident entities where a single interest expense on a single borrowing is claimed as a deductible business expense in two different sovereign tax jurisdictions against two separate income bases.
  2. Deduction without Corresponding Income Inclusion (D/NI):
    Generating a deductible expense in the payer jurisdiction without any corresponding tax inclusion in the recipient jurisdiction, typically through hybrid financial instruments treated as deductible debt/interest by the payer state but treated as tax-exempt equity/dividend by the payee state.
  3. Misuse of Foreign Tax Credits:
    Structuring transactions with hybrid entities or repo arrangements to claim duplicate or artificial foreign tax credits against domestic taxes where the underlying foreign income was either exempt or lightly taxed.
  4. Exploitation of Participation Exemption Regimes:
    Routing intercompany financing through intermediate conduit holding companies that qualify for participation exemptions or territorial exemptions on outbound dividends, ensuring profits escape taxation at all levels.
