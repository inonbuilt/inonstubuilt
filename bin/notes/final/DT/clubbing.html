Chapter 6: Income of Other Persons Included in Total Income of Assessee

Comprehensive statutory guide to Clubbing of Income under Sections 96 to 100 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (formerly Sections 60 to 65 of the 1961 Act). Covers transfer of income without asset, revocable transfers, spouse remuneration and substantial interest, firm and business investments, minor child income and statutory exceptions, cross transfers, HUF property conversion and partition, tax recovery from transferee, all 9 ICAI Illustrations and all 5 Test Your Knowledge practical questions.

Foundations of Tax Planning & Anti-Avoidance

Statutory Scheme & Rationale of Clubbing Provisions Sections 96 to 100

In general, an assessee is taxed only on income that legally accrues or arises to him or is received by him. However, taxpayers often attempt to minimize their tax liability by transferring income-yielding assets or fragmented income streams to their family members (such as spouse, minor children, or family trusts) who either have no other income or fall in lower income-tax slabs, while the transferor continues to enjoy indirect dominion and economic control over the underlying wealth.

Statutory Architecture under the Income-tax Act, 2025:

To counteract such tax avoidance mechanisms, Sections 96 to 100 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (corresponding to Sections 60 to 65 of the legacy Income-tax Act, 1961) mandate that in specified situations, the income earned by another person shall be compulsorily included (clubbed) in the total income of the assessee.

  • Section 96 [formerly Sec 60]: Transfer of income without transferring the underlying income-yielding asset.
  • Section 97 [formerly Sec 61 & 62]: Revocable transfer of assets and specific statutory exceptions.
  • Section 98 [formerly Sec 63]: Definitions of "Transfer" and "Revocable Transfer".
  • Section 99 [formerly Sec 64]: Clubbing of income in the hands of an individual (spouse remuneration, transferred assets, partnership firm capital, minor child income, and HUF property conversion).
  • Section 100 [formerly Sec 65]: Liability of transferee for payment of tax on clubbed income.
Fundamental Statutory Principle: Head of Income Remains Unchanged

When income of another person is clubbed in the hands of the transferor, it retains its original character and head of income. It is computed under the relevant head (Salary, House Property, PGBP, Capital Gains, or Other Sources) after allowing all eligible statutory deductions and allowances before being aggregated with the transferor's other income. For example, if rental income from a let-out house property is clubbed, the 30% statutory deduction under Section 22(1)(a) is deducted first!

Statutory Symmetry

Income Includes Loss [Section 99(5)(d)] Section 99(5)(d) | Formerly Sec 64 Expl. 2

A taxpayer cannot argue that clubbing provisions apply only to positive income and that losses must remain with the transferee. The statute establishes full symmetry between profits and losses:

Statutory Mandate of Section 99(5)(d):

As per Section 99(5)(d), the term "income" includes "loss". Accordingly, where any specified income arising to a spouse, minor child, or son's wife is a negative figure (i.e., a business loss, capital loss, or house property loss), such loss must be taken into account while computing the total income of the individual transferor.

Landmark Judicial Precedent: CIT v. J.H. Gotla (1985) 156 ITR 323 (SC)

The Supreme Court settled that the object of the clubbing provisions is to treat the transferor as if he had never transferred the asset. Consequently, if the transferee sustains a loss in a business or investment funded by the transferred asset, the transferor is entitled to set off that loss against his other taxable income under the general provisions of Chapter VII (Sections 110 to 119 / legacy Sections 70 to 80), subject to statutory loss restrictions.

Comparative Legal Analysis

Distinction Between Section 97 and Section 99 Key Exam Distinction

Parameter Section 97 (Revocable Transfer of Assets) Section 99 (Individual Clubbing Provisions)
Applicability to Assessees Applies to ANY person (Individual, HUF, Firm, LLP, Company, AOP/BOI, AJP). Applies STRICTLY to an INDIVIDUAL assessee.
Identity of Transferee Any person (relative or non-relative, individual or corporate entity). Specific family categories: Spouse, Son's Wife, Minor Child, Trust for their benefit, or HUF.
Nature of Transfer Applies only to REVOCABLE transfers (whether made with or without consideration). Applies to BOTH revocable and irrevocable transfers, provided made without adequate consideration.
Consideration Aspect Even if transfer is for adequate consideration, if it is revocable, income is clubbed! Adequacy of consideration eliminates clubbing (except remuneration u/s 99(1)(a)(i) and minor child u/s 99(1)(c)).
Accretion to Asset Rule Income from accretion is generally not covered unless the deed explicitly retains revocable power over accretions. Income on accretion (second-generation income) is STRICTLY NOT clubbed (*CIT v. M. S. S. Rajan*), except in case of minor child.
Separation of Fruit from the Tree

Transfer of Income Without Transfer of Asset [Section 96] Section 96 | Formerly Sec 60

Section 96 codifies the classic tax jurisprudence principle: "You cannot separate the fruit from the tree for tax purposes while retaining the tree." If an assessee attempts to assign, transfer, or surrender an income stream while retaining ownership and control over the income-generating property, the income continues to be taxed in the transferor's hands.

Statutory Mandate of Section 96:

All income arising to any person by virtue of a transfer from assets whose ownership remains with the transferor shall be chargeable to income-tax as the income of the transferor and shall be included in his total income.

Statutory Conditions for Applicability:
  1. The assessee transfers an income arising from an asset without transferring the asset itself.
  2. The transfer may be revocable or irrevocable — the irrevocability of the assignment does NOT prevent clubbing!
  3. The transfer may have been effected before or after the commencement of this Act.
  4. The rule applies to all categories of assessees (Individuals, HUFs, Firms, Companies, etc.).
Important Judicial Distinction: Section 96 vs Diversion by Overriding Title

Section 96 strikes down voluntary assignments of income. However, where income is diverted at source by an anterior overriding legal obligation (e.g., a court decree ordering maintenance directly charged upon an estate, as held in CIT v. Sitaldas Tirathdas), the income never accrues to the assessee at all and Section 96 does not apply. In contrast, a private deed of covenant assigning future rental income to an elderly parent without transferring the property falls squarely under Section 96 and is fully clubbed.

Retaining Indirect Strings of Control

Revocable Transfer of Assets [Section 97(1) & 97(2)] Section 97 | Formerly Sec 61 & 62

Where an assessee transfers an asset to another person, but attaches a clause or condition that enables him to recall, revoke, or re-assume control over the asset or its income at a future point in time, the law treats the transfer as revocable. Because the transferor retains strings attached to the property, the income generated by the asset is clubbed back into his total income.

Charging Mandate of Section 97(1):

All income arising to any person by virtue of a revocable transfer of assets shall be chargeable to income-tax as the income of the transferor and shall be included in his total income.

Statutory Exceptions: When Revocable Transfers Are NOT Clubbed [Section 97(2)]

Section 97(2) carves out specific protected safe-harbors where the clubbing provisions of Section 97(1) will not apply during a temporary non-revocable window:

1. Transfer by Way of Trust

Transfer is made by way of trust which is not revocable during the lifetime of the beneficiary, provided the transferor derives no direct or indirect benefit from such income during the beneficiary's lifetime.

2. Direct Transfer to Transferee

Any other transfer which is not revocable during the lifetime of the transferee, provided the transferor derives no direct or indirect benefit from such income.

Critical Statutory Trigger: Cessation of Exception [Section 97(2) Proviso]

As soon as the power to revoke the transfer arises or is exercised (for instance, upon the demise of the beneficiary/transferee), the statutory protection ends immediately! All income arising from the asset from that date onwards shall be included in the total income of the transferor.

Statutory Construction

Definitions of "Transfer" and "Revocable Transfer" [Section 98] Section 98 / Sec 97(3) | Formerly Sec 63

For the purposes of Sections 96 and 97, the Act provides comprehensive definitions of the terms "transfer" and "revocable transfer" to prevent clever drafting from evading tax:

Statutory Definitions under Section 98 [Section 97(3)]:

  • "Transfer": Includes any settlement, trust, covenant, agreement or arrangement.
  • "Revocable Transfer": A transfer is statutorily deemed to be revocable if:
    1. It contains any provision for the re-transfer, directly or indirectly, of the whole or any part of the income or assets to the transferor; OR
    2. It in any way gives the transferor a right to re-assume power, directly or indirectly, over the whole or any part of the income or assets.
Numerical Example: Term-Based Revocable Trust vs Lifetime Exception Exam Focus

Facts: On 01.04.2026, Mr. Anand transfers commercial bonds worth ₹40,00,000 yielding an annual interest of ₹3,60,000 to an irrevocable trust for the benefit of his nephew, Master Chirag (aged 22 years). The trust deed contains a clause specifying: "The trust shall remain irrevocable for a fixed period of 8 years. On 31.03.2034, the entire trust corpus along with accumulated investments shall revert back to Mr. Anand." During Tax Year 2026-27, the trust earns interest of ₹3,60,000. Examine in whose hands the interest will be taxed.

Statutory Analysis:
Statutory Parameter Factual Evaluation & Legal Position
Is the transfer revocable? YES. As per Section 98, the deed provides for the re-transfer of the asset to the transferor after 8 years. It is statutorily deemed to be a revocable transfer.
Does Section 97(2) exception apply? NO. The statutory exception under Section 97(2) requires the transfer to be non-revocable during the entire lifetime of the beneficiary. A non-revocation period of 8 years (or any fixed term) does not satisfy the lifetime condition!
Final Tax Treatment for T.Y. 2026-27 The entire annual interest of ₹3,60,000 is fully clubbed in the hands of Mr. Anand under Section 97(1) right from Tax Year 2026-27 itself.
Employment & Directorship Remuneration

Remuneration to Spouse from Concern with Substantial Interest [Section 99(1)(a)(i)] Section 99(1)(a)(i) | Formerly Sec 64(1)(ii)

Where an individual has a substantial interest in a business entity (company, firm, sole proprietorship, AOP) and his or her spouse receives any remuneration from that concern, the remuneration is prima facie clubbed in the hands of the individual.

Statutory Rule under Section 99(1)(a)(i):

In computing the total income of any individual, there shall be included all such income as arises directly or indirectly to the spouse of such individual by way of salary, commission, fees or any other form of remuneration (whether in cash or in kind) from a concern in which such individual has a substantial interest.

Meaning of "Substantial Interest" [Section 2(94) / formerly 2(32)]:
  • In case of a Company: The individual, either by himself or along with his relatives, beneficially holds equity shares carrying not less than 20% of the total voting power at any time during the previous year.
  • In case of Non-Corporate Concerns (Firm/AOP): The individual, either by himself or along with his relatives, is entitled to not less than 20% of the profits of such concern at any time during the previous year.
  • "Relative" for this section includes: Husband, wife, brother, sister, or any lineal ascendant or descendant of that individual.
Rule Where Both Spouses Have Substantial Interest

Where both husband and wife have a substantial interest in the concern and both are in receipt of remuneration from such concern, the remuneration received by both spouses will be clubbed in the hands of that spouse whose total income (excluding such remuneration) is higher.
Consistency Principle: Once such remuneration is clubbed in the total income of either spouse for any tax year, it shall continue to be clubbed in that spouse's hands in subsequent years, unless the Assessing Officer is satisfied, after giving that spouse a reasonable opportunity of being heard, that it is necessary to assess it in the hands of the other spouse.

Statutory Exception (Proviso): Technical or Professional Qualification

Proviso to Section 99(1)(a)(i):

Clubbing provisions shall NOT apply to any income arising to the spouse where the spouse possesses technical or professional qualifications and the income is solely attributable to the application of his or her technical or professional knowledge and experience.

Landmark Interpretation: CIT v. Smt. R. Bharati (1999) 240 ITR 697 (Mad)

The Madras High Court held that the words "technical or professional qualifications" do NOT necessarily require a formal degree or diploma conferred by a recognized university. The term must be given a broad meaning to encompass any occupation requiring specialized skill, experience, and competence. A person possessing demonstrable skill and competence (such as an experienced fashion model, skilled interior designer, or culinary artist) qualifies as professionally qualified under the proviso!

Direct & Indirect Asset Transfers

Income from Assets Transferred to Spouse [Section 99(1)(a)(ii)] Section 99(1)(a)(ii) | Formerly Sec 64(1)(iv)

Section 99(1)(a)(ii) prevents individuals from splitting their investment portfolios or property holdings with their spouse without receiving adequate commercial consideration.

Statutory Rule under Section 99(1)(a)(ii):

Where an individual transfers any asset (other than house property) directly or indirectly to his or her spouse otherwise than for adequate consideration or not in connection with an agreement to live apart, any income arising directly or indirectly from such transferred asset shall be included in the total income of the transferor spouse.

1. Dual Subsistence of Relationship

The marital relationship between the transferor and transferee must subsist BOTH at the time of the transfer of the asset AND at the time the income accrues or arises. If an asset is gifted before marriage (e.g. to a fiancee), clubbing is NOT attracted even after marriage! (Philip John Plasket Thomas v. CIT). Similarly, post-divorce income is not clubbed.

2. Natural Love & Affection ≠ Consideration

Adequate consideration means consideration having measurable monetary value. "Natural love and affection" does not constitute adequate consideration for tax purposes. A transfer made out of love and affection without monetary consideration attracts full clubbing.

3. House Property Carve-Out [Section 25(a)]

If house property is transferred to the spouse without adequate consideration, Section 25(a) [formerly Sec 27(i)] steps in first. The transferor is deemed to be the statutory owner of the house property, and rental income is taxed directly in his hands under Income from House Property. Section 99(1)(a)(ii) does not double-club it.

4. Accretion / Second-Generation Rule

Income arising directly from the transferred asset is clubbed. However, income on accretion (income earned by reinvesting the accumulated income) is STRICTLY NOT clubbed with the transferor (CIT v. M. S. S. Rajan (2001) 252 ITR 126 (Mad)).

Proportionate Attribution Formula

Transferred Assets Invested in Business or Partnership Firm [Section 99(2)] Section 99(2) | Formerly Sec 64(1) Expl. 3

Where gifted funds or transferred assets are invested by the spouse or son's wife in a sole proprietary business or as a capital contribution in a partnership firm/LLP, Section 99(2) provides a mandatory mathematical formula to determine the exact proportion of profit or interest to be clubbed.

$$\text{Income to be Clubbed} = \text{Total Income from Business or Interest on Capital} \times \frac{\text{Value of Gifted Asset as on 1st Day of Tax Year}}{\text{Total Capital Contribution as on 1st Day of Tax Year}}$$

Two Strict Statutory Ground Rules under Section 99(2):

  1. The "First Day of the Tax Year" Rule: The ratio is determined STRICTLY based on the capital balances as on the first day of the relevant tax year (i.e. 1st April). Any additional capital introduced or gifted funds invested subsequent to the 1st day of the tax year (e.g. on 2nd April, 1st May, etc.) are completely ignored for the current tax year! They will be factored in only from the 1st day of the next tax year.
  2. Share of Profit from Firm is 100% EXEMPT: Under Schedule III, Sl. No. 2 [formerly Section 10(2A)], a partner's share in the total income of a partnership firm is totally exempt from income-tax. Clubbing provisions apply only to taxable income; exempt income is NOT clubbed! Therefore, only interest on capital paid to the spouse is subject to clubbing.

In-Law Wealth Transfers

Income from Assets Transferred to Son's Wife [Section 99(1)(b)] Section 99(1)(b) | Formerly Sec 64(1)(vi)

Section 99(1)(b) extends clubbing to transfers made to a daughter-in-law (son's wife) without adequate consideration:

Statutory Rule under Section 99(1)(b):

In computing the total income of any individual, there shall be included all such income as arises directly or indirectly to the son's wife of such individual from assets transferred directly or indirectly on or after 1st June, 1973, by such individual to the son's wife, otherwise than for adequate consideration.

  • Subsistence of Relationship: The relationship of father-in-law / mother-in-law and daughter-in-law must exist both at the time of transfer and at the time income accrues. A gift made to a prospective daughter-in-law prior to the son's marriage is NOT covered!
  • Indirect Transfers Covered: Transfers routed through holding-subsidiary structures, private companies, or mutual arrangements are fully struck down.

Ltd. subject to a condition that out of the rental income, a sum of ₹42,000 per annum shall be utilized for the benefit of her son's wife. Mrs. Komal claims that the amount of ₹42,000 (utilized by her son's wife) should not be included in her total income as she no longer owned the property. Statutory Issue Legal Analysis & Findings

Trusts & Intermediary Entities

Transfers to Third Persons for Benefit of Spouse or Son's Wife [Section 99(1)(d)] Section 99(1)(d) | Formerly Sec 64(1)(vii) & (viii)

To prevent individuals from circumventing Sections 99(1)(a)(ii) and 99(1)(b) by creating private trusts or appointing intermediaries, Section 99(1)(d) targets all transfers made to third parties for the immediate or deferred benefit of the spouse or son's wife:

Statutory Scope of Section 99(1)(d):

In computing the total income of any individual, there shall be included all such income as arises directly or indirectly to any person or Association of Persons (AOP) from assets transferred directly or indirectly by such individual without adequate consideration, to the extent to which the income from such assets is for the immediate or deferred benefit of his/her spouse or son's wife.

Complete Minor Ring-Fencing

Clubbing of Minor Child's Income [Section 99(1)(c)] Section 99(1)(c) | Formerly Sec 64(1A)

Section 99(1)(c) incorporates an exhaustive blanket clubbing regime for all income accruing to a minor child, subject to three narrow statutory exceptions:

The Three Statutory Rules of Minor Child Clubbing:

  1. Blanket Clubbing in Parent with Higher Income: All income accruing or arising to a minor child (including a minor married daughter, step-child, or legally adopted child) shall be included in the total income of that parent whose total income (excluding the minor's income) is higher.
  2. Where Marriage Does Not Subsist: Where the marriage of the parents does not subsist (e.g. divorce or separation), the minor's income shall be included in the total income of that parent who maintains the minor child in the relevant tax year.
  3. Attainment of Majority During the Year: If a child attains majority (completes 18 years of age) during the tax year, income earned up to the date of attaining majority is clubbed with the parent, while income earned after attaining majority is assessed separately in the child's own hands!

The 3 Statutory Exceptions: When Minor's Income is NOT Clubbed

1. Disability under Section 154

Income of a minor child suffering from any disability of the nature specified in Section 154 [formerly Section 80U] (e.g., blindness, permanent locomotor disability, mental retardation, autism). Assessed in minor's own return.

2. Manual Work Done by Child

Income earned by the minor child on account of any manual labour or physical work performed by him or her. Taxable in minor's own hands.

3. Specialized Skill or Talent

Income earned by the child on account of any activity involving the application of his or her specialized skill, talent, or specialized knowledge and experience (e.g. child artist, sports champion, musical prodigy, chess grandmaster).

Critical Regime Alert: Exemption of ₹1,500 per Child [Schedule III, Sl. No. 17 / Sec 10(32)]

Under Schedule III (Table: Sl. No. 17) [formerly Section 10(32)], an individual is entitled to an exemption of up to ₹1,500 per minor child whose income is clubbed in his hands.
EXAM TRAP: This exemption of ₹1,500 is available ONLY IF the parent has exercised the option of shifting out of the default tax regime (i.e. under the normal/optional regime). Under the default tax regime of Section 202, this exemption is COMPLETELY WITHDRAWN (NIL)!

Piercing the Circuitous Veil

Cross Transfers & Reciprocal Arrangements Landmark Judicial Precedent

Taxpayers frequently attempt to defeat the direct wording of Section 99 by designing reciprocal or circuitous transfers involving two or more family units. For example, Brother A gifts an asset to Brother B's wife, while Brother B simultaneously gifts an asset of equivalent or similar value to Brother A's wife. Neither husband transferred an asset directly to his own wife. The Supreme Court dismantled this tax avoidance device through the doctrine of Cross Transfers.

The Supreme Court Doctrine: CIT v. Keshavji Morarji [1967] 66 ITR 142 (SC)

The Apex Court held that if two or more transfers are inter-connected and are parts of the same transaction in such a manner that it can be reasonably concluded that the circuitous method was adopted as a device to evade tax, the clubbing provisions are fully attracted.

  • Mutual Consideration: Each transfer constitutes the consideration for the other transfer, whether mutual or otherwise.
  • Deemed Direct Transfer: The law pierces the circular path and treats the transaction as an indirect transfer by each individual to his own spouse or minor child!
  • Quantum of Clubbing (Smaller Amount Rule): Where the two gifts are for unequal amounts, the cross transfer doctrine applies only to the extent of the matching smaller amount. The excess remains taxable in the hands of the actual recipient.

Throwing into the Common Hotchpot

Conversion of Self-Acquired Property into HUF Property [Section 99(3)] Section 99(3) | Formerly Sec 64(2)

Under Hindu Law, a coparcener has the right to convert his separate individual property into joint family property by impressing it with the character of HUF property (often referred to as "throwing into the common hotchpot"). Since an HUF is a distinct taxable entity with its own basic exemption slabs, taxpayers historically used this mechanism to split their taxable income. Section 99(3) specifically plugs this loophole.

Statutory Framework of Section 99(3):

Where an individual, being a member of a Hindu Undivided Family, after 31st December 1969, converts his self-acquired property into property belonging to the family otherwise than for adequate consideration, the tax implications operate in two distinct chronological phases:

  • Phase 1 – Before Partition of the HUF: The entire income derived from such converted property shall be included in the total income of the individual transferor. The HUF cannot claim that this income belongs to the family.
  • Phase 2 – After Partition of the HUF: Where the converted property is divided amongst the members of the family upon a total or partial partition:
    1. Income from the share allotted to the transferor individual: Assessed in his own hands.
    2. Income from the share allotted to the spouse of the transferor: Deemed to arise from assets transferred indirectly by the individual and SHALL BE CLUBBED in the hands of the individual transferor!
    3. Income from the share allotted to any minor child: Clubbed in the hands of the parent having higher income under Section 99(1)(c).
    4. Income from the share allotted to major children: Taxable in the respective hands of the major children.

Secondary Tax Liability & Enforcement

Liability of Transferee for Payment of Tax [Section 100] Section 100 | Formerly Sec 65

When income of another person (e.g. spouse, minor child, son's wife) is clubbed in the hands of the transferor, the primary tax assessment is made on the transferor. However, what happens if the transferor fails to pay the tax assessed on such clubbed income, or what if the revenue authorities wish to recover the tax directly from the person who actually possesses the funds? Section 100 establishes a secondary recovery mechanism.

Statutory Mechanism under Section 100:

Where by reason of the provisions of Sections 96, 97, or 99, any income is included in the total income of any individual, a notice of demand under Section 288 [formerly Section 156] may be served upon the person to whom the asset was transferred (the transferee) or upon the firm/partnership.

Statutory Terms of Recovery:
  • The transferee is liable to pay that portion of the tax levied on the transferor which is strictly attributable to the income so included.
  • Joint and Several Liability: Where any such asset is held jointly by more than one person, they shall be jointly and severally liable to pay such tax.
  • Proportionate Tax Formula: $$\text{Tax Recoverable from Transferee} = \text{Total Tax Assessed on Transferor} \times \frac{\text{Clubbed Income Included}}{\text{Total Income of Transferor}}$$

TYK Question 5: Fashion Designer Paying Salary to Model Wife ICAI TYK 5

Problem Statement: Naresh is a fashion designer having a lucrative business. His wife is a model. Naresh pays her a monthly salary of ₹10,000. The Assessing Officer, while admitting that the salary is an admissible business deduction in computing the total income of Naresh, has applied the provisions of Section 99(1)(a) and clubbed the salary income of his wife in Naresh's hands. Discuss the correctness of the action of the Assessing Officer.

Statutory Analysis & Landmark Ruling:

This issue is directly governed by the principles laid down by the Madras High Court in CIT v. Smt. R. Bharati (1999) 240 ITR 697, where the interpretation of the terms "professional qualifications" and "knowledge" under the proviso to Section 99(1)(a)(i) [formerly Section 64(1)(ii)] came up for consideration:

High Court Legal Principles on "Professional Qualifications":

  • The words "technical or professional qualifications" do not necessarily connote a formal academic qualification conferred by a recognized university after examining the candidate who has undergone a course of study.
  • The term "qualification" must be given a wide meaning as referring to the qualities, competence, and skill which are required to be possessed by a person performing the work that he or she does, so long as that work is capable of being regarded as professional.
  • Modeling is a recognized profession requiring a high degree of poise, personal skill, competence, physical fitness, and aesthetic experience. A person possessing such skill and experience in her line of work is professionally qualified for the purpose of the proviso to Section 99(1)(a)(i).

Is Mrs. Naresh professionally qualified? YES. As an experienced model possessing skill and competence, she falls squarely within the broad judicial interpretation of professional qualifications.
Is the salary solely attributable to her work? YES. The Assessing Officer admitted that the salary of ₹10,000 p.m. is an admissible business expenditure incurred for the fashion business.

Key Principle 1: 1st Day Capital Rule

In business or partnership firm investments under Section 99(2), the ratio is calculated based strictly on the capital balance as on 1st April. Additions during the year are ignored for the current tax year!

Key Principle 2: Accretion Not Clubbed

Income generated from reinvesting the income of a transferred asset (second-generation income) is NEVER clubbed with the transferor (*CIT v. M.S.S. Rajan*), except in the case of a minor child!

Key Principle 3: House Property Override

Gifts of house property to spouse or minor child fall under Section 25(a) deemed ownership first. The transferor is assessed directly as the owner under House Property; Section 99 does not apply.
