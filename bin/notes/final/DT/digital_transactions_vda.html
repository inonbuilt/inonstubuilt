Taxation of Digital Transactions, Equalisation Levy & Virtual Digital Assets (VDAs)

An exhaustive CA Final treatise mastering the digital taxation framework: e-commerce operating models (B2B, B2C, Marketplace vs. Inventory), cross-border taxation hurdles (Permanent Establishment nexus, characterization, and profit attribution), OECD BEPS Action Plan 1, Significant Economic Presence (SEP), phase-out of the Equalisation Levy, comprehensive Virtual Digital Asset (VDA) regime under Section 2(111), flat 30% tax charging under Section 194(1) [legacy 115BBH], strict loss ring-fencing, 1% TDS under Section 393(1) [legacy 194S], and Crypto-asset Reporting Framework (CARF) obligations under Sections 509 & 446.

1 Chapter Overview & Statutory Scheme of Digital Taxation

Statutory Map under Income-tax Act, 2025 Chapter IV • Chapter XII-G • Chapter XVII-B

The digitalization of the global economy has revolutionized commercial transactions, eliminating the necessity of physical presence for cross-border market penetration. To capture digital value creation and regulate cryptographic assets, the direct tax framework operates through an integrated statutory architecture:

Digital Economy & E-Commerce
Section 402(12) defines electronic commerce. Significant Economic Presence (SEP) under Section 5 expands source-based nexus based on revenue (> ₹2 Cr) and active users (> 3,00,000). Section 393(1) Table Sl. 8(v) levies 0.1% TDS on e-commerce operators.
Virtual Digital Assets (VDAs)
Section 2(111) defines VDAs (cryptocurrencies & NFTs). Section 194(1) Table Sl. 4 levies a flat 30% tax on transfer gains with strict loss isolation. Section 92(2)(m) taxes gifted VDAs under IFOS.
VDA Withholding & CARF Reporting
Section 393(1) Table Sl. 8(vi) mandates 1% TDS on transfer payments (₹50k/₹10k thresholds). Section 509 implements the global Crypto-asset Reporting Framework (CARF) with stringent penalties under Section 446.

2 What is Electronic Commerce? Core Business Models

Statutory Definition & Commercial Classifications Section 402(12)

Under Section 402(12), “electronic commerce” is statutorily defined to mean the supply of goods or services or both, including digital products over digital or electronic network.

1. The Four Primary E-Commerce Segments
B2B Business-to-Business

Inter-company wholesale transactions, automated supply-chain procurement, enterprise software integration, and bulk commodity exchanges.

B2C Business-to-Consumer

Commercial sales of tangible merchandise, electronic gadgets, streaming entertainment, and digital downloads directly to retail end-users.

C2C Consumer-to-Consumer

Online consumer marketplaces, classified advertisement portals, and peer-to-peer resale platforms facilitating transactions between private individuals.

C2B Consumer-to-Business

Freelance service platforms, stock photography portals, influencer affiliate marketing, and crowdsourced consumer initiatives.

2. Marketplace Model vs. Inventory-Based Model

As recognized under direct tax regulations and Consolidated FDI Policy:

Operating Feature Marketplace-Based Model Inventory-Based Model
Core Nature Provides an information technology platform on a digital/electronic network to act as a facilitator between third-party buyers and sellers. The e-commerce entity owns the inventory of goods and services and sells directly to consumers.
Ownership of Inventory The marketplace operator does not own or take title to the goods sold on the platform. The e-commerce entity maintains legal ownership, warehousing, and inventory risk.
Primary Revenue Stream Commission fees, listing fees, fulfillment charges, and digital advertising space sold to sellers. Direct sales revenue (retail price minus cost of goods sold).
TDS Applicability Subject to TDS @ 0.1% on gross sales under Section 393(1) Table Sl. 8(v) [legacy 194-O]. Normal corporate assessment provisions; TDS u/s 393(1) on purchases where applicable.

3 The Three Core International Tax Challenges in Digital Commerce

Nexus, Characterization & Data Attribution Hurdles International Jurisprudence

Traditional international tax treaties (OECD & UN Model Conventions) were drafted in the 1920s predicated on physical brick-and-mortar factories. The digital economy exposes three fatal structural vulnerabilities in this legacy framework:

1. The Nexus Challenge (Permanent Establishment Breakdown)

Under Article 5 and Section 9(1)(i), a source country can tax the business profits of a foreign enterprise only if it maintains a Permanent Establishment (PE) — a fixed place of business or dependent agent. Digital conglomerates operate without physical offices, warehouses, or personnel in market jurisdictions, servicing millions of Indian consumers entirely via automated cloud servers situated in low-tax countries, completely escaping source taxation.

2. The Characterization Challenge

Digital business models blur the legal demarcation between business profits, royalties, and fees for technical services (FTS):
• Software-as-a-Service (SaaS): Is payment for accessing cloud accounting software a royalty for use of copyright, or pure business profit?
• Online Subscriptions: Are fees paid to read digital journals or access legal databases royalties for process/information, or business receipts?
• Targeted Online Advertising: Are advertising payments made to search engines business profits or technical service fees?

3. The Data Valuation & User Attribution Challenge

In modern digital platforms (social networks, search engines, ride-hailing apps), economic value is co-created by user engagement, search habits, uploaded content, and continuous algorithmic data harvesting. Traditional transfer pricing rules (functions, assets, risks - FAR analysis) fail to attribute any taxable profit to the jurisdiction where the users reside and generate this valuable data.

4 OECD BEPS Action Plan 1 & Significant Economic Presence (SEP)

Action Plan 1 Recommendations & The Indian SEP Framework BEPS Action 1 • Sec 5 / Sec 9(1)(i)

Under the G20-OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) Action Plan 1, member nations recognized that the digital economy exacerbates BEPS risks. The committee recommended three domestic safeguard options: (i) a new nexus based on Significant Economic Presence; (ii) a withholding tax on digital transactions; and (iii) an equalisation levy.

The Significant Economic Presence (SEP) Test in Indian Law

India was among the first nations to enact the Significant Economic Presence (SEP) doctrine under Section 9(1)(i) [read with Section 5 of ITA 2025], establishing a statutory ‘business connection’ based strictly on economic interaction, regardless of physical location:

SEP Limb / Test Statutory Condition & Quantitative Threshold Scope of Coverage
Limb A: Revenue / Transaction Threshold Aggregate payments arising from transactions in respect of any goods, services, or property carried out by a non-resident with any person in India exceed ₹2 Crore (INR 20,000,000) during the tax year. Includes digital transactions, cloud hosting, software/data downloads, digital content streaming, and online subscriptions.
Limb B: User / Interaction Threshold Systematic and continuous soliciting of business activities or engaging in interaction with 3,00,000 users (3 Lakh active users) in India through digital means. Covers social networking platforms, search engines, ride-hailing networks, interactive gaming, and online streaming applications.
Physical Presence Expressly Irrelevant:
The statute explicitly mandates that an arrangement shall constitute a Significant Economic Presence in India whether or not:
  • (i) The agreement for such transactions or activities is entered into in India;
  • (ii) The non-resident has a residence or place of business in India; or
  • (iii) The non-resident renders services in India!

Interplay with Tax Treaties (DTAA): Under Section 90(2), a foreign tax resident can claim the beneficial provisions of a DTAA. Since traditional tax treaties require a physical Permanent Establishment (PE) under Article 5, foreign enterprises resident in treaty partner countries continue to be protected from SEP taxation unless they have a fixed place PE in India.

5 Equalisation Levy: Genesis, Operation & Legislative Phase-Out

The Rise and Sunset of Equalisation Levy FA 2016 • FA 2020 • FA 2025 Abolition

Pending a global multilateral consensus on digital taxation (OECD Pillar One), India implemented unilateral Equalisation Levies as interim equalizers:

Levy Classification Enacting Statute Rate & Scope Legislative Status & Sunsetting
Online Advertisement Levy Chapter VIII, Finance Act, 2016 6% on gross consideration exceeding ₹1,00,000 received by a non-resident without an Indian PE from an Indian business resident for specified online advertising services. ABOLISHED by Finance Act, 2025 in alignment with international bilateral agreements and transitional consensus.
E-Commerce Supply Levy Finance Act, 2020 Amendment 2% on gross consideration received by non-resident e-commerce operators exceeding ₹2 Crore per annum for online supply of goods or facilitation of services to Indian residents. WITHDRAWN w.e.f. 01.08.2024 by Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 to foster digital trade and prevent retaliatory international tariffs.

6 Section 393(1) Table Sl. 8(v): TDS on E-Commerce Operators

Withholding Tax on E-Commerce Marketplaces Sec 393(1) Table Sl. 8(v) • Legacy Sec 194-O

To establish a digital audit trail and monitor transactions facilitated through online marketplaces, a dedicated withholding regime operates under Section 393(1) Table Sl. No. 8(v):

Rate Concessional 0.1% Rate

Tax is deductible @ 0.1% (reduced from 1% to prevent working capital blockage for e-commerce sellers) on the gross amount of sales of goods or provision of services.

Deductor E-Commerce Operator

Every e-commerce operator responsible for paying or crediting consideration to a resident e-commerce participant.

Threshold ₹5 Lakh Exemption

No deduction is required if the participant is an individual or HUF, gross annual sales ≤ ₹5,00,000, and PAN/Aadhaar is furnished.

Deemed Credit & Overlap Precedence:
• Direct Payments Included: Any payment made directly by a purchaser to an e-commerce participant (e.g. Cash on Delivery or direct UPI) is statutorily deemed to be an amount credited/paid by the e-commerce operator and subject to 0.1% TDS.
• Statutory Precedence: Once tax is deducted under this entry, no other TDS provision (Section 194C contractor, Section 194J professional, or Section 194Q purchase of goods) shall apply to the same transaction.

7 Section 2(111): Definition & Scope of Virtual Digital Assets

Meaning of ‘Virtual Digital Asset’ (VDA) Section 2(111) • Legacy Sec 2(47A)

Under Section 2(111), “virtual digital asset” (VDA) is statutorily defined to mean:

(a) Cryptographic Codes & Tokens: Any information or code or number or token (not being Indian currency or foreign currency), generated through cryptographic means or otherwise, by whatever name called, providing a digital representation of value exchanged with or without consideration, with the promise or representation of having inherent value, or functioning as a store of value or a unit of account.
(b) Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs): A non-fungible token or any other token of similar nature, by whatever name called, which provides cryptographic ownership rights over underlying digital or physical assets.
(c) Notified Digital Assets: Any other digital asset as the Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, specify.
Statutory Exclusions from the VDA Definition:
The Central Government has specifically excluded the following digital assets from the ambit of VDA:
  • • Gift cards or vouchers that can be redeemed for goods or services or discounts from specified merchants;
  • • Mileage points, reward points, or loyalty points awarded under loyalty programs without direct monetary redemption;
  • • Subscriptions to websites, software platforms, or mobile applications.

VDA Need Not Be a Capital Asset: The statutory definition of ‘transfer’ under Section 2(109) applies to any virtual digital asset. Whether held as an investment, stock-in-trade, or personal asset, any sale, exchange, or relinquishment of a VDA triggers tax liability exclusively under the special charging code of Section 194(1).

8 Section 194(1) Table Sl. 4: Flat 30% Tax & Strict Ring-Fencing

Special Tax Rate on Income from Transfer of VDAs Sec 194(1) Table Sl. 4 • Legacy Sec 115BBH

Section 194(1) [Table: Sl. No. 4] prescribes an uncompromising, ring-fenced tax regime for income arising from the transfer of any virtual digital asset:

Statutory Element Legislative Mandate & Restriction Statutory Impact on Taxpayer
Flat Tax Rate 30% (+ applicable Surcharge + 4% HEC) Effective basic rate of 31.20% (prior to surcharge). Applies regardless of holding period (no long-term concessional rate).
Permissible Deductions Cost of Acquisition ONLY Under clause (a), no deduction is allowed in respect of any expenditure (other than cost of acquisition). Disallowed expenses include: exchange commissions, trading fees, transfer gas charges, electricity, mining equipment depreciation, and interest on loans!
Set-Off Against Other Heads STRICTLY PROHIBITED Under clause (b), no loss from transfer of VDA can be set off against income under any other head (Salaries, House Property, PGBP, Capital Gains, or IFOS).
Inter-VDA Loss Set-Off STRICTLY PROHIBITED Loss from the transfer of one VDA (e.g. loss on an NFT) cannot be set off against gain from another VDA (e.g. gain on Bitcoin). Each profitable VDA transaction is taxed @ 30% in isolation!
Carry Forward of Losses NIL CARRY FORWARD Unabsorbed loss arising from VDA transfers lapses in the year of occurrence and cannot be carried forward to any subsequent assessment year.
Basic Exemption Adjustment NO ADJUSTMENT VDA income cannot be reduced by the unexhausted basic exemption limit (under either default or optional tax regimes).
Chapter VIII Deductions NO DEDUCTION No deduction under Chapter VIII (Section 122 to 154 / 80C to 80U) is allowable from income from transfer of VDAs.

9 Section 92(2)(m): Taxation of Gifted VDAs under IFOS

VDA as ‘Property’ under Deemed Gift Provisions Sec 92(2)(m) • Sec 92(5)(f) • Legacy Sec 56(2)(x)

To prevent tax avoidance through off-market token transfers, Section 92(5)(f) explicitly incorporates Virtual Digital Assets within the statutory definition of “property”:

1. Transfer Without Consideration

Where any person receives any VDA without consideration from any person, and the aggregate Fair Market Value (FMV) of such VDAs exceeds ₹50,000 during the tax year, the entire aggregate FMV is chargeable to tax in the hands of the recipient under the head “Income from Other Sources” (taxed at regular slab rates).

2. Inadequate Consideration

Where any VDA is received for a consideration that is less than the aggregate FMV by an amount exceeding ₹50,000, the excess of aggregate FMV over such consideration is taxable in the hands of the recipient under IFOS.

Statutory Exemptions Applicable to VDA Gifts:
The standard statutory exemptions under Section 92(2)(m) apply to VDA receipts as well. No tax is leviable if the VDA is received:
  • (a) From any relative (spouse, brother/sister, lineal ascendant/descendant);
  • (b) On the occasion of the marriage of the individual;
  • (c) Under a will or by way of inheritance; or
  • (d) From any registered charitable trust, medical or educational institution.

10 Section 393(1) Table Sl. 8(vi): 1% TDS on Transfer of VDAs

Comprehensive Withholding Code on Virtual Digital Assets Sec 393(1) Table Sl. 8(vi) • Legacy Sec 194S

To establish an unalterable audit trail across crypto transactions, Section 393(1) [Table: Sl. No. 8(vi)] mandates deduction of tax at source at the point of transfer:

Rate Flat 1% Withholding

Tax is deductible @ 1% of the gross sum paid or credited to any resident transferor by way of consideration for transfer of a VDA.

Timing Credit or Payment

Deduction must be made at the time of credit of such sum to the account of the resident or at the time of payment by any mode, whichever is earlier.

In-Kind VDA-for-VDA Exchanges

Where consideration is wholly or partly in kind or in exchange of another VDA, the payer must ensure tax has been paid before releasing the consideration.

CBDT Guidelines: Circular No. 13/2022 & 14/2022
1. Transactions on an Exchange: When a transfer takes place on or through a registered Crypto Exchange, the primary duty to deduct tax rests with the Exchange crediting or making payment to the seller. If payment is made through a broker, the broker must deduct tax.
2. VDA-to-VDA Exchange (Cryptocurrency Swap): In a crypto-for-crypto swap on an Exchange, both parties are transferors. The Exchange can deduct 1% tax in kind from both legs, convert the withheld crypto to INR on the Exchange at market rates at day-end, and remit to the Government. No further TDS is triggered on such conversion.
3. Precedence Over Other TDS Provisions: Where tax is deductible under Section 393(1) Table Sl. 8(vi), NO tax is deductible under Section 393(1) Table Sl. 8(ii) [goods purchase / legacy 194Q] or Table Sl. 8(v) [e-commerce / legacy 194-O].
4. Payment Gateway Exemption: In transactions where buyers pay through payment gateways, the payment gateway is not required to deduct tax if the Exchange has already deducted tax.

11 Sections 509 & 446: Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF)

Reporting Obligations & Penal Enforcement Sections 509 • 446

Under international commitments to the OECD Crypto-asset Reporting Framework (CARF), India mandates comprehensive third-party reporting to eliminate black-market offshore crypto flows:

Section 509: Obligation to Furnish Information

Every reporting entity — including crypto exchanges, custodial wallet providers, token issuers, and peer-to-peer brokerages — must furnish an annual statement of financial transactions (SFT) detailing all crypto transactions, wallet IDs, transaction values, and user identities.

Section 446: Stringent Penal Consequences

• Delay in Filing: Penalty of ₹500 per day of default under Section 446(1).
• Inaccurate Information: Where an entity deliberately furnishes inaccurate crypto reporting data or fails to rectify errors, a flat penalty of ₹50,000 is leviable under Section 446(2).

13 Test Your Knowledge (TYK) • Practical Examination Bank

TYK Question 1: Distinctions Between E-Commerce and Traditional Commerce ICAI TYK 1 • Conceptual

Problem Statement: Explain the core reasons for the difference between e-commerce transactions and traditional business transactions that give rise to typical international taxation challenges.

Statutory Analysis & Solution
The transition from physical commerce to digital networks introduces five fundamental structural shifts:
1. Disembodied and Dematerialized Nature: In traditional business, commercial transactions require tangible goods, physical warehouses, retail showrooms, and face-to-face negotiation. E-commerce operates through dematerialized digital bits, automated cloud servers, and electronic smart contracts without physical manifestation.
2. Bypassing Physical Borders: While traditional supply chains are monitored at international customs ports, digital services, data streams, and software downloads traverse internet backbones instantaneously, escaping conventional border checks.
3. Complete Mobility of Functions: Digital enterprises can host databases in Jurisdiction A, execute algorithmic matching in Jurisdiction B, receive payments in Jurisdiction C, and service customers in Jurisdiction D with zero fixed geographic nexus.
4. Unprecedented Scale Without Mass: Digital platforms achieve multi-billion dollar commercial scale in source countries without employing local personnel or incorporating subsidiary entities (‘scale without mass’).
5. User Participation and Value Creation: In digital businesses (social networks, search engines), users contribute valuable personal data, content, and network effects that drive enterprise monetization, which traditional tax allocation rules fail to recognize.
TYK Question 2: Erosion of Traditional International Taxation Concepts ICAI TYK 2 • International Jurisprudence

Problem Statement: E-commerce transactions have replaced concepts generally associated with international transactions traditionally. Discuss the typical taxation issues arising from this structural transition.

Statutory Analysis & Solution
The growth of e-commerce has fractured the foundational concepts of source-based international taxation:
1. Difficulty in Characterizing Nature of Payments: Traditional taxation depends on compartmentalizing income into business profits (Article 7), royalties (Article 12), or technical service fees (Article 12A). In digital transactions — such as payments for software-as-a-service (SaaS), digital subscriptions, cloud infrastructure (IaaS), and online advertising — it is extraordinarily difficult to ascertain whether payment is for the copyright/process (royalty) or for automated commercial services (business profit).
2. Breakdown of the Physical Nexus Rule (PE): International tax treaties allocate taxing rights to source countries only if a foreign enterprise maintains a physical Permanent Establishment (PE). E-commerce enterprises generate massive commercial profits in source countries without maintaining any physical storefront, office, or personnel, leaving the source country with zero taxing jurisdiction under legacy treaties.
3. Difficulty in Locating Transactions and Identifying Taxpayers: Encrypted peer-to-peer networks, proxy IP routing, offshore server mirror sites, and anonymous digital wallets make it nearly impossible for tax administrations to identify the real beneficial owner, locate where the transaction was substantively executed, and quantify the value created in the domestic economy.

TYK Question 4: Mr. Raj • Business Loss Set-Off Restrictions & VDA Tax ICAI TYK 4 • Section 194(1)

Problem Statement: Compute the net income-tax payable (after TDS, if any) by Mr. Raj, aged 32 years, who has the following income for the tax year 2026-27:

  • (i) Business loss: (₹3,18,000)
  • (ii) Interest on fixed deposits with HDFC Bank: ₹18,000

• Business loss of the current year (₹3,18,000) can be set off against income from other sources (FD interest of ₹18,000).
• Remaining unabsorbed business loss = ₹3,00,000 (₹3,18,000 − ₹18,000).

2. Strict Ban on Set-Off Against VDA Income:
Under Section 194(1) Table Sl. No. 4, business loss CANNOT be set off against income from transfer of VDA.
Therefore, the unabsorbed business loss of ₹3,00,000 cannot touch the VDA income and must be carried forward to subsequent assessment years under Section 112 [legacy Section 72].
