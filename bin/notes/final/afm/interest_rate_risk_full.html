1. Core Concepts & Theoretical Architecture

12.1 Introduction & Macro-Economic Determinants of Interest Rates

Interest rate is both the cost of borrowing capital and the compensation paid to the lender for parting with liquidity, bearing credit risk, and enduring inflation erosion. Firms with low operating profit margins and heavy capital debt gearing are exceptionally vulnerable to interest rate movements.

The primary macro-economic drivers governing interest rate fluctuations include:

Factor Economic Dynamic Impact on Interest Rates
Demand and Supply of Funds During economic expansions, corporate demand for capex loans and working capital outstrips available savings supply. Interest rates rise to clear the market. Conversely, during recessions or low liquidity demand, rates drop.
Inflation Expectations Lenders demand a real return over expected inflation to preserve purchasing power: $(1 + \text{Nominal Rate}) = (1 + \text{Real Rate})(1 + \text{Expected Inflation})$. Higher expected inflation shifts yield curves upward across all maturities (Fisher Effect).
Fiscal Policy & Sovereign Borrowing The Government is typically the single largest borrower in domestic debt markets. High fiscal deficits increase sovereign bond issuance. Heavy government borrowing absorbs loanable funds, "crowds out" private borrowers, and pushes market yields up.
Monetary Policy & Central Bank Actions Central banks (e.g., RBI, US Federal Reserve) modulate policy rates (Repo Rate, Fed Funds Rate) and statutory reserve ratios (CRR/SLR) to manage price stability. Policy rate hikes raise commercial bank funding costs, transmitting higher lending and deposit rates through the banking system.

12.2 Benchmark Rates & The Transition from LIBOR to Alternative Reference Rates (ARRs)

A benchmark interest rate is an objective, publicly observable reference interest rate that forms the base pricing mechanism for financial contracts, corporate loans, floating rate notes, and derivatives (FRAs, Swaps, Caps, Floors).

The Historical Role and Vulnerability of LIBOR

Historically, the London Interbank Offered Rate (LIBOR) was the preeminent global benchmark across five currencies (USD, GBP, EUR, JPY, CHF). However, LIBOR suffered structural deficiencies: it relied heavily on subjective bank panel estimates ("expert judgment") rather than observable market trades, making it susceptible to manipulation during the global financial crisis.

Transition to Alternative Reference Rates (ARRs)

Global regulatory bodies mandated the transition to robust, transaction-backed, near-risk-free overnight rates:

Currency / Region Alternative Reference Rate (ARR) Governing Regulator Transaction Nature
USA (USD) SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate) Federal Reserve Bank of New York Secured (backed by US Treasury repo transactions)
UK (GBP) SONIA (Sterling Overnight Index Average) Bank of England Unsecured wholesale overnight deposit market
Eurozone (EUR) €STR (Euro Short-Term Rate) European Central Bank (ECB) Unsecured wholesale deposit market
Japan (JPY) TONAR (Tokyo Overnight Average Rate) Bank of Japan Unsecured call money market
Switzerland (CHF) SARON (Swiss Average Rate Overnight) SIX Swiss Exchange Secured repo market

Indian Benchmark Rates

  • MIBOR (Mumbai Interbank Offered Rate): Computed daily by FBIL (Financial Benchmarks India Pvt Ltd) based on trades executed on the NDS-Call platform. Used widely in Overnight Index Swaps (OIS).
  • MIFOR (Modified MIBOR): Blend of USD SOFR/LIBOR and forward foreign exchange rates, widely utilized by Indian corporate borrowers to hedge foreign currency borrowings.
  • MCLR (Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate): Internal benchmark utilized by Indian banks to price domestic floating rate retail and commercial loans.

12.3 Taxonomy of Interest Rate Risks

Interest rate risk arises from the exposure of a firm's or financial institution's financial condition to adverse movements in interest rates. The key classifications include:

Risk Type Core Definition Illustration & Corporate Exposure
Gap / Mismatch Exposure Arises when the interest rate reset timing or maturity of rate-sensitive assets (RSA) differs from rate-sensitive liabilities (RSL). A bank funding long-term 5-year fixed loans with 3-month floating deposits faces margin contraction when interest rates rise.
Basis Risk Arises when assets and liabilities are both floating rate, but are benchmarked to different market reference indexes. Funding an asset earning 1-year Treasury Bill yield using a liability priced on 1-month SOFR. If the spread between T-Bill and SOFR widens, profitability deteriorates.
Embedded Option Risk Arises when financial contracts contain unilateral early redemption or prepayment options. Borrowers refinance fixed mortgages when market rates fall (leaving lender with cash to reinvest at lower yields); depositors withdraw term deposits when rates spike.
Yield Curve Risk Arises when non-parallel shifts, twists, or changes in the slope (steepening or flattening) of the yield curve occur. Hedging long-term assets with short-term liabilities assuming parallel shifts leaves the institution exposed if short rates rise while long rates fall.
Price Risk The change in the market capital value of fixed-income instruments resulting from interest rate changes: $\Delta P \approx -D^* \times P \times \Delta y$. When bond yields increase, bond market prices fall. Longer maturity and lower coupon instruments exhibit maximum price volatility.
Reinvestment Risk The uncertainty of the rate at which intermediate coupon cash inflows and principal amortizations can be reinvested. If interest rates drop, incoming cash flows must be reinvested at lower prevailing yields, reducing terminal wealth.

12.4 Measuring Interest Rate Risk: Duration, Modified Duration & Convexity

Quantitative measurement of price sensitivity is fundamental to structuring bond portfolio immunization and interest rate hedges:

Key Formulas for Price Sensitivity:
  • Macaulay Duration ($D$): Weighted average maturity of cash flows: $$D = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^n \frac{t \times C_t}{(1+y)^t}}{\text{Market Price } (P_0)}$$
  • Modified Duration ($D^*$): Measures percentage change in bond price for a 100 bps shift in yield: $$D^* = \frac{D}{1 + \frac{y}{m}}$$
  • Price Change Approximation: $$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D^* \times \Delta y$$
  • Convexity Adjustment ($C$): Refines the duration estimate for large yield shifts: $$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D^* \times \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} C \times (\Delta y)^2$$

12.5 Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs) – Mechanics & Settlement

A Forward Rate Agreement (FRA) is an over-the-counter (OTC) forward contract where two parties agree on an interest rate to be paid on a notional principal over a specified future period starting at a future date.

Notation: "$m \times n$" FRA

A "$6 \times 9$" FRA represents:

  • The contract begins 6 months from today (Effective / Settlement Date).
  • The loan/deposit duration is 3 months ($9 - 6 = 3$ months).
  • The total period from today is 9 months (Maturity Date).

ICAI Master FRA Cash Settlement Formula

Settlement occurs at the beginning of the borrow/invest period (the fixing date) in discounted present value terms because interest is ordinarily paid in arrears at maturity:

$$\mathbf{\text{Settlement Amount}} = \frac{N \times (RR - FR) \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)}{1 + RR \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)}$$

Where:

  • $N$ = Notional Principal Amount
  • $RR$ = Reference Market Rate on the fixing date (e.g., MIBOR, SOFR)
  • $FR$ = Agreed Forward Contract Rate
  • $dtm$ = Number of days in the FRA period (e.g., 90 days or 3 months)
  • $DY$ = Day count convention (360 or 365 days)
Settlement Rules:
  • If $RR > FR$: Market rate is higher than agreed forward rate. The seller pays the buyer the positive settlement amount.
  • If $RR < FR$: Market rate is lower than agreed forward rate. The buyer pays the seller the settlement amount.

12.6 Interest Rate Options: Caps, Floors, and Collars

Interest rate options give the holder the right, but not the obligation, to pay or receive a specified benchmark rate against a floating index:

Instrument Option Analogy Primary User & Hedging Purpose Payout Formula on Reset Date
Interest Rate Cap Series of European call options ("caplets") on the benchmark interest rate. Floating-rate Borrower: Sets an upper ceiling (strike rate $R_k$) on borrowing costs. If benchmark rises above strike, bank pays the excess interest. $$\text{Payout} = N \times \max(0, R_{\text{market}} - R_k) \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)$$
Interest Rate Floor Series of European put options ("floorlets") on the benchmark interest rate. Floating-rate Investor / Lender: Sets a minimum floor yield ($R_k$) on deposits. If benchmark falls below strike, seller compensates the shortfall. $$\text{Payout} = N \times \max(0, R_k - R_{\text{market}}) \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)$$
Interest Rate Collar Simultaneous purchase of an out-of-the-money Cap and sale of an out-of-the-money Floor. Cost Reduction: The premium received from writing the Floor finances the purchase of the Cap. A "Zero-Cost Collar" matches the two option premiums. Effective interest rate is bounded within a fixed band: between the Floor strike and Cap strike.

12.7 Interest Rate Swaps (IRS) & Overnight Index Swaps (OIS)

An Interest Rate Swap is a contractual agreement between two counterparties to exchange streams of interest cash flows based on a specified notional principal over an agreed tenor.

Key Swap Structures

  • Plain Vanilla Fixed-for-Floating Swap: Counterparty A pays a fixed coupon rate ("Swap Rate") and receives a floating index (e.g., 6-month SOFR). Counterparty B pays floating and receives fixed. Only the net interest differential is exchanged on settlement dates.
  • Basis Swap: Both legs are floating rate, but tied to different benchmark indexes (e.g., 1-month SOFR vs 3-month SOFR) or different tenors.
  • Overnight Index Swap (OIS): A swap where the floating leg is tied to the geometric compounding of daily overnight rates (such as daily MIBOR or SOFR) over the calculation period, compared against a predetermined fixed rate.
  • Currency Swap: Counterparties exchange both principal amounts at inception (at current spot rate), exchange interest payments in differing currencies throughout the tenor, and re-exchange principal at maturity at the initial spot rate. Driven by comparative borrowing advantages across national debt markets.

12.8 Swaptions: Structures, Pricing & Strategic Applications

A Swaption is an option to enter into an Interest Rate Swap at a specified future date at a predetermined swap rate (strike):

Type of Swaption Holder's Right When to Exercise (Market Condition) Strategic Use
Payer Swaption Right to pay fixed rate and receive floating rate in the underlying swap. Exercised when prevailing market swap rates rise above the strike rate. Protects prospective floating-rate borrowers against rising fixed borrowing rates on future loan drawdowns.
Receiver Swaption Right to receive fixed rate and pay floating rate in the underlying swap. Exercised when prevailing market swap rates fall below the strike rate. Guarantees prospective lenders or bondholders a minimum fixed return when market yields decline.

Theoretical Question 1: Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs)

Write a short note on Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs).

ICAI Study Material Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown

Short Note on Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs)

A Forward Rate Agreement (FRA) is an over-the-counter (OTC) financial contract between two parties to lock in an interest rate to be paid or received on a notional principal amount over a future designated period.

  1. Structural Purpose: FRAs enable corporations, banks, and institutional investors to hedge against future adverse movements in interest rates (e.g., a borrower hedging against rising borrowing rates or an investor hedging against falling deposit yields) without exchanging underlying principal amounts.
  2. Terminology and Notation ("$m \times n$"):
    • In an "$m \times n$" FRA, the contract commences at the end of month $m$ from contract inception (the settlement or fixing date) and matures at the end of month $n$.
    • The duration of the underlying deposit or borrowing period is $(n - m)$ months. For example, a $3 \times 6$ FRA covers a 3-month loan commencing in 3 months' time.
  3. Determination of Settlement Cash Flow:

    At the contract settlement date, the agreed Forward Rate ($FR$) is compared against the actual market Reference Rate ($RR$) (e.g., MIBOR or SOFR). Because interest in money markets is traditionally paid in arrears at the conclusion of the deposit period, the settlement cash flow is discounted back to present value terms at the beginning of the period using the market Reference Rate:

    $$\text{Settlement Amount} = \frac{\text{Notional Principal } (N) \times (RR - FR) \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)}{1 + RR \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)}$$

    Where $dtm$ is the actual number of days in the FRA period and $DY$ is the day count convention (360 or 365 days).

  4. Payoff Symmetry:
    • If $RR > FR$: The seller pays the buyer the settlement amount (compensating the buyer for higher market borrowing costs).
    • If $RR < FR$: The buyer pays the seller the settlement amount.
  5. Advantages: Tailored notional amounts and maturity dates; zero initial cash outlay; effective removal of interest rate uncertainty.

Theoretical Question 2: Swaptions and Their Strategic Uses

What do you know about swaptions and their uses?

ICAI Study Material Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown

Nature and Concept of Swaptions

A Swaption (Swap Option) is an option contract that grants the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to enter into an Interest Rate Swap (IRS) with the seller on a predetermined future date at a specified swap rate (strike rate):

Type of Swaption Buyer's Right Optimal Exercise Scenario Underlying Hedging Objective
Payer Swaption Right to pay fixed and receive floating in the underlying swap. Exercised when prevailing market swap rates rise above the strike rate ($R_{\text{market}} > R_{\text{strike}}$). Used by future borrowers to establish a ceiling on fixed-rate borrowing costs while retaining the flexibility to borrow at lower market rates if yields drop.
Receiver Swaption Right to receive fixed and pay floating in the underlying swap. Exercised when prevailing market swap rates fall below the strike rate ($R_{\text{market}} < R_{\text{strike}}$). Used by institutional investors/lenders to establish a floor on future investment yields.

Exercise Styles

  • European Swaption: Exercisable only on the exact expiration date.
  • American Swaption: Exercisable on any business day up to and including the expiration date.
  • Bermudan Swaption: Exercisable on pre-specified discrete dates (e.g., each coupon reset date).

Strategic Corporate Uses

  1. Hedging Anticipated Debt Issues: A company planning a major bond issuance in 6 months can buy a Payer Swaption. If market yields surge, it exercises the swaption to lock in the lower strike rate; if yields decline, it lets the swaption expire and issues bonds at the prevailing low yields.
  2. Embedding into Bond Issues: Issuers can embed swaptions into callable or puttable bonds, significantly lowering initial coupon costs by monetizing the embedded option.
  3. Restructuring Existing Swap Positions: Provides dynamic flexibility to terminate or restructure existing interest rate swap obligations.

Practical Question 1: M/s. Parker & Co. - FRA Settlement Calculation

M/s. Parker & Co. is contemplating to borrow an amount of ₹ 60 crores for a period of 3 months in the coming 6 month's time from now. The current rate of interest is 9% p.a., but it may go up in 6 month’s time. The company wants to hedge itself against the likely increase in interest rate.

The Company's Bankers quoted an FRA (Forward Rate Agreement) at 9.30% p.a.

What will be the Final settlement amount, if the actual rate of interest after 6 months happens to be (i) 9.60% p.a. and (ii) 8.80% p.a.?

ICAI Study Material Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown

Master Settlement Formula for Forward Rate Agreements

$$\text{Settlement Amount} = \frac{N \times (RR - FR) \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)}{1 + RR \times \left(\frac{dtm}{DY}\right)}$$

Where:

  • $N = ₹ 60 \text{ Crores} = ₹ 60,00,00,000$
  • $FR = \text{Agreed Forward Rate} = 9.30\% = 0.093$
  • $dtm / DY = \text{FRA duration} = 3 \text{ months} / 12 \text{ months} = 0.25$

Case (i): Actual Rate of Interest after 6 months is 9.60% p.a. ($RR = 0.096$)

Since the actual reference rate ($9.60\%$) exceeds the agreed forward rate ($9.30\%$), the banker will pay Parker & Co.:

$$\text{Numerator} = ₹ 60,00,00,000 \times (0.096 - 0.093) \times \frac{3}{12} = ₹ 60,00,00,000 \times 0.003 \times 0.25 = ₹ 4,50,000$$

$$\text{Denominator} = 1 + \left[0.096 \times \frac{3}{12}\right] = 1 + 0.024 = 1.024$$

$$\text{Settlement Amount} = \frac{₹ 4,50,000}{1.024} = \mathbf{₹ 4,39,453.13 \approx ₹ 4,39,453}$$

Conclusion (i): The banker will pay Parker & Co. a sum of ₹ 4,39,453.

Case (ii): Actual Rate of Interest after 6 months is 8.80% p.a. ($RR = 0.088$)

Since the actual reference rate ($8.80\%$) is lower than the agreed forward rate ($9.30\%$), Parker & Co. will pay the banker:

$$\text{Numerator} = ₹ 60,00,00,000 \times (0.088 - 0.093) \times \frac{3}{12} = ₹ 60,00,00,000 \times (-0.005) \times 0.25 = -₹ 7,50,000$$

$$\text{Denominator} = 1 + \left[0.088 \times \frac{3}{12}\right] = 1 + 0.022 = 1.022$$

$$\text{Settlement Amount} = \frac{-₹ 7,50,000}{1.022} = \mathbf{-₹ 7,33,855.19 \approx -₹ 7,33,855}$$

Conclusion (ii): Parker & Co. will pay the banker a sum of ₹ 7,33,855.

Final Settlement Summary:
• At 9.60% p.a.: Banker pays Parker & Co. ₹ 4,39,453.13.
• At 8.80% p.a.: Parker & Co. pays Banker ₹ 7,33,855.19.

Practical Question 2: TM Fincorp - 6x9 FRA Settlement & Basis Points

TM Fincorp has bought a $6 \times 9$ ₹ 100 crore Forward Rate Agreement (FRA) at 5.25%. On fixing date reference rate i.e. MIBOR turns out be as follows:

PeriodRate (%)
3 months5.50%
6 months5.70%
9 months5.85%

You are required to determine:

  1. Profit/Loss to TM Fincorp in terms of basis points.
  2. The settlement amount. (Assume 360 days in a year).
ICAI Study Material Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown

Part (a): Determination of Profit/Loss in Basis Points

A $6 \times 9$ FRA is a forward contract on an underlying 3-month interest rate starting 6 months from today ($9 - 6 = 3$ months).

Therefore, on the fixing date (6 months from inception), the relevant benchmark reference rate is the 3-month MIBOR, which is 5.50%.

$$\text{Agreed Forward Rate } (FR) = 5.25\%$$

$$\text{Reference Rate on Fixing Date } (RR) = 5.50\%$$

$$\text{Gain in Basis Points} = 5.50\% - 5.25\% = 0.25\% = 0.25 \times 100 = \mathbf{25 \text{ Basis Points (bps)}}$$

Since TM Fincorp bought the FRA and the reference rate increased, TM Fincorp makes a profit of 25 basis points.

Part (b): Computation of Settlement Amount (360-Day Basis)

$$\text{Settlement Amount} = \frac{N \times (RR - FR) \times \left(\frac{dtm}{360}\right)}{1 + RR \times \left(\frac{dtm}{360}\right)}$$

Where:

  • $N = ₹ 100 \text{ Crores} = ₹ 1,00,00,00,000$
  • $RR = 5.50\% = 0.055$
  • $FR = 5.25\% = 0.0525$
  • $dtm = 3 \text{ months} = 90 \text{ days}$

Substituting into the formula:

$$\text{Numerator} = ₹ 1,00,00,00,000 \times (0.055 - 0.0525) \times \left(\frac{90}{360}\right)$$

$$\text{Numerator} = ₹ 1,00,00,00,000 \times 0.0025 \times 0.25 = ₹ 6,25,000$$

$$\text{Denominator} = 1 + \left[0.055 \times \frac{90}{360}\right] = 1 + [0.055 \times 0.25] = 1 + 0.01375 = 1.01375$$

$$\mathbf{\text{Settlement Amount}} = \frac{₹ 6,25,000}{1.01375} = \mathbf{₹ 6,16,522.81 \approx ₹ 6,16,523}$$

Final Results:
(a) Profit in basis points: 25 basis points.
(b) Settlement Amount received by TM Fincorp: ₹ 6,16,523.

Step 1: Computation of Amortized Semi-Annual Cap Premium

The total lump sum premium payable is 1.00% of £ 15,000,000 = £ 150,000.

There are 4 semi-annual reset periods over the 24-month loan ($t = 4$).

The semi-annual fixed interest discount rate is $i = 7.00\% / 2 = 3.50\% = 0.035$.

Practical Question 4: Generic Swap - Semi-Annual Cash Flow & Net Settlement

Suppose a dealer quotes ‘All-in-cost’ for a generic swap at 8% against six month SOFR flat. If the notional principal amount of swap is US$ 5,00,000:

  1. Calculate semi-annual fixed payment.
  2. Find the first floating rate payment for (i) above if the six month period from the effective date of swap to the settlement date comprises 181 days and that the corresponding SOFR was 6% on the effective date of swap.
  3. In (ii) above, if the settlement is on ‘Net’ basis, how much the fixed rate payer would pay to the floating rate payer?

Generic swap is based on 30/360 days basis.

ICAI Study Material Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown

Part (i): Computation of Semi-Annual Fixed Payment

Generic swaps apply the 30/360 day count convention for the fixed leg:

$$\text{Fixed Payment} = N \times \text{All-in-Cost (AIC)} \times \left(\frac{180}{360}\right)$$

$$\text{Fixed Payment} = \$500,000 \times 0.08 \times 0.5 = \$500,000 \times 0.04 = \mathbf{US\$ 20,000}$$

Part (ii): Computation of First Floating Rate Payment

For the floating leg, payment is determined using actual days (181 days) over 360 days:

$$\text{Floating Rate Payment} = N \times \text{SOFR} \times \left(\frac{dt}{360}\right) = \$500,000 \times 0.06 \times \left(\frac{181}{360}\right)$$

$$\frac{181}{360} = 0.502778 \quad (\text{or rounded to } 0.503)$$

$$\text{Floating Payment} = \$500,000 \times 0.06 \times 0.502778 = \mathbf{US\$ 15,083.33 \approx US\$ 15,083}$$

(Using 0.503 factor: $\$500,000 \times 0.06 \times 0.503 = \mathbf{US\$ 15,090}$).

Part (iii): Net Settlement Payment by Fixed Rate Payer

On a net settlement basis, the fixed rate payer pays the difference between the fixed obligation and the floating receipt:

$$\text{Net Payment} = \text{Fixed Payment} - \text{Floating Payment}$$

$$\text{Net Payment} = \$20,000 - \$15,083 = \mathbf{US\$ 4,917}$$

(Or using $\$15,090$: $\$20,000 - \$15,090 = \mathbf{US\$ 4,910}$).

Final Answers:
• (i) Semi-annual Fixed Payment = US$ 20,000.
• (ii) Floating Rate Payment = US$ 15,083 (or US$ 15,090).
• (iii) Net Amount paid by Fixed Payer = US$ 4,917 (or US$ 4,910).
