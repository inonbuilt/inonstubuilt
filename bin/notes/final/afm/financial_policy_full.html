Advanced Role of CFO in Various Matters Including Value Creation

ICAI Para 1.0 • Evolution from traditional controller to strategic catalyst in sustainable wealth generation
📖 Content in ICAI Book Study Material Text

Traditionally, the main role of CFO was concentrated to wealth maximisation for shareholders by taking care of financial health of an organization and overseeing and implementing adequate financial controls.

However, in recent time because of globalization, growth in information and communications, pandemic situation etc. their range of responsibilities has been drastically expanded, driven by complexity and changing expectations.

Now a days in addition to fulfilling traditional role relating to governance, compliances and controls, and business ethics as a part of the leadership of role CFOs are also expected to contribute their support in strategic and operational decision making.

In post-pandemic time their role has been advanced in the following areas in addition to traditional role:

  • a. Risk Management: Now a days the CFOs are expected to look after the overall functioning of the framework of Risk Management system of an organisation.
  • b. Supply Chain: Post pandemic supply chain management system has been posing the challenge for the company to maintain the sustainable growth. Since CFOs are care takers of finance of the company, considering the financial viability of the Supply Chain Management their role has now become more critical.
  • c. Mergers, acquisitions, and Corporate Restructuring: Since in recent period to maintain the growth and capture the market share there has been a spate of Mergers and Acquisitions and hence the role of CFOs has become more crucial because these are strategic decision and any error in them can lead to collapse of the whole business.
  • d. Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) Financing: With the evolving of the concept of ESG their role has been shifted from traditional financing to sustainability financing.

Conclusion: Thus, from above discussion it can be concluded that in today’s time CFOs are taking a leadership role in Value Creation for the organisation and that too on sustainable basis for a longer period.

💡 Clear Conceptual Explanation Analysis

The Evolution of the Modern CFO: Historically, a CFO functioned as a corporate scorekeeper focused primarily on historical accounting records, tax compliance, and treasury cash management. Today, macroeconomic disruptions, geopolitical conflicts, and technological acceleration have shifted the CFO into a strategic co-pilot alongside the CEO.

Breakdown of the 4 Key Modern Frontiers:

  • Enterprise Risk Management (ERM): Modern CFOs do not simply buy insurance; they architect proactive risk management frameworks, calibrate Value at Risk (VaR), stress-test balance sheet liquidity, and maintain emergency capital buffers.
  • Supply Chain Financial Viability: Global chokeholds revealed that operational supply interruptions quickly translate into acute liquidity crunches. CFOs evaluate supplier financial solvency, dynamic discounting, and inventory cost-benefit trade-offs (Just-in-Time vs. Just-in-Case).
  • M&A and Corporate Restructuring: Capital allocation through mergers carries massive strategic risk. CFOs oversee valuation modeling, debt-equity structuring, synergy realization, and post-merger integration to ensure acquisitions create net shareholder value rather than destroy it.
  • ESG & Sustainability Financing: Global institutional investors require strict environmental and social accountability. CFOs lead the issuance of Green Bonds, Sustainability-Linked Loans (SLLs), and BRSR disclosures under SEBI mandates.

Strategic Financial Decision-Making Framework

ICAI Para 2.0 • Capital investment as springboard for wealth creation & the four strategic decision pillars
📖 Content in ICAI Book Study Material Text

Capital investment is the springboard for wealth creation. In a world of economic uncertainty, the investors want to maximize their wealth by selecting optimum investment and financial opportunities that will give them maximum expected returns at minimum risk.

Since management is ultimately responsible to the investors, the objective of corporate financial management should implement investment and financing decisions which should satisfy the shareholders by placing them all in an equal, optimum financial position. The satisfaction of the interests of the shareholders should be perceived as a means to an end, namely maximization of shareholders’ wealth.

Since capital is the limiting factor, the problem that the management will face is the strategic allocation of limited funds between alternative uses in such a manner, that the companies have the ability to sustain or increase investor returns through a continual search for investment opportunities that generate funds for their business and are more favourable for the investors.

Therefore, all businesses need to have the following three fundamental essential elements:

  • A clear and realistic strategy;
  • The financial resources, controls and systems to see it through; and
  • The right management team and processes to make it happen.
$$\mathbf{Strategy + Finance + Management = Fundamentals\ of\ Business}$$

Definition of Strategy: Strategy may be defined as the long-term direction and scope of an organization to achieve competitive advantage through the configuration of resources within a changing environment for the fulfilment of stakeholder’s aspirations and expectations.

2.1 Meaning of Strategic Financial Management: We define strategic financial management as being the application of financial management techniques to strategic decisions in order to help achieve the decision-maker's objectives.

Although linked with accounting, the focus of strategic financial management is different. Strategic financial management combines the backward-looking, report-focused discipline of (financial) accounting with the more dynamic, forward-looking subject of financial management. It is basically about the identification of possible strategies capable of maximizing an organization's market value.

2.2 Functions of Strategic Financial Management: In this connection, it is necessary to distinguish between strategic, tactical and operational financial planning:

  • Strategy: Long-term course of action, decided by Senior Management.
  • Tactics: Intermediate plans, decided by Middle-Level Management.
  • Operations: Short-term day-to-day functions, looked after by Line Management.

Irrespective of time horizon, investment and financial decisions involve five functions (Robert Alan Hill):

  1. Continual search for best investment opportunities;
  2. Selection of the best profitable opportunities;
  3. Determination of optimal mix of funds for the opportunities;
  4. Establishment of systems for internal controls; and
  5. Analysis of results for future decision-making.

The Four Key Decisions:

  1. Financing Decisions: Deals with mode of financing or mix of equity capital and debt capital.
  2. Investment Decisions: Deals with profitable utilization of funds in long-term projects evaluated for return and risk.
  3. Dividend Decisions: Determines division of earnings between distribution to shareholders and reinvestment.
  4. Portfolio Decisions: Evaluates investments based on their contribution to aggregate corporate performance rather than isolated characteristics.
💡 Clear Conceptual Explanation Analysis

1. Why Capital Investment is the "Springboard": Wealth is created when an enterprise commits capital to productive assets that yield returns greater than the cost of capital ($$NPV > 0$$). Because capital is scarce, financial strategy acts as an allocator directing funds to maximum-yield uses.

2. Strategic Planning vs Tactical vs Operational:

Dimension Strategic Planning Tactical Planning Operational Planning
Time Horizon Long-Term (3 to 10+ years) Medium-Term (1 to 3 years) Short-Term (Day-to-day to 1 year)
Managerial Level Senior Management (Board, CEO, CFO) Middle Management (Department Heads) Line Management (Plant Supervisors)
Focus Scope, Direction, M&A, Capital Structure Department budgets, target execution Daily cash flows, receivables, billing

3. Portfolio Decisions: Unlike standalone project evaluation, portfolio decisions evaluate covariance and correlation between projects to minimize overall corporate volatility and bankruptcy risk.

Scoring Keywords:

Strategy at Different Hierarchy Levels

ICAI Para 3.0 • Corporate, SBU & Functional levels; Suitability, Feasibility & Acceptability
📖 Content in ICAI Book Study Material Text

Strategies at different levels are the outcomes of different planning needs. There are three levels of Strategy – Corporate level; Business unit level; and Functional or departmental level.

3.1 Corporate Level Strategy: Corporate level strategy fundamentally is concerned with selection of businesses in which a company should compete and with the development and coordination of that portfolio of businesses.

Corporate level strategy should be able to answer three basic questions:

Criteria Core Focus & Definition
Suitability Whether the strategy would work for the accomplishment of common objective of the company.
Feasibility Determines the kind and number of resources required to formulate and implement the strategy.
Acceptability It is concerned with the stakeholders’ satisfaction and can be financial and non-financial.

3.2 Business Unit Level Strategy: Strategic Business Unit (SBU) may be any profit centre that can be planned independently from the other business units of a corporation. At the business unit level, strategic issues are about practical coordination of operating units and developing and sustaining competitive advantage for products and services.

3.3 Functional Level Strategy: The functional level is the level of operating divisions and departments (R&D, operations, marketing, finance, HR). Functional units provide input to higher level strategies and translate higher level strategies into discrete departmental action plans.

Supremacy of Finance: Among different functional activities, finance assumes highest importance during top-down and bottom-up planning. Corporate strategy deals with deployment of resources and financial strategy is concerned with mobilization and effective utilization of money, the most critical resource. Other resources can be easily mobilized if the firm has an adequate monetary base.

💡 Clear Conceptual Explanation Analysis

1. Hierarchy Breakdown:

  • Corporate Level ("Which businesses to own?"): Conglomerate-level decisions regarding entering new industries, capital allocation between divisions, or divestments (e.g., Tata Group expanding into electronics and aerospace).
  • Business Unit Level ("How to compete?"): Competitive positioning of a specific division (Cost Leadership, Differentiation, Focus).
  • Functional Level ("How to execute?"): Departmental roadmaps that realize SBU objectives.

2. Why Finance is Supreme: Operational plans across marketing, manufacturing, and HR are non-functional without capital. Finance sets the feasible resource boundary and provides the common denominator (cash flow, ROI, EVA) for evaluating multi-departmental success.

Financial Planning

ICAI Para 4.0 • The planning backbone: FR + FT + FG equation & corporate outcomes
📖 Content in ICAI Book Study Material Text

Financial planning is the backbone of business planning and corporate planning. It helps in defining the feasible area of operation for all types of activities and thereby defines the overall planning framework.

Financial planning is a systematic approach whereby the financial planner helps the customer to maximize his existing financial resources by utilizing financial tools to achieve his financial goals.

There are 3 major components of Financial planning:

  • Financial Resources (FR)
  • Financial Tools (FT)
  • Financial Goals (FG)
$$\mathbf{Financial\ Planning = FR + FT + FG}$$

Individual vs. Corporate Financial Planning: For an individual, it is the process of meeting life goals through proper money management. For a corporation, it establishes the big picture of current standing, future requirements, and action steps.

Outcomes of Financial Planning:

  1. Financial Objectives: Decided at the outset; consistent with corporate mission.
  2. Financial Decision-Making: Analyzing problems and determining courses of action.
  3. Financial Measures for Evaluation: Ratio analysis and cash flow statement analysis used to evaluate corporate performance against objectives.
💡 Clear Conceptual Explanation Analysis

Deconstructing FR + FT + FG:

  • Financial Resources (FR): Internal cash reserves, operating cash generation, and borrowing capacity.
  • Financial Tools (FT): Analytical instruments like DCF modeling, capital budgeting, working capital formulas, and risk hedging.
  • Financial Goals (FG): Quantified corporate targets (e.g., achieving 18% ROE, maintaining Debt/Equity below 1.0, generating ₹500 Cr free cash flow).
🎯 Exam Relevance & Examiner Focus 3–4 Marks

Exam Question: "What is financial planning? State its major components and primary outcomes."

Scoring Keywords:
Backbone of Planning FR + FT + FG Feasible Area of Operation Financial Objectives Evaluation Measures (Ratios & Cash Flow)

Interface of Financial Policy and Strategic Management

ICAI Para 5.0 • Core exam theory: 4 dimensions of strategic financial interface & reciprocal causation
📖 Content in ICAI Book Study Material Text

The interface of strategic management and financial policy will be clearly understood if we appreciate the fact that the starting point of an organization is money and the end point of that organization is also money. No organization can run an existing business and promote a new expansion project without a suitable internally mobilized financial base or both i.e. internally and externally mobilized financial base.

The interface manifests across four vital dimensions:

1. Sources of Finance and Capital Structure: The need for fund mobilization to support expansion is vital. Funds arise from ownership capital (equity, preference shares) and borrowed capital (debentures, public deposits, bank loans, trade credit). Public deposits for fixed periods have become a major source.

Policy makers decide capital structure indicating desired mix of equity and debt. Industry norms for debt-equity ratio include:

  • Public Sector Organizations Norm: $$1:1$$ ratio.
  • Private Sector Firms Norm: $$2:1$$ ratio.
  • Variations: In capital-intensive industries, proportion of debt is much higher; similar for priority sectors and underdeveloped regions.

2. Investment and Fund Allocation Decisions: Planners frame policies regulating investments in fixed assets and restraining current assets. Investment proposals fall into three groups:

  • Group 1: Addition of a new product by the firm.
  • Group 2: Increasing level of operation of existing product (capacity expansion in existing plant or setting up another plant).
  • Group 3: Cost reduction and efficient utilization of resources through a new approach and/or closer monitoring.

Project evaluation and project selection are the two most important jobs under fund allocation under resource constraints.

3. Dividend Policy Decisions: Deals with extent of earnings distributed as dividend vs retained for expansion. From long-term funding perspective, dividend is that part of earnings which cannot be profitably utilized by the company.

Options include: stable dividend per share (positive market reputation), constant percentage payout (matches firm ability), minimum regular dividend plus extra dividend, and cash vs. stock dividends (bonus shares).

4. Mutual Interdependence (Reciprocal Causation): Financial policy cannot be worked out in isolation. Corporate strategy is the cause and financial policy is the effect; AND sometimes financial policy is the cause and corporate strategy is the effect.

💡 Clear Conceptual Explanation Analysis

1. Why "Money is the Starting and End Point": Businesses begin with liquid capital, transform it into operational assets and inventories, generate sales, and ultimately recover liquid capital. If terminating cash flows exceed initial capital cost-adjusted, enterprise value is created.

2. Public Sector (1:1) vs Private Sector (2:1): Sovereign entities prioritize solvency and conservative risk profiles ($$1:1$$). Private corporations utilize trading on equity to magnify Return on Equity ($$2:1$$). Capital-intensive projects (power, highways) require leverage up to 3:1 or 4:1 due to extended payback periods.

3. Reciprocal Causation Explained:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Strategy (Cause)} &\longrightarrow \text{Finance (Effect)} \quad [\text{Aggressive expansion forces debt mobilization}] \\ \text{Finance (Cause)} &\longrightarrow \text{Strategy (Effect)} \quad [\text{Borrowing limits force conservative organic growth}] \end{aligned}$$

Mandatory Phrases for Top Marks:

Balancing Financial Goals vis-à-vis Sustainable Growth

ICAI Para 6.0 & 6.1 • Robert C. Higgins SGR model, PRAT decomposition, CIVICUS criteria & inflation dynamics
📖 Content in ICAI Book Study Material Text

The concept of sustainable growth forces managers to consider the financial consequences of sales increases and set sales growth goals consistent with operating and financial policies.

Stable Growth Strategies: Right distribution of resources must consider future stakeholders. In the fuel industry, resources are limited. The "Save Fuel Campaign" is a demarketing campaign preaching fuel conservation across generations. Incremental growth strategy, profit strategy, and pause strategy are other variants.

What makes an organisation financially sustainable? (CIVICUS Criteria):

  • Have more than one source of income;
  • Have more than one way of generating income;
  • Do strategic, action and financial planning regularly;
  • Have adequate financial systems;
  • Have a good public image;
  • Be clear about its values (value clarity); and
  • Have financial autonomy.

What makes an organisation sustainable overall? (CIVICUS Criteria):

  • Clear strategic direction;
  • Ability to scan environment/context to identify opportunities;
  • Attract, manage and retain competent staff;
  • Adequate administrative and financial infrastructure;
  • Demonstrate effectiveness and impact; and
  • Get community support and involvement.

6.1 Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR) — Robert C. Higgins Concept:

The sustainable growth rate (SGR) of a firm is the maximum rate of growth in sales that can be achieved, given the firm's profitability, asset utilization, desired dividend payout and debt (financial leverage) ratios.

SGR measures how much a firm can grow without borrowing more money beyond its target leverage.

$$\mathbf{SGR = ROE \times (1 - \text{Dividend Payout Ratio}) = ROE \times b}$$

3 Assumptions of Sustainable Growth Models:

  1. Maintain target capital structure without issuing new equity;
  2. Maintain target dividend payment ratio; and
  3. Increase sales as rapidly as market conditions allow.

Twin Cornerstones: Growth Strategy and Growth Capability. Paying inadequate attention to either guarantees failure.

Macro Concepts of Sustainability:

  • Very Weak Sustainability: Overall stock of capital assets should remain constant.
  • Weak Sustainability: Preservation of critical resources to ensure support over long horizon.
  • Strong Sustainability: Preservation of resources under primacy of ecosystem functioning.

Mature Firms with Actual Growth < SGR: Principal objective is finding productive uses for excess cash flows: returning money via higher dividends or buybacks, retiring debt, or acquiring fast-growing firms.

Impact of Inflation on SGR: Inflationary increases in assets must be financed as real growth. Inflation increases external financing required and increases historical debt-to-equity ratios. Thus, inflation lowers the firm's sustainable growth rate.

Mitsubishi Corporation (MC) Case Study: MC abolished traditional 3-year fixed targets in favor of a circa 2020 long-term growth vision as a sogo shosha. Key pillars: (1) Future pull approach, (2) Reshaping portfolio proactively, (3) Deliver returns while maintaining financial discipline.

💡 Clear Conceptual Explanation Analysis

The Higgins PRAT Model: Decomposing ROE via the Du-Pont identity yields four managerial levers for sustainable sales growth:

$$\mathbf{SGR = P \times A \times T \times b}$$ $$\text{Where: } P = \frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Sales}} \text{ (Margin)},\quad A = \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Assets}} \text{ (Turnover)},\quad T = \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Net Worth}} \text{ (Leverage)},\quad b = (1 - d) \text{ (Retention)}$$

The Danger of Growing Too Fast ("Overtrading"): When sales grow faster than SGR ($$g > \text{SGR}$$), working capital requirements surge. If the firm cannot issue equity and cannot take more debt due to covenants, liquid reserves deplete rapidly, causing operational paralysis and insolvency.

🎯 Exam Relevance & Examiner Focus 4–5 Marks

Key Questions: "Define SGR and state its assumptions", "CIVICUS financial sustainability criteria", or "Impact of inflation on SGR."

Scoring Keywords:
Maximum growth without new equity ROE $\times$ (1 - Dividend Payout) PRAT Model Levers CIVICUS 7 Financial Points Inflation lowers SGR

External Funding Requirement (EFR) & All ICAI Book Illustrations

ICAI Para 6.2 • Analytical Approach vs Formula Approach; Master Illustration of M/s. Sundry Ltd.
📖 Content in ICAI Book Study Material Text

When sales increase, current and non-current assets must grow. To maintain financial covenants (Debt-Equity ratio or Current ratio), management determines the External Funding Requirement (EFR).

Two approaches can be used:

6.2.1 Analytical Approach: Analyzing balance sheet items in relation to sales changes under prescribed conditions.

6.2.2 Formula based Approach:

$$\mathbf{EFR = \left(\frac{TA - P}{S}\right) \times \Delta S - [N \times S_1 \times (1 - d)]}$$

Where: $$\Delta S = S_1 - S$$, $$TA$$ = Total Assets, $$P$$ = Spontaneous Payables & Provisions, $$N$$ = Profit Margin %, $$d$$ = Dividend Payout Ratio.

🧮 Solved Illustrations (ICAI Master Problem: M/s Sundry Ltd.) Full Working

The Balance Sheet of M/s. Sundry Ltd. as on 31-03-2023:

Liabilities ₹ in lakhs Assets ₹ in lakhs
Share Capital 3,000 Fixed Assets 6,000
Reserves & Surplus 2,000 Inventory 5,000
Long Term Loan 4,000 Receivables 2,400
Short Term Loan 3,000 Cash 600
Payables & Provisions 2,000
Total 14,000 Total 14,000

Given Parameters: Sales ₹ 6,000 lakhs, growing by 20% to ₹ 7,200 lakhs. Profit margin 4%, dividend payout 50%. Conditions: Sales to (Short Term Loan + Payables & Provisions) = 4:3; Fixed Assets to Long Term Loans = 1.5; Debt-Equity Ratio $\le 1.5$.

Solution: Method 1 — Analytical Approach

Particulars Calculation Basis Amount (₹ Lakhs)
Expected Sales ($$S_1$$) ₹ 6,000 + 20% 7,200.00
Projected Net Profit 4% of ₹ 7,200 288.00
Less: Dividend Payout 50% of ₹ 288 (144.00)
Retained Earnings (A) Ploughed Back 144.00
Additional Assets Required 20% of ₹ 14,000 2,800.00
Less: Spontaneous Increase in Payables 20% of ₹ 2,000 (400.00)
Net Funds Required (B) (₹ 14,000 - ₹ 2,000) $\times$ 0.20 2,400.00
External Funds Requirement (EFR = B - A) ₹ 2,400.00 - ₹ 144.00 ₹ 2,256.00 Lakhs

Funds Allocation:

  1. Short-Term Funds: Required Total = $$\frac{3}{4} \times 7,200 = ₹\ 5,400\ \text{Lakhs}$$. Existing Total = Spontaneous Payables ($$2,000 \times 1.2 = 2,400$$) + Existing ST Loan ($$3,000$$) = ₹ 5,400 Lakhs. Amount to raise = Nil.
  2. Long-Term Funds: New Fixed Assets = $$6,000 \times 1.2 = ₹\ 7,200\ \text{Lakhs}$$. Required LT Loans = $$\frac{7,200}{1.5} = ₹\ 4,800\ \text{Lakhs}$$. Less: Existing LT Loans (₹ 4,000). Amount to raise = ₹ 800.00 Lakhs.
  3. New Equity to be Issued: $$\text{EFR } (2,256) - \text{ST Funds } (0) - \text{LT Funds } (800) = \mathbf{₹\ 1,456.00\ \text{Lakhs}}.$$

Solution: Method 2 — Formula Based Approach

$$\text{EFR} = \left(\frac{14,000 - 2,000}{6,000}\right) \times 1,200 - [0.04 \times 7,200 \times 0.50] = (2 \times 1,200) - 144 = \mathbf{₹\ 2,256\ \text{Lakhs}}$$

Algebraic Equations:

  • Short-Term: $$\frac{4}{3} = \frac{7,200}{2,400 + X} \implies X = 3,000 \implies \text{Additional} = 3,000 - 3,000 = \mathbf{₹\ 0\ \text{Lakhs}}$$
  • Long-Term: $$1.5 = \frac{7,200}{Y} \implies Y = 4,800 \implies \text{Additional} = 4,800 - 4,000 = \mathbf{₹\ 800\ \text{Lakhs}}$$
  • New Equity: $$2,256 - 0 - 800 = \mathbf{₹\ 1,456\ \text{Lakhs}}$$
Debt-Equity Ratio Verification: $$\text{New DER} = \frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{Equity}} = \frac{4,800}{3,000 + 1,456 + 2,000 + 144} = \frac{4,800}{6,600} = \mathbf{0.727} \le 1.5 \quad \text{(Condition Satisfied)}$$

❓ ICAI TYK Question 1 (Theoretical) Model Answer

Question: Explain the Interface of Financial Policy and Strategic Management.

Answer: The interface of strategic management and financial policy is anchored in the reality that the starting point of an organization is money and the end point of that organization is also money. Four key dimensions govern this interface:

  1. Sources of Finance and Capital Structure: Choice of debt and equity capital balances risk and return. Norms prescribe $$1:1$$ for public sector firms and $$2:1$$ for private sector firms, with higher leverage allowed in capital-intensive industries.
  2. Investment and Fund Allocation Decisions: Scarce capital is allocated across three distinct proposal types: new product lines, capacity expansion of existing products, and cost reduction/efficiency projects.
  3. Dividend Policy: Dividends represent the portion of earnings that cannot be profitably reinvested ($$r < k_e$$). Policies balance stability against internal expansion retention.
  4. Mutual Interdependence: Corporate strategy is the cause and financial policy is the effect; conversely, financial policy is the cause and corporate strategy is the effect.
❓ ICAI TYK Question 2 (Theoretical) Model Answer

Question: Write a short note on Balancing Financial Goals vis-a-vis Sustainable Growth.

Answer:

  • Concept & SGR Formula: Sustainable growth is the maximum sales expansion achievable without issuing new equity while maintaining target financial leverage and payout: $$\text{SGR} = \text{ROE} \times (1 - d) = P \times A \times T \times b$$
  • Twin Cornerstones: Success requires a balanced Growth Strategy and Growth Capability.
  • CIVICUS Criteria: An organization achieves financial sustainability through diversified revenue sources, regular strategic and financial planning, robust financial systems, value clarity, and financial autonomy.
  • Inflation: Inflation inflates asset replacement costs on historical cost balance sheets, raising external financing requirements and lowering the real SGR.
❓ Advanced Practice Numerical Question 3 (SGR Sensitivity Problem) Practical

Problem: Beta Ltd. has 40% Debt and 60% Equity. Net Profit is ₹150 Lakhs on Sales of ₹2,000 Lakhs. Total Assets = ₹1,200 Lakhs. Dividend declared = ₹45 Lakhs. Calculate current SGR, and the retention ratio required if sales demand grows by 22%.

Solution:

  • Equity = $$0.60 \times 1,200 = \text{₹ 720 Lakhs}$$
  • $$\text{ROE} = \frac{150}{720} = 20.833\%$$
  • $$\text{Dividend Payout } (d) = \frac{45}{150} = 30\% \implies \text{Retention Ratio } (b) = 70\%$$
  • $$\mathbf{\text{Current SGR}} = \text{ROE} \times b = 20.833\% \times 0.70 = \mathbf{14.58\%}$$
  • For 22% Growth: $$b_{\text{required}} = \frac{0.22}{0.20833} = \mathbf{105.6\%}$$. Since retention cannot exceed 100%, the firm cannot achieve 22% growth without either issuing new equity, raising profit margins, accelerating asset turnover, or increasing debt leverage.
