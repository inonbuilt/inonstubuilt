1. Master Formula Sheet

A. Forward and Futures Pricing (Cost of Carry Model)

1. Non-Income Paying Asset (Simple Compounding):

$$F_0 = S_0 \cdot \left(1 + r \cdot \frac{t}{12}\right)$$

2. Continuous Compounding:

$$F_0 = S_0 \cdot e^{rt}$$

3. Asset with Known Cash Dividend ($D$):

$$F_0 = (S_0 - D \cdot e^{-rt_d}) \cdot e^{rt} \quad \text{or} \quad F_0 = S_0 \cdot (1 + rt) - D$$

4. Asset with Continuous Dividend Yield ($y$):

$$F_0 = S_0 \cdot e^{(r - y)t}$$

5. Commodity with Storage ($u$) & Convenience Yield ($c$):

$$F_0 = S_0 \cdot e^{(r + u - c)t}$$
Arbitrage Condition Market Status Arbitrage Strategy Execution
$F_{\text{actual}} > F_{\text{fair}}$ Futures Overvalued Cash-and-Carry: Borrow money, buy stock in spot, sell (short) futures contract.
$F_{\text{actual}} < F_{\text{fair}}$ Futures Undervalued Reverse Cash-and-Carry: Short stock in spot, invest proceeds at risk-free rate, buy (long) futures.
💡 Practical Numerical • Dividend in Rupees Adjustment
Question 1: A stock is currently trading at ₹100. It will pay a dividend of ₹1.00 in one month. The risk-free interest rate is 12% per annum with continuous compounding. Given $e^{0.01} = 1.01005$ and $e^{0.03} = 1.03045$, what is the theoretical Fair Futures Price (FFP) for a 3-month futures contract?
A) ₹102.02
B) ₹103.05
C) ₹104.09
D) ₹101.00
Correct Answer: Option A (₹102.02)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{PV of Dividend} = \frac{₹1.00}{e^{0.12 \times 1/12}} = \frac{₹1.00}{e^{0.01}} = \frac{₹1.00}{1.01005} = ₹0.99$$ $$\text{Adjusted Spot Price} = S_0 - \text{PV}(\text{Div}) = ₹100 - ₹0.99 = ₹99.01$$ $$\text{Fair Futures Price} = \text{Adjusted Spot} \times e^{rt} = 99.01 \times e^{0.12 \times 3/12} = 99.01 \times e^{0.03} = 99.01 \times 1.03045 = \mathbf{₹102.02}$$
💡 Practical Numerical • Storage Cost Adjustment
Question 2: The spot price of Gold is ₹800 per gram. The risk-free interest rate is 12% per annum (3% per quarter). Storage costs of ₹5.00 per gram are payable at the end of 3 months. What is the fair price of a 3-month gold futures contract under simple quarterly compounding?
A) ₹805.00
B) ₹824.00
C) ₹829.00
D) ₹819.00
Correct Answer: Option C (₹829.00)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{PV of Storage Cost} = \frac{₹5.00}{1 + 0.03} = ₹4.854$$ $$\text{Adjusted Spot Price} = \text{Spot Price} + \text{PV}(\text{Storage}) = 800 + 4.854 = ₹804.854$$ $$\text{Fair Futures Price} = 804.854 \times (1 + 0.03) = \mathbf{₹829.00}$$

2. Stock Index Futures & Beta Hedging

Number of Contracts to Trade ($N$):

$$N = \frac{V_p \cdot (\beta^* - \beta_p)}{V_f}$$

Where $V_f = \text{Futures Price} \times \text{Lot Size / Multiplier}$.

  • To reduce beta ($\beta^* < \beta_p$): Result is negative $\implies$ Sell (Short) $N$ Contracts.
  • To increase beta ($\beta^* > \beta_p$): Result is positive $\implies$ Buy (Long) $N$ Contracts.
  • For complete hedge ($\beta^* = 0$): $N = \frac{V_p \cdot \beta_p}{V_f}$ (Sell).
Daily Marking-to-Market (MTM) Margin Rules

$$\text{Initial Margin} = \mu + 3\sigma$$

$$\text{Maintenance Margin} = 75\% \text{ of Initial Margin (or specified %)}$$

If account drops below maintenance margin $\implies$ Margin Call for full deficit back to Initial Margin.

💡 Portfolio Beta Hedging • Contract Sizing
Question 3: An investment manager holds an equity portfolio valued at ₹10,00,00,000 with a portfolio beta of 1.40. The manager wishes to reduce the portfolio's effective beta to 0.80 using 3-month Nifty index futures. Nifty futures currently trade at 18,000 with a contract lot size of 50. What futures position should the manager take, and how many contracts must be traded?
A) Short 67 contracts of Nifty futures
B) Long 67 contracts of Nifty futures
C) Short 156 contracts of Nifty futures
D) Short 89 contracts of Nifty futures
Correct Answer: Option A (Short 67 contracts)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{Value of 1 Futures Contract } (V_f) = 18,000 \times 50 = ₹9,00,000$$ $$N = \frac{V_p \times (\beta_T - \beta_p)}{V_f} = \frac{₹10,00,00,000 \times (0.80 - 1.40)}{₹9,00,000} = \frac{-₹60,00,000}{₹9,00,000} = \mathbf{-66.67 \approx -67\text{ contracts}}$$ The negative sign denotes that the manager must Short (Sell) 67 contracts of Nifty index futures.
💡 Minimum Variance Hedge Ratio • Optimal Beta
Question 4: The variance of monthly returns of a stock is 144, and the variance of monthly returns of its stock futures contract is 324. The correlation coefficient between the stock and futures returns is 0.75. What is the Minimum Variance Hedge Ratio (optimal beta) for hedging this stock position?
A) 0.75
B) 0.50
C) 0.33
D) 1.125
Correct Answer: Option B (0.50)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\sigma_s = \sqrt{144} = 12 \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma_f = \sqrt{324} = 18$$ $$\text{Minimum Variance Hedge Ratio } (h^*) = r_{s,f} \times \frac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_f} = 0.75 \times \frac{12}{18} = 0.75 \times 0.6667 = \mathbf{0.50}$$

💡 Option Strategies • Long Straddle Analysis
Question 5: An investor anticipates high price volatility in ABC Ltd ahead of its earnings release and enters a Long Straddle by purchasing a 3-month Call option with strike ₹500 at premium ₹30 and a 3-month Put option with strike ₹500 at premium ₹20. What are the upper and lower breakeven points of this straddle, and what is the net profit/(loss) if the stock expires at ₹580?
A) Breakevens: ₹450 & ₹550; Net Profit = ₹30
B) Breakevens: ₹470 & ₹530; Net Profit = ₹50
C) Breakevens: ₹480 & ₹520; Net Profit = ₹80
D) Breakevens: ₹450 & ₹550; Net Loss = ₹20
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{Total Premium Paid} = 30 + 20 = ₹50$$ $$\text{Upper Breakeven Point} = \text{Strike} + \text{Total Premium} = 500 + 50 = \mathbf{₹550}$$ $$\text{Lower Breakeven Point} = \text{Strike} - \text{Total Premium} = 500 - 50 = \mathbf{₹450}$$ At expiry $S_T = ₹580$: Call Payoff $= 580 - 500 = ₹80$; Put Payoff $= ₹0$.
$$\text{Net Profit} = \text{Total Payoff} - \text{Total Premium} = 80 - 50 = \mathbf{₹30}$$
💡 Option Strategies • Bull Call Spread Payoff
Question 6: An investor constructs a Bull Call Spread by purchasing a Call option with strike $K_1 = ₹400$ at a premium of ₹35 and selling a Call option with strike $K_2 = ₹460$ at a premium of ₹15. What is the net initial debit, maximum potential profit, and maximum potential loss of this spread?
A) Net Debit = ₹20; Max Profit = ₹40; Max Loss = ₹20
B) Net Debit = ₹35; Max Profit = ₹60; Max Loss = ₹35
C) Net Debit = ₹20; Max Profit = ₹60; Max Loss = ₹20
D) Net Debit = ₹50; Max Profit = ₹20; Max Loss = ₹50
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{Net Initial Debit (Max Loss)} = 35 - 15 = \mathbf{₹20}$$ $$\text{Spread Difference} = K_2 - K_1 = 460 - 400 = ₹60$$ $$\text{Maximum Profit} = \text{Spread Difference} - \text{Net Debit} = 60 - 20 = \mathbf{₹40}$$

4. Binomial Model & Black-Scholes-Merton Formula

A. Binomial Model & Risk-Neutral Pricing

1. Hedge Ratio (Delta $\Delta$):

$$\Delta = \frac{C_u - C_d}{S_u - S_d}$$

2. Risk-Neutral Probability ($p$):

$$p = \frac{R - d}{u - d} \quad \text{where } R = 1 + r \cdot t \text{ (or } e^{rt}\text{)}$$

3. Single-Period Call Value:

$$C_0 = \frac{p \cdot C_u + (1-p) \cdot C_d}{R}$$

B. Black-Scholes-Merton Model

$$C_0 = S_0 \cdot N(d_1) - X \cdot e^{-rt} \cdot N(d_2)$$ $$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0 / X) + \left(r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t}{\sigma \sqrt{t}}; \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{t}$$

C. Put-Call Parity Theorem

$$C_0 + X \cdot e^{-rt} = P_0 + S_0 \implies P_0 = C_0 + X \cdot e^{-rt} - S_0$$
💡 Valuation Models • Binomial Risk-Neutral Pricing
Question 7: A stock currently trades at ₹200. Over the next 6 months, it can either increase by 20% to ₹240 ($u = 1.20$) or decrease by 10% to ₹180 ($d = 0.90$). The risk-free rate of interest is 8% per annum continuously compounded ($e^{0.08 \times 0.5} = e^{0.04} = 1.0408$). What is the risk-neutral probability of an upward movement ($p$) and the fair price of a 6-month European Call option with strike price $K = ₹210$?
A) p = 0.4693; Call Value = ₹13.53
B) p = 0.5000; Call Value = ₹15.00
C) p = 0.6000; Call Value = ₹18.25
D) p = 0.4693; Call Value = ₹28.82
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$p = \frac{e^{rt} - d}{u - d} = \frac{1.0408 - 0.90}{1.20 - 0.90} = \frac{0.1408}{0.30} = \mathbf{0.46933}$$ Payoffs at expiry: $C_u = \max(240 - 210, 0) = ₹30$; $C_d = \max(180 - 210, 0) = ₹0$.
$$\text{Expected Payoff} = (0.46933 \times 30) + (1 - 0.46933) \times 0 = ₹14.08$$ $$\text{Call Option Value} = ₹14.08 \times e^{-0.04} = \frac{₹14.08}{1.0408} = \mathbf{₹13.53}$$
💡 Valuation Models • Put-Call Parity Arbitrage
Question 8: A 6-month European Call option on a non-dividend-paying stock with strike price $K = ₹100$ trades at ₹12.00. The current stock price is ₹105. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is 10% per annum ($e^{-0.10 \times 0.5} = e^{-0.05} = 0.9512$). According to Put-Call Parity, what is the fair theoretical price of a 6-month European Put option on the same stock with strike price ₹100?
A) ₹2.12
B) ₹4.88
C) ₹7.12
D) ₹5.00
Correct Answer: Option A (₹2.12)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{Put-Call Parity Formula}: C + K e^{-rt} = P + S_0$$ $$12.00 + (100 \times 0.9512) = P + 105$$ $$12.00 + 95.12 = P + 105 \implies 107.12 = P + 105$$ $$P = 107.12 - 105 = \mathbf{₹2.12}$$
💡 Valuation Models • Black-Scholes-Merton Interpretation
Question 9: In the Black-Scholes-Merton European Call pricing formula $C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rt} N(d_2)$, what do the cumulative normal distribution terms $N(d_1)$ and $N(d_2)$ represent conceptually?
A) N(d1) represents the Delta (hedge ratio) of the call option; N(d2) represents the risk-neutral probability that the call option will expire in-the-money
B) N(d1) represents the risk-free discount rate; N(d2) represents the stock volatility parameter
C) N(d1) represents corporate default probability; N(d2) represents bond recovery rate
D) N(d1) and N(d2) always equal 0.50 in complete capital markets
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Explanation: In Black-Scholes-Merton valuation, $N(d_1) = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S}$ is the option's Delta ($\Delta$), indicating the number of shares needed to create a riskless hedge per written call. $N(d_2)$ represents the risk-neutral probability that the terminal stock price $S_T$ will exceed the strike price $K$ (i.e. the probability of exercise).

5. The Option Greeks at a Glance

Gamma ($\Gamma$) $\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} = \frac{\partial \Delta}{\partial S}$ Always $> 0$ Always $> 0$ At-The-Money (ATM)
Theta ($\Theta$) $\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}$ Typically $< 0$ (Decay) Typically $< 0$ (Decay) ATM near Expiration

Rho ($\rho$) $\frac{\partial C}{\partial r}$ Always $> 0$ Always $< 0$ Deep ITM Call
💡 Option Greeks • Delta Neutrality
Question 10: A portfolio manager writes 10,000 European Call options on the Nifty index. The delta ($\Delta$) of each call option is +0.65. To make the entire portfolio "Delta-Neutral" against small market price fluctuations, what transaction must the manager execute in the underlying index?
A) Sell 6,500 units of Nifty index
B) Buy 6,500 units of Nifty index
C) Buy 10,000 units of Nifty index
D) Sell 3,500 units of Nifty index
Correct Answer: Option B (Buy 6,500 units)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{Portfolio Delta} = -10,000 \times (+0.65) = -6,500$$ To achieve Delta-Neutrality ($\Delta_{\text{total}} = 0$), the manager requires a delta addition of $+6,500$. Since each unit of underlying stock/index carries a delta of $+1.00$, the manager must Buy 6,500 units of the Nifty index.
💡 Option Greeks • Gamma & Vega Sensitivity
Question 11: At what moneyness level do Option Gamma ($\Gamma$) and Option Vega ($\nu$) reach their peak values, and what does this imply for risk management?
A) Deep out-of-the-money options have peak Gamma and Vega
B) Both Gamma and Vega reach their absolute peak for At-The-Money (ATM) options, meaning ATM options experience the fastest delta shifts and highest sensitivity to changes in implied volatility
C) Deep in-the-money options have highest Gamma and Vega
D) Gamma is constant for all option strikes regardless of underlying price
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: Both Gamma ($\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}$) and Vega ($\frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma}$) peak when options are At-The-Money (ATM). For ATM options, any small change in stock price causes the largest shift in Delta (high Gamma risk), and option premiums are most sensitive to changes in market implied volatility (high Vega risk).

💡 Credit Derivatives • Credit Default Swap Settlement
Question 12: In a Credit Default Swap (CDS) contract with a notional principal of ₹50 Crores, the reference corporate entity defaults. Under the "Cash Settlement" method, an independent poll determines that the defaulted bonds have a post-default recovery market value of 35% of face value. What net settlement amount must the Protection Seller pay to the Protection Buyer?
A) ₹50.00 Crores
B) ₹17.50 Crores
C) ₹32.50 Crores
D) ₹0.00 Crores
Correct Answer: Option C (₹32.50 Crores)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{Loss Given Default (LGD \%)} = 100\% - \text{Recovery Rate} = 100\% - 35\% = 65\%$$ $$\text{Cash Settlement Amount} = \text{Notional Principal} \times (1 - \text{Recovery Rate}) = ₹50\text{ Cr} \times 0.65 = \mathbf{₹32.50\text{ Crores}}$$
💡 Real Options • Financial Option Mapping
Question 13: In strategic capital budgeting, how are the Real Option to Expand and the Real Option to Abandon mapped onto standard financial option contracts?
A) Option to Expand is a Put option; Option to Abandon is a Call option
B) Option to Expand is a Call option (where follow-on capital investment is strike price K); Option to Abandon is a Put option (where liquidation/salvage value is strike price K)
C) Both are Forward contracts with compulsory execution
D) Option to Expand is an interest rate collar; Option to Abandon is a CDS
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: In Real Options analysis: (1) An Option to Expand gives the firm the right to invest additional capital (Exercise Price $K$) to acquire larger future operating cash flows (Asset Price $S$) $\implies$ Call Option. (2) An Option to Abandon gives the firm the right to exit an underperforming project and sell the assets for salvage/liquidation value (Exercise Price $K$) instead of continuing operations $\implies$ Put Option.

7. High-Frequency Exam Traps & Shortcuts

  • Continuous vs Simple Discounting: If exponential values ($e^{rt}$) are provided in question footnotes, use continuous compounding ($S e^{rt}$). Otherwise, use simple interest $S(1+rt)$.
  • Portfolio Beta with Cash: Cash always has a beta of zero ($\beta_{\text{cash}} = 0$). Remember to include cash in total portfolio value $V_p$ when weighting!
  • Sensex Multiplier vs Nifty Lot: Sensex multiplier is typically 50. Nifty lots in past exams are 25, 50, 100, or 200. Always multiply index price by lot size to obtain $V_f$!
  • Margin Call Top-up: The amount of money to deposit on margin call brings the balance back to Initial Margin, NOT just to the maintenance margin.
  • Option Payoff Tables: When constructing payoff tables for combinations (Straddle, Collar), always tabulate columns: $S_T$, Call Payoff, Put Payoff, Total Gross Payoff, Total Premium Paid, Net Profit/(Loss).
💡 Exam Trap • Reverse Working of Spot Price
Question 14: On 1st September, the BSE Sensex futures contract expiring on 1st January (4 months duration) trades at 58,580. The dividend yield on the index is 6% per annum (2% for 4 months), and the risk-free rate is 9% per annum (3% for 4 months). Using the Cost of Carry formula with periodic compounding, what is the theoretical Spot Price of the Sensex on 1st September?
A) 58,580
B) 58,000
C) 59,160
D) 60,000
Correct Answer: Option B (58,000)
Detailed Step-by-Step Working:
$$\text{Fair Futures Price} = \text{Spot Price} \times [1 + (r - y)]$$ $$58,580 = \text{Spot Price} \times [1 + (0.03 - 0.02)] = \text{Spot Price} \times 1.01$$ $$\text{Spot Price} = \frac{58,580}{1.01} = \mathbf{58,000}$$
💡 Exam Trap • Contango vs Backwardation Basis
Question 15: A stock's cash spot price is ₹2,400 while its 1-month futures contract trades at ₹2,300. Which market condition does this scenario reflect, and what is the market "Basis"?
A) Contango market with Basis of -₹100
B) Backwardation market with Basis of +₹100
C) Inverted Contango with zero basis
D) Perfect carrying cost equilibrium
Correct Answer: Option B (Backwardation; Basis = +₹100)
Detailed Explanation:
$$\text{Basis} = \text{Spot Price} - \text{Futures Price} = 2,400 - 2,300 = \mathbf{+₹100}$$ When Futures Price is lower than Spot Price (Basis is positive), the futures curve is downward sloping, which represents a Backwardation market (often driven by high convenience yield or severe near-term physical shortages). In a normal Contango market, Futures Price exceeds Spot Price (Basis is negative).
