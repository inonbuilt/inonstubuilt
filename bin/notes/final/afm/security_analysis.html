1. Scope of Security Analysis & The Valuation Gap

Security Analysis is the systematic process of evaluating financial assets (stocks, bonds, derivatives) to estimate their future risk and return profile, establish their intrinsic value, and make rational investment decisions (Buy, Hold, or Sell).

Intrinsic Value (\(V_0\))

The true, justified fundamental economic worth of a security derived from future expected cash flows discounted at the required rate of return commensurate with its risk.

Market Price (\(P_0\))

The prevailing quoted transaction price on stock exchanges determined by the instantaneous equilibrium between aggregate market demand and supply.

Decision Rule Based on Valuation Gap

• If \(V_0 > P_0\): The stock is Undervalued by the market \(\implies\) BUY / ACCUMULATE signal.
• If \(V_0 < P_0\): The stock is Overvalued by the market \(\implies\) SELL / SHORT signal.
• If \(V_0 = P_0\): The stock is Fairly Valued \(\implies\) HOLD signal.

Security analysis is broadly bifurcated into two foundational analytical disciplines:

  • Fundamental Analysis: Assumes every security has an underlying intrinsic value determined by economic, industry, and corporate financial health.
  • Technical Analysis: Assumes stock prices are determined solely by market psychology, supply and demand, and that price history and volume patterns repeat over time.

2. Fundamental Analysis: The EIC Framework

Fundamental analysis adopts a structured Top-Down Approach, universally recognized as the E-I-C Framework (Economy \( ightarrow\) Industry \( ightarrow\) Company). The fundamental premise is that a firm's operational and financial fortunes are fundamentally constrained by its macroeconomic operating environment and industry structure.

1. Economic Analysis

Assesses the macroeconomic climate to gauge overall economic expansion, purchasing power, monetary policy, and systemic profitability.

2. Industry Analysis

Evaluates the structural attractiveness, growth stage, competitive dynamics, regulatory regime, and cost drivers of the specific sector.

3. Company Analysis

Examines company-specific financial performance (earnings, leverage, margins) and qualitative advantages (management moat, corporate governance).

3. Economic Analysis

The health of the macroeconomy sets the broad direction for capital markets. Corporate revenue, profitability, input costs, and valuation multiples are directly linked to macroeconomic aggregates.

3.1 Key Macroeconomic Factors

Macro Factor Economic Significance Stock Market Impact
GDP Growth Rate Measures aggregate national economic output and income expansion. Robust GDP growth increases corporate earnings, boosting equity valuations.
Savings & Investment Provides domestic capital formation to finance commercial capex. Higher domestic institutional investment stabilizes equity markets against foreign portfolio outflows.
Inflation (CPI / WPI) Erodes purchasing power and escalates raw material input costs. High inflation prompts central bank rate hikes, raising discount rates and depressing equity P/E multiples.
Interest Rate Regimes Determines corporate borrowing costs and alternative yield on fixed income. Low interest rates stimulate capex and equity demand; rising rates suppress equity valuations.
Fiscal Deficit & Budget Indicates government borrowing requirements and sovereign fiscal discipline. Excessive deficits crowd out private investment and fuel inflation, while targeted capital expenditure stimulates growth.
Balance of Payments & Forex Reflects external sector stability and currency resilience. Depreciating domestic currency hurts import-heavy sectors but benefits exporters (IT, Pharma).
Monsoon & Agriculture In agrarian economies like India, agriculture drives rural disposable incomes. Good monsoons boost rural consumer demand (FMCG, two-wheelers, tractors, fertilizers).

3.2 Techniques Used in Economic Analysis

1. Anticipatory Surveys

Surveys of business confidence, consumer sentiment, and corporate capital spending intentions that provide forward-looking indicators before official lagging data is published.

2. Barometric / Indicator Approach

• Leading Indicators: Turn ahead of the economy (Stock prices, money supply M3, new order book).
• Coincident Indicators: Move simultaneously with GDP (Industrial production IIP, manufacturing output).
• Lagging Indicators: Turn after economic shifts (Unemployment rate, bank loan default rates).

3. Econometric Model Building

Mathematical formulations connecting dependent variables (GDP, inflation) to independent variables using simultaneous regression equations to forecast macroeconomic trajectories.

4. Industry Analysis

Once macroeconomic prospects are established, an investor must select the most promising industries. Even in a flourishing economy, structural shifts cause some industries to boom while others stagnate.

4.1 Industry Life Cycle (ILC) Analysis

Life Cycle Stage Characteristics & Market Dynamics Investment Risk-Return Profile
1. Pioneering Stage Nascent technology, rapid innovation, high product experimentation, severe competition, numerous business failures. Extremely high risk; substantial capital loss possibility, but enormous upside for surviving market leaders.
2. Rapid Growth Stage Industry survivors emerge; demand expands exponentially; market barriers solidify; profit margins peak. Prime investment phase; significant capital appreciation with moderate, manageable business risk.
3. Maturity / Stabilization Growth slows down to match GDP; product standardization; fierce price competition; stable oligopoly. Low risk, steady dividend yields; limited capital appreciation; cash-cow companies.
4. Decline Stage Obsolescence caused by technological breakthroughs or substitute products; shrinking sales and margins. High risk of capital erosion; poor investment prospects; divestment candidates.

4.2 Structural Forces: Michael Porter's Five Forces Model

ICAI stresses Porter's Five Forces framework for determining the long-run intrinsic profitability and pricing power of an industry:

  1. Threat of New Entrants: High barriers (patents, massive capex, scale economies) protect industry margins.
  2. Bargaining Power of Buyers: Concentrated buyers squeeze selling prices and margins.
  3. Bargaining Power of Suppliers: Monopolistic suppliers increase raw material input costs.
  4. Threat of Substitute Products: Availability of viable alternatives caps product pricing power.
  5. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors: Cutthroat price wars destroy sector return on equity (ROE).

4.3 Analytical Techniques for Industry Forecasting

  • End-Use Method: Maps demand for an industry’s output based on consumption requirements of consuming user industries (e.g. steel demand forecasted via construction and automotive production).
  • Input-Output Analysis: Uses national input-output tables to trace inter-industry dependency matrices.
  • Regression Analysis: Correlates historical industry sales against macroeconomic indices (such as GDP, disposable income).

5. Company Analysis

Company analysis evaluates whether an individual enterprise can outperform its industry peers and convert sectoral tailwinds into shareholder wealth.

Financial (Quantitative) Parameters

• Earnings Per Share (EPS): Core driver of equity returns.
• Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Multiple: Market valuation multiple.
• Return on Equity (ROE): Efficiency in deploying shareholder net worth (DuPont analysis).
• Operating & Net Margins: Pricing power and cost efficiency.
• Debt-to-Equity Ratio: Solvency and financial risk.
• Interest Coverage Ratio: Safety cushion against debt default.

Qualitative (Non-Financial) Parameters

• Competitive Moat: Sustainable competitive advantage (brand power, network effects, patents).
• Management Quality & Integrity: Proven capital allocation track record and shareholder alignment.
• Corporate Governance: Independent board oversight, transparent disclosures, clean audit reports.
• ESG Compliance: Environmental footprint, social responsibility, ethical compliance.

6. Dividend Growth Model & The P/E Multiple

ICAI highlights the mathematical connection between the Gordon Dividend Growth Model and the Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Multiple.

P/E Multiple Derived from Gordon Dividend Discount Model Valuation Synthesis
$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{K_e - g} = \frac{E_1 (1 - b)}{K_e - b \cdot r} \quad \implies \quad \frac{P_0}{E_1} = \frac{1 - b}{K_e - g}$$
Where:
• \(P_0\) = Current intrinsic market price of the share
• \(E_1\) = Expected Earnings Per Share (EPS) for the next period
• \(D_1 = E_1(1 - b)\) = Expected Dividend Per Share for the next period
• \(b\) = Retention ratio \((1 - \text{Dividend Payout Ratio})\)
• \((1 - b)\) = Dividend payout ratio
• \(K_e\) = Cost of equity / required rate of return of equity investors
• \(g = b \times r\) = Sustainable growth rate of earnings and dividends
• \(r\) = Return on Equity (ROE) earned on retained earnings
Analytical Insights for CA Final Exams

1. Higher Payout \((1 - b)\): Increases current dividend income, but lowers retention and hence lowers future growth \(g\).
2. If \(r > K_e\) (Growth Company): A higher retention ratio \(b\) increases growth faster than it reduces payouts, resulting in a higher P/E multiple.
3. If \(r < K_e\) (Declining Company): Retention destroys value; a higher dividend payout \((1 - b)\) commands a higher P/E multiple.
4. If \(K_e\) decreases: (e.g. lower interest rates or lower risk premium), the denominator shrinks, causing the P/E multiple to expand.

7. Technical Analysis: Meaning, Assumptions & Principles

Technical Analysis is the study of historical market action—primarily price graphs, chart formations, trading volume, and statistical indicators—to forecast future price movements. Rather than assessing fundamental financial statements, technical analysts study investor psychology and market sentiment.

7.1 Core Assumptions of Technical Analysis

  1. Supply and Demand Determine Price: The market price of a stock is determined solely by the interaction of buyers and sellers.
  2. Rational and Irrational Drivers: Supply and demand are governed by countless fundamental, psychological, rational, and emotional variables.
  3. Prices Move in Trends: Stock prices do not jump randomly; they move in persistent upward, downward, or sideways trends that endure for measurable periods.
  4. Shifts in Trend Can Be Detected: Reversals in supply and demand balance create recognizable patterns on charts that signal impending trend shifts.

7.2 Three Cardinal Principles of Technical Analysis

1. Market Discounts Everything

All information—fundamentals, earnings forecasts, geopolitical events, monetary policies, and emotional bias—is instantly reflected in the market price.

2. Price Moves in Trends

A price in motion is far more likely to continue its established path than reverse, until counteracting forces become dominant.

3. History Tends to Repeat Itself

Human psychology is invariant over centuries. Greed, fear, and panic produce identical chart patterns across generations.

8. The Dow Theory

Formulated by Charles H. Dow (founder of the Dow Jones Company and The Wall Street Journal), the Dow Theory is the foundational bedrock of modern technical analysis.

8.1 The Three Market Movements

Trend Classification Time Horizon Analogy & Characteristics
Primary Movement 1 to 3+ years Tides of the Ocean: The broad, overarching macro bull or bear market. Reflects basic fundamental trends in business activity.
Secondary Movement 3 weeks to 3 months Waves: Sharp counter-trend reactions retracing \(33\%\) to \(66\%\) (one-third to two-thirds) of the preceding primary swing.
Minor Fluctuations Hours to under 3 weeks Ripples: Daily price oscillations, day-to-day noise, and short-term volatility. Subject to manipulation and ignored by Dow theorists.

8.2 Phases of Primary Markets

Three Phases of a Bull Market

1. Accumulation Phase: Informed, astute institutional investors quietly buy undervalued stocks from distressed sellers while public sentiment is utterly despondent.
2. Public Participation Phase: Corporate earnings improve, economic data turns positive, and the general public enters the market, accelerating price gains.
3. Speculative / Excess Phase: Rampant euphoria; speculative buying drives prices far beyond intrinsic value; uneducated retail investors rush in.

Three Phases of a Bear Market

1. Distribution Phase: Astute investors who bought during accumulation recognize overvaluation and quietly unload holdings to euphoric retail buyers.
2. Panic Phase: Buyers evaporate, credit tightens, selling turns frantic, and prices cascade downwards in sharp, vertical declines.
3. Despair / Distress Phase: Protracted erosion of prices where fundamentally sound stocks are dumped in utter hopelessness before the next accumulation cycle begins.

8.3 Indispensable Rules of Dow Theory

  • Averages Must Confirm Each Other: In the US, the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) and Dow Jones Transportation Average (DJTA) must confirm each other. A bull market is not validated unless both indices break to new highs together (production must be confirmed by shipment).
  • Volume Must Confirm the Trend: In a bull market, trading volume must expand on rallies and dry up on downward corrections. In a bear market, volume expands on declines and shrinks on corrective bounces.
  • Trend Presumed to Continue: An established primary trend is presumed to remain in continuous effect until definitive reversal signals are confirmed by both averages.

9. Elliott Wave Theory

Developed in 1934 by Ralph Nelson Elliott, the Elliott Wave Theory posits that financial markets unfold in repetitive, identifiable rhythmic cycles driven by the natural rhythm of crowd psychology, rooted in the mathematical properties of the Fibonacci Sequence.

9.1 The Basic 8-Wave Cycle

A complete market cycle consists of 8 waves: a 5-wave impulsive trend followed by a 3-wave corrective consolidation.

5-Wave Impulsive Phase (1 - 2 - 3 - 4 - 5)

Drives the dominant primary trend direction:
• Wave 1: Initial recovery from bottom.
• Wave 2: Sharp correction retracing much of Wave 1, but holding above Wave 1's starting low.
• Wave 3: The most powerful, elongated, and high-volume impulse wave.
• Wave 4: Complex, sideways consolidation.
• Wave 5: Final push to new highs driven by broad retail euphoria.

3-Wave Corrective Phase (A - B - C)

Corrects the preceding 5-wave impulsive advance:
• Wave A: Initial break down, often mistaken for a minor pullback.
• Wave B: Counter-trend "bull trap" bounce on lower volume.
• Wave C: Severe, broad decline finishing the corrective cycle.

The Three Inviolable Cardinal Rules of Elliott Wave Theory

1. Rule 1: Wave 2 can never retrace more than \(100\%\) of Wave 1 (cannot break below the origin of Wave 1).
2. Rule 2: Wave 3 can never be the shortest among the three impulse waves (Waves 1, 3, and 5). It is frequently the longest.
3. Rule 3: Wave 4 can never enter the price territory of Wave 1 (no overlap between the low of Wave 4 and the peak of Wave 1).

10. Random Walk Theory

Pioneered by British statistician M. G. Kendall (1953) and later refined by Eugene Fama, the Random Walk Hypothesis challenges technical analysis fundamentally.

The Drunkard's Walk Analogy

Kendall observed that weekly changes in stock prices behaved nearly as if they were generated by a suitably designed roulette wheel where each spin is statistically independent. In layman's terms, successive price movements behave exactly like a drunkard walking in a dark lane—stumbling unpredictably, taking random steps left and right, with no memory of his previous steps.

Core Assertions of Random Walk Theory

  • Successive price changes are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables.
  • Past price history contains zero predictive information about future price movements.
  • Chart patterns (double tops, head and shoulders) are mere optical illusions and ex-post statistical artifacts, offering no actionable predictive edge.

11. Charting Techniques & Graphical Analysis

Technical analysts deploy four primary charting methodologies to visually inspect market action:

Chart Type Data Used Construction & Visual Features Key Utility
1. Line Chart Closing Price Only Connects successive periodic closing prices with a continuous line. Filters out intraday price volatility. Best for detecting long-term macro primary trends.
2. Bar Chart (OHLC) Open, High, Low, Close A vertical bar connects the Low and High. A left horizontal tick indicates Open; a right horizontal tick indicates Close. Displays full periodic range and intraday trading range.
3. Japanese Candlestick Open, High, Low, Close Constructs a "Real Body" between Open and Close, with thin vertical wicks/shadows reaching the High and Low.
• White / Green Candle: Close > Open (Bullish session).
• Black / Red Candle: Close < Open (Bearish session).
• Doji Candle: Open = Close (Cross shape; signifies severe market indecision).		 Provides instant visual identification of buyer vs seller dominance and immediate reversal cues.
4. Point & Figure (P&F) Price Reversals Only (No Time/Volume) Uses columns of 'X' for rising prices and 'O' for falling prices. Determined by:
• Box Size: Minimum price movement required to plot a new mark (e.g. ₹1 or ₹2).
• Reversal Criterion: Number of boxes (typically 3 boxes) needed to reverse direction and open a new column.		 Completely eliminates time and minor price noise, highlighting pure support, resistance, and breakouts.

12. Technical Market Indicators & Breadth Measures

In addition to individual price charts, technicians utilize aggregate market indicators to gauge the broad health of the financial system:

Market Breadth Index Breadth Indicator
$$\text{Breadth Index} = \frac{\text{Net Advances or Declines}}{\text{Total Number of Issues Traded}} = \frac{\text{Advances} - \text{Declines}}{\text{Total Issues Traded}}$$
• Interpretation: A rising market index supported by a strong positive Breadth Index signifies genuine widespread market strength.
• Divergence: If the headline index rises while Breadth declines (only a handful of mega-caps rally while majority decline), it signals severe underlying technical weakness and an imminent reversal.
Confidence Index Smart Money Indicator
$$\text{Confidence Index} = \frac{\text{Average Yield on High-Grade (AAA) Bonds}}{\text{Average Yield on Low-Grade / Speculative (BBB) Bonds}}$$
• Rationale: When institutional bond investors are confident in economic prosperity, they demand a smaller risk premium on speculative bonds, narrowing the yield gap and driving the Confidence Index closer to 1.0 (Rising Index \(\implies\) Bullish signal).
• When fear mounts, investors flee to safety (AAA bonds), widening the spread and causing the Confidence Index to plunge (Falling Index \(\implies\) Bearish warning).

Other Significant Market Indicators

  • Volume of Transactions: Volume confirms price action. A breakout on massive trading volume validates the move; a breakout on dry volume indicates a trap.
  • Relative Strength Analysis: Compares the price performance ratio of a stock relative to its benchmark index \(\left(\frac{\text{Price of Stock}}{\text{Market Index}}\right)\). Stocks exhibiting sustained relative strength outshine peers during both bull and bear markets.
  • Odd-Lot Theory: A contrary-opinion theory tracking trades of small retail investors (odd lots < 100 shares). The theory assumes small retail traders are consistently wrong at market tops and bottoms. When odd-lot purchases soar near an index peak, technicians take it as a sell signal.
  • Support and Resistance:
    • Support: Price level or trough where demand is sufficiently strong to halt and reverse a price decline.
    • Resistance: Price level or peak where selling pressure overcomes buying interest, capping further advances.
    • Role Reversal: Once a resistance level is decisively breached on high volume, it flips to become a new support floor.

13. Price Chart Patterns & Visual Interpretation

Chart patterns are geometric shapes formed by price bars that signal either a continuation of the ongoing trend or a major trend reversal.

Reversal Patterns (Trend Change)

• Head and Shoulders Top: Left shoulder, higher head, lower right shoulder. The horizontal line joining the troughs is the Neckline. A decisive breakdown below the neckline signals a major Bearish reversal. The projected minimum downward price target equals the vertical height from the top of the Head to the Neckline.
• Inverse Head and Shoulders: Upside-down formation indicating a powerful Bullish reversal when price breaks above the neckline.
• Double Top ("M" Pattern): Two consecutive peaks at roughly the same price level; indicates exhaustion of buyers and impending fall.
• Double Bottom ("W" Pattern): Two consecutive troughs testing the same support level; indicates powerful buying support and impending rally.

Continuation Patterns (Trend Resumption)

• Channels: Parallel trendlines containing price swings (ascending channel in uptrends, descending channel in downtrends).
• Triangles / Coils: Price converges between converging trendlines with diminishing volatility:
  - Ascending Triangle: Flat upper resistance with rising support (Bullish bias).
  - Descending Triangle: Flat support with falling lower peaks (Bearish bias).
  - Symmetrical Triangle: Coiling indecision; breakout indicates direction.
• Flags and Pennants: Brief, compact consolidations appearing after steep flagpole moves, signaling rapid resumption of the prior trend.
• Gaps: Unfilled spaces between consecutive trading sessions (Common, Breakaway, Runaway/Measuring, and Exhaustion gaps).

14. Quantitative Moving Averages (AMA & EMA)

Moving averages smooth out short-term erratic price fluctuations to reveal the underlying trend direction. ICAI tests both Arithmetic and Exponential Moving Averages.

14.1 Arithmetic Moving Average (AMA / SMA)

Arithmetic Moving Average Formula Simple Moving Average
$$\text{AMA}_{n, t} = \frac{P_t + P_{t-1} + P_{t-2} + \dots + P_{t-(n-1)}}{n} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} P_{t-i}$$
• Standard horizons: 200-day SMA (Major long-term trend), 50/60-day SMA (Intermediate trend), 10-day SMA (Short-term trend).
• Golden Cross: Short-term MA crosses above long-term MA \(\implies\) Powerful Bullish buy signal.
• Death Cross: Short-term MA crosses below long-term MA \(\implies\) Severe Bearish sell signal.

14.2 Exponential Moving Average (EMA)

Unlike the AMA which gives equal \(1/n\) weight to all days, the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) applies exponentially decreasing weights to older data, assigning the highest weight to the most recent trading price.

Exponential Moving Average (EMA) Calculation Core ICAI Exam Formula
$$\alpha (\text{Smoothing Constant / Exponent}) = \frac{2}{n + 1}$$ $$\text{EMA}_t = \alpha \times P_t + (1 - \alpha) \times \text{EMA}_{t-1}$$ $$\text{Or Equivalently: } \text{EMA}_t = \text{EMA}_{t-1} + \alpha \times (P_t - \text{EMA}_{t-1})$$
Where:
• \(\text{EMA}_t\) = Exponential Moving Average of current day \(t\)
• \(P_t\) = Closing price of current day \(t\)
• \(\text{EMA}_{t-1}\) = Exponential Moving Average of the preceding day \(t-1\)
• \(\alpha\) = Weight / smoothing exponent (e.g. for a 31-day EMA, \(\alpha = \frac{2}{31 + 1} = \frac{2}{32} = 0.0625 \approx 0.062\))
• \(n\) = Specified number of days for the moving average window
Buy and Sell Signals from Moving Averages

• BUY Signal: Stock price rises above the moving average line while the MA is flattening out or turning upwards, or bounces up off a rising MA line.
• SELL Signal: Stock price falls below the moving average line while the MA is flattening out or turning downwards, or drops after failing to break above a declining MA line.

15. Fundamental Analysis vs Technical Analysis Comparison

ICAI frequently tests the comparative evaluation of these two opposing philosophies:

Basis of Comparison Fundamental Analysis Technical Analysis
Core Philosophy Every share has an intrinsic value determined by underlying economic fundamentals. Prices are determined strictly by the law of supply and demand and market psychology.
Primary Data Source Financial statements, balance sheets, P&L accounts, macroeconomic data, industry reports. Historical price series, trading volume, open interest, and visual chart formations.
Investment Horizon Medium-to-long-term investing (1 to 5+ years). Short-term trading and tactical timing (intraday, swing, days to weeks).
Primary Function What to buy: Identifies fundamentally undervalued securities. When to buy: Identifies optimal market entry and exit timing.
View on Market Efficiency Assumes the market may misprice assets in the short run, but price converges to intrinsic value in the long run. Assumes market price immediately discounts all known facts and focuses on recurring human behavior patterns.
Key Criticism Slow to adjust to rapid market psychology shifts; financial statements are historical and subject to accounting bias. Highly subjective interpretations; critics argue it is self-defeating and contradicted by Random Walk Theory.

16. Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)

Formulated by Nobel Laureate Eugene Fama in 1970, the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) states that financial asset prices fully, instantly, and accurately reflect all available and relevant information. In an efficient capital market, current security prices are unbiased estimates of intrinsic value.

Three Common Misconceptions Debunked

Misconception 1: "No One Can Make Returns"

Reality: EMH does not state investors cannot earn positive returns. Investors earn fair, normal returns commensurate with their level of risk exposure. EMH only asserts that investors cannot consistently earn abnormal / excess risk-adjusted returns (Alpha \(\alpha = 0\)).

Misconception 2: "Throwing Darts is Just as Good"

Reality: While an investor cannot outsmart an efficient market in stock picking, portfolio management is still indispensable for risk profiling, asset allocation, optimal diversification, and tax efficiency.

Misconception 3: "Price Swings Prove Markets are Irrational"

Reality: In an efficient market, new information arrives randomly and unpredictably. Because new news is random, immediate price adjustments must also be random, giving the appearance of erratic fluctuation.

17. The Three Forms of Market Efficiency & Empirical Tests

Fama categorized market efficiency into three progressively encompassing tiers based on the subset of information incorporated into market prices:

Level of Efficiency Information Incorporated into Stock Prices Analytical Implication Standard Empirical Testing Method
1. Weak Form All historical price data, trading volumes, and trading records. Technical Analysis is futile and cannot generate abnormal returns. Fundamental analysis can still yield superior returns. • Wald-Wolfowitz Run Test: Tests randomness of price change signs.
• Serial Correlation / Autocorrelation: Tests correlation between \(r_t\) and \(r_{t-k}\).
• Filter Tests: Tests trading rules (e.g. buy if up 5%).
2. Semi-Strong Form All publicly available information (historical prices + financial statements, annual reports, corporate news, macroeconomic data). Both Technical and Fundamental Analysis are futile. Prices adjust instantly upon announcement of public news. Only private insider information can generate alpha. • Event Studies: Measures abnormal returns around corporate announcements (earnings surprises, stock splits, mergers, dividend declarations).
3. Strong Form All information whatsoever, whether publicly known or privately held insider knowledge. No analytical method or insider trading can consistently generate abnormal profits. Market is perfectly omniscient. • Analysis of corporate insider trades, specialist performance, and mutual fund manager alpha over decades.
Wald-Wolfowitz Run Test for Weak Form Efficiency Statistical Test Formula
$$\mu_r (\text{Expected Number of Runs}) = \frac{2 n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2} + 1$$ $$\sigma_r (\text{Standard Error of Runs}) = \sqrt{\frac{2 n_1 n_2 (2 n_1 n_2 - n_1 - n_2)}{(n_1 + n_2)^2 (n_1 + n_2 - 1)}}$$ $$Z \text{ or } t = \frac{r - \mu_r}{\sigma_r}$$
Where:
• \(n_1\) = Number of positive price changes (+)
• \(n_2\) = Number of negative price changes (-)
• \(r\) = Total number of observed runs (unbroken sequences of like signs)
• \(\mu_r\) = Theoretical mean expected runs under the null hypothesis of randomness
• \(\sigma_r\) = Standard deviation / standard error of the sampling distribution of runs
• Decision Rule: If \(|t| < t_{\text{critical}}\), the null hypothesis of randomness is Accepted \(\implies\) Market is Weak-Form Efficient.

18. Market Anomalies & Challenges to EMH

Empirical market research has documented persistent historical patterns that contradict the strict predictions of EMH, known as Market Anomalies:

1. Calendar Anomalies

• January Effect: Stocks (especially small-caps) historically outperform in January due to year-end tax-loss harvesting in December followed by aggressive reinvestment.
• Weekend / Monday Effect: Average returns on Mondays are significantly lower (often negative) than other trading weekdays.

2. Fundamental Anomalies

• Size Effect (Small-Firm Effect): Portfolios of small-cap companies consistently outperform large-cap portfolios on a risk-adjusted basis.
• Value Anomaly: Stocks with low P/E ratios and high Book-to-Market (B/M) ratios consistently beat high-multiple growth stocks.

3. Behavioral Finance Insights

Cognitive biases prevent markets from achieving perfect rationality:
• Overconfidence & Anchoring: Investors anchor to purchase prices.
• Loss Aversion (Prospect Theory): Pain of losing ₹1 is twice the joy of winning ₹1.
• Herd Mentality: Creates market bubbles and panic crashes.

20. Comprehensive Solved ICAI Practical Problems

Solved Problem 1: Exponential Moving Average (EMA) & Decision Analysis (ICAI TYK 1)

Problem Statement: Closing values of Sensex from the 6th to 17th day of January were as follows:

Day No. Date Trading Day Sensex Closing Value
1 6 Jan Thursday 14,522
2 7 Jan Friday 14,925
3 8 Jan Saturday No Trading
4 9 Jan Sunday No Trading
5 10 Jan Monday 15,222
6 11 Jan Tuesday 16,000
7 12 Jan Wednesday 16,400
8 13 Jan Thursday 17,000
9 14 Jan Friday No Trading
10 15 Jan Saturday No Trading
11 16 Jan Sunday No Trading
12 17 Jan Monday 18,000

Required: Calculate the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of Sensex during this period. The previous day EMA of Sensex prior to 6th January can be assumed as 15,000. The value of the exponent for 31 days EMA is 0.062. Provide a detailed market analysis on the basis of your calculations.

Solution & Working Notes

Formula:

Problem Statement: The closing values of a stock market index for the month of October 2007 (20 trading days) were as follows:

Required: Test the Weak Form of the Efficient Market Hypothesis by applying the Run Test at the 5% and 10% levels of significance.
Given: Value of \(t\) at 5% significance is 2.101 (18 degrees of freedom); Value of \(t\) at 10% significance is 1.734 (18 degrees of freedom).

Solution & Working Notes

Step 1: Determine the Signs of Price Changes (+ or -) between Successive Trading Days:

There are 20 trading days \(\implies\) 19 price changes:

  • 03.10: \(2,780 - 2,800 = -20\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 04.10: \(2,795 - 2,780 = +15\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 05.10: \(2,830 - 2,795 = +35\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 08.10: \(2,760 - 2,830 = -70\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 09.10: \(2,790 - 2,760 = +30\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 10.10: \(2,880 - 2,790 = +90\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 11.10: \(2,960 - 2,880 = +80\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 12.10: \(2,990 - 2,960 = +30\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 15.10: \(3,200 - 2,990 = +210\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 16.10: \(3,300 - 3,200 = +100\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 17.10: \(3,450 - 3,300 = +150\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 19.10: \(3,360 - 3,450 = -90\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 22.10: \(3,290 - 3,360 = -70\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 23.10: \(3,360 - 3,290 = +70\) \(\implies\) (+)
  • 24.10: \(3,340 - 3,360 = -20\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 25.10: \(3,290 - 3,340 = -50\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 29.10: \(3,240 - 3,290 = -50\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 30.10: \(3,140 - 3,240 = -100\) \(\implies\) (–)
  • 31.10: \(3,260 - 3,140 = +120\) \(\implies\) (+)

Step 2: Sign Sequence and Count of Runs (\(r\)):
Sequence: \((-), (+, +), (-), (+, +, +, +, +, +, +), (-, -), (+), (-, -, -, -), (+)\)
• Total Price Changes \(N = 19\)
• Total Positive Changes \(n_1 = 11\)
• Total Negative Changes \(n_2 = 8\)
• Observed Number of Runs \(r = 8\)

Step 3: Calculate Expected Mean Number of Runs (\(\mu_r\)):

$$\mu_r = \frac{2 n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2} + 1 = \frac{2 \times 11 \times 8}{11 + 8} + 1 = \frac{176}{19} + 1 = 9.263 + 1 = 10.263$$

Step 4: Calculate Standard Deviation of Runs (\(\sigma_r\)):

$$\sigma_r = \sqrt{\frac{2 n_1 n_2 (2 n_1 n_2 - n_1 - n_2)}{(n_1 + n_2)^2 (n_1 + n_2 - 1)}} = \sqrt{\frac{176 \times (176 - 19)}{19^2 \times (19 - 1)}} = \sqrt{\frac{176 \times 157}{361 \times 18}} = \sqrt{\frac{27,632}{6,498}} = \sqrt{4.2524} = 2.062$$

Step 5: Calculate the Test Statistic (\(t\)):

$$t = \frac{r - \mu_r}{\sigma_r} = \frac{8 - 10.263}{2.062} = \frac{-2.263}{2.062} = -1.0975 \quad \implies \quad |t| = 1.098$$

Step 6: Statistical Inference:
• At 5% Significance Level: Critical \(t = 2.101\). Since \(|t| = 1.098 < 2.101\), the difference is statistically not significant.
• At 10% Significance Level: Critical \(t = 1.734\). Since \(|t| = 1.098 < 1.734\), the difference is also statistically not significant.
• Conclusion: The null hypothesis of randomness CANNOT BE REJECTED. Price changes are statistically independent. Therefore, the stock market satisfies the Weak Form of the Efficient Market Hypothesis during the sample period.

21. Self-Test Practice Questions (ICAI TYK)

Q1: Explain the Dow Theory of technical analysis. How are market movements classified?
Model Answer: Originated by Charles Dow, the Dow Theory posits that market averages reflect the aggregate impact of all available information and act as a reliable barometer of business trends.
Dow divided market movements into three simultaneous classifications:
1. Primary Trend (1–3 years): The broad underlying bull or bear trend representing ocean tides;
2. Secondary Reactions (3 weeks to 3 months): Counter-trend swings retracing 33% to 66% of the primary swing, analogous to waves;
3. Minor Fluctuations (daily noise to under 3 weeks): Day-to-day random ripples.
Key tenants require that the Industrial and Transportation averages confirm each other, and trading volume must confirm the price direction.
Q2: Explain the Elliott Wave Theory of technical analysis. What are impulse and corrective waves?
Model Answer: Formulated by Ralph Nelson Elliott in 1934, this theory defines market price action in repetitive wave structures rooted in the Fibonacci sequence and crowd psychology.
A complete market cycle consists of 8 waves:
• 5-Wave Impulsive Pattern (1, 2, 3, 4, 5): Moves in the direction of the dominant primary trend. Waves 1, 3, and 5 are impulsive thrusts, while Waves 2 and 4 are corrective pullbacks.
• 3-Wave Corrective Pattern (A, B, C): Corrects the entire 5-wave impulsive advance against the basic trend.
Three cardinal rules: Wave 2 never retraces more than 100% of Wave 1; Wave 3 is never the shortest impulse wave; and Wave 4 never overlaps with the price territory of Wave 1.
Q3: What is the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)? Explain the major misconceptions associated with it.
Model Answer: EMH (Eugene Fama) states that share prices fully, instantaneously, and unbiasly reflect all available information. Consequently, no investor can consistently earn abnormal risk-adjusted returns.
Three Misconceptions:
1. "Investors cannot earn any returns": False; investors earn normal returns proportional to the risk assumed; only abnormal alpha is zero.
2. "Stock selection is a lottery / dart-throwing exercise": False; professional portfolio management remains crucial for diversification, risk tailoring, and tax optimization.
3. "Random price swings mean market is irrational": False; because new fundamental information arrives randomly, instantaneous price adjustments must also be random.
Q4: Explain the differences between the Breadth Index and the Confidence Index as technical market indicators.
Model Answer:
• Breadth Index: Calculated as \(\frac{\text{Net Advances or Declines}}{\text{Total Issues Traded}}\). It evaluates the internal breadth of market participation. If the index rises but breadth is negative, it signals an unconfirmed, fragile rally dominated by a few heavyweights.
• Confidence Index: Calculated as \(\frac{\text{Yield on High-Grade Bonds}}{\text{Yield on Low-Grade / Speculative Bonds}}\). It measures the willingness of institutional "smart money" to accept credit risk. When confidence is high, low-grade yields drop closer to high-grade yields, raising the ratio towards 1.0 (Bullish). A falling ratio warns of capital flight to safety before equity markets crack.
