Topic 01

Overview of Valuation

📌 Content in ICAI Book (Official Study Material)

"The definition of an Investment is - Investment involves commitment of funds with an objective to obtain a return that would pay off the investor for the time during which the funds are invested or locked, for the expected rate of inflation over the investment horizon, and for the risk involved. Most investments are expected to have future cash flows and a stated market price (e.g., price of a common stock), and one must estimate a value for the investment to determine if its current market price is consistent with his estimated intrinsic value. Investment returns can take many forms, including earnings, cash flows, dividends, interest payments, interest on interest payments or capital gains (increases in value) during an investment horizon." "Knowing what an asset is worth and what determines its value is a pre-requisite for making intelligent investment decisions while choosing investments for a portfolio or in deciding an appropriate price to pay or receive in a business takeover and in making investment, financing and dividend choices when running a business. We can make reasonable estimates of value for most assets, and that the fundamental principles determining the values of all types of assets whether real or financial, are the same. Some assets may be easier to be valued than others and for different assets the details of valuation and the uncertainty associated with their value estimates may vary. However, the core" principles of valuation always remain the same.

💡 Clear Pedagogical Explanation & Analytical Framework

Intuitive Concept of Valuation: Valuation is the analytical process of determining the present economic worth of an asset or security. In financial theory, the intrinsic value of an asset is the present value of all expected future cash flows discounted at an appropriate risk-adjusted rate:

Fundamental Valuation Principle
$$V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + k)^t}$$
  • Intrinsic Value ($V_0$): The true, fundamental worth of a security based on objective underlying financial data, earning capacity, and risk characteristics.
  • Market Price ($P_0$): The actual price determined by the forces of demand and supply in the open market at any point in time.
  • Decision Rules:
    • If $P_0 < V_0$: The security is Undervalued $\rightarrow$ BUY signal.
    • If $P_0 > V_0$: The security is Overvalued $\rightarrow$ SELL / AVOID signal.
    • If $P_0 = V_0$: The security is Fairly Valued (Market is in equilibrium).

Subjectivity vs. Objectivity: While the market price is objective (an observed transaction price), intrinsic value is inherently subjective because it depends on the valuer's estimates of future growth rates, reinvestment needs, and discount rates.

🎯 Exam Relevance & High-Scoring Focus

Exam Weightage & Past Trends: Frequently tested as a 4-mark conceptual question in CA Final AFM. Examiners often test whether students understand the difference between Book Value, Liquidation Value, and Intrinsic Value.

  • Common Pitfall: Confusing market price with intrinsic value. Remember that markets can be irrational in the short term, but fundamental prices tend to converge toward intrinsic value over the long horizon.
  • Key Scoring Tip: Always state the investment recommendation explicitly (e.g., "Since Intrinsic Value (₹95) > Market Price (₹82), the share is undervalued and the investor should BUY").
📝 Worked Example: Valuation Decision Rule

Question: An equity analyst estimates that Share XYZ will generate annual dividends of ₹5 per share perpetually. If the required rate of return is 10% and the current market price is ₹42, should an investor purchase the share?

Solution:

$$V_0 = \frac{D}{K_e} = \frac{₹5}{0.10} = ₹50$$
Intrinsic Value $V_0 = ₹50$. Current Market Price $P_0 = ₹42$.
Decision: Since $P_0 (₹42) < V_0 (₹50)$, the share is Undervalued by ₹8. The investor should BUY.

Return Concepts & IRR

📌 Content in ICAI Book (Official Study Material)

A sound investment decision depends on the correct use and evaluation of the rate of return. Some of the different concepts of return are given as below:
2.1 Required Rate of Return
Required rate of return is the minimum rate of return that the investor is expected to receive while making an investment in an asset over a specified period of time. This is also called Opportunity Cost or Cost of Capital because it is the highest level of expected return forgone which is available elsewhere from investment of similar risks. Many times required rate of return and expected return
are used interchangeably.
2.2 Discount Rate
"Discount Rate is the rate used to calculate present value of future cash flows Discount rate depends on the risk-free rate and risk premium of an investment. Actually, each cash flow stream coming"
"from different assets can be discounted at a different discount rate. This is because of variation in risk premium which may be due to expected inflation rate, different maturity levels and probability of defaults. This can be explained with the help of term structure of interest rates. For instance, in upward sloping term structure of interest rates, interest rates increase with the maturity as longer maturity may mean more inflation risk, more liquidity risk or more default risk."
"Though future cash flows can be discounted at different discount rate, one may use the same discount rate to get the same present value of a stream of cash flows. When a single discount rate is applied instead of many discount rates, many individual discount rates can be replaced with an equivalent single discount rate which eventually gives the same present value."

💡 Clear Pedagogical Explanation & Analytical Framework

Return Concepts Breakdown:

  • Holding Period Return (HPR): Total percentage return earned by an investor over an arbitrary investment holding period: $$HPR = \frac{\text{Cash Income (Dividends/Interest)} + (P_1 - P_0)}{P_0}$$
  • Realized Return vs. Expected Return: Realized return is the historical, ex-post return actually generated. Expected return is the ex-ante, probability-weighted forecast of future returns.
  • Discount Rate: The rate used to equate future cash flows to present value, representing the opportunity cost of capital for investments with identical risk.
  • Internal Rate of Return (IRR): The unique discount rate that sets the Net Present Value (NPV) of all cash inflows and outflows to zero: $$NPV = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + IRR)^t} - C_0 = 0$$
🎯 Exam Relevance & High-Scoring Focus

Exam Weightage: 4-6 marks. IRR calculation using linear interpolation is a staple calculation technique across AFM Paper 2.

  • Interpolation Formula: $$IRR = L + \left[ \frac{NPV_L}{NPV_L - NPV_H} \right] \times (H - L)$$
  • Scoring Tip: When using interpolation, always state your trial discount rates clearly and write out the full present value calculation table.
📝 Worked Example: Estimating IRR of Multi-Period Asset

Question (ICAI Book): Suppose you are recommended to invest ₹20,000 now in an asset that offers a cash flow of ₹3,000 one year from now and ₹23,000 two years from now. Estimate the IRR of the investment.

Solution:

$$NPV = \frac{₹3,000}{(1 + r)^1} + \frac{₹23,000}{(1 + r)^2} - ₹20,000 = 0$$

Let us test $r = 15\%$:

$$PV = \frac{₹3,000}{1.15} + \frac{₹23,000}{(1.15)^2} = ₹2,608.70 + ₹17,391.30 = ₹20,000$$
Since Present Value at 15% exactly equals the initial outlay of ₹20,000, $NPV = 0$.
Internal Rate of Return (IRR) = 15.00% p.a.
Topic 03

Equity Risk Premium

📌 Content in ICAI Book (Official Study Material)

"Equity risk premium is the excess return that an investment in equity shares provides over a risk free rate, such as return from tax free government bonds. This excess return compensates investors for taking on the higher risk of investing in equity shares of a company. The size of the premium will change depending upon the level of risk in a particular portfolio and will also change over time as market risk fluctuates. Generally, high-risk investments are compensated with a higher premium."
"The equity risk premium is based on the idea of the risk-reward trade-off. However, equity risk premium is a theoretical concept because it is difficult to predict that how a particular stock or the stock market as a whole will perform in the future. It can only be estimated by observing stock market and government bond market over a specified period of time, for instance from 1990 to the present period. Further, estimates may vary depending on the time frame and method of calculation."
3.1 Explanation of Equity Risk Premium
"Investment in equity shares of a company is a high risk investment. If an investor is investing in equity shares of a company, he wants some risk premium over the risk-free investment avenues such as government bonds. For example, if an investor could earn a 7% return on a Government Bond (which is generally considered as risk free investment), a company's share should earn 7% return plus an additional return (the equity risk premium) in order to attract the investor."
"Equity investors try to achieve a balance between risk and return. If a company wants to pursue investors to put their money into its stock, it must provide a stimulus in the form of a premium to attract the equity investors. If the stock gives a 15% return, in the example mentioned in the previous paragraph, the equity risk premium would be 8% (15% - 7% risk free rate). However, practically, the price of a stock, including the equity risk premium, moves with the market. Therefore, the investors"
"use the equity risk premium to look at historical values, risks, and returns on investments."
3.2 Calculating the Equity Risk Premium
"To calculate the equity risk premium, we can use the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), which is usually written:"
Rx = Rf + βx (Rm - Rf) Where:
"Rx = expected return on equity investment in ""x""(company x)"
Rf = risk-free rate of return
"βx = beta of ""x"""
Rm = expected return of market
"Now, if we assume that x is identical to the Market Index, m, then Rx = Rm. Beta is a measure of a stock's systematic risk ; and if x = m, then βx = βm = 1. Whereas Rm - Rf is known as the Market Risk Premium; Rx - Rf is the risk premium of a particular stock. If x is an equity investment, then Rx - Rf is the equity risk premium; if x = m, then the market premium and the equity risk premium are the same."
"Therefore, the equity risk premium can be calculated as follows:"
Equity Risk Premium = Rx - Rf = βx (Rm - Rf)

💡 Clear Pedagogical Explanation & Analytical Framework

Understanding Equity Risk Premium (ERP): The Equity Risk Premium is the incremental return that investors demand for taking on the additional risk of investing in equity securities compared to a risk-free government bond:

Equity Risk Premium Formula
$$ERP = E(R_m) - R_f$$

Key Estimation Approaches:

  • 1. Historical Approach: Measures the historical difference between broad market index returns (e.g., Nifty 50 or S&P 500) and government bond yields over a multi-decade timeframe.
    • Arithmetic Mean: Appropriate for single-period estimation.
    • Geometric Mean (CAGR): Preferred for long-term multi-period compounding valuation models.
  • 2. Forward-Looking / Implied Approach: Uses the current market price of the aggregate index and projected future dividends/earnings to solve for the implied expected market return: $$E(R_m) = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g \implies ERP = \left( \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g \right) - R_f$$
  • 3. Macroeconomic Model Approach: Models ERP as a function of economic fundamentals like expected GDP growth, inflation spread, and productivity growth.
🎯 Exam Relevance & High-Scoring Focus

Critical Examiner Trap: Pay acute attention to the phrasing in examination questions:

  • If the question says "Market return is 14%", then $E(R_m) = 14\%$ and $ERP = 14\% - R_f$.
  • If the question says "Market risk premium is 8%", then $(E(R_m) - R_f) = 8\%$ directly. Do not subtract $R_f$ again!
📝 Worked Example: Implied ERP Calculation

Question: The current dividend yield on the Nifty 50 Index is 1.8%, expected long-term dividend growth rate is 10.2%, and the yield on 10-year Government Securities is 7.2%. Compute the forward-looking Equity Risk Premium.

Solution:

$$E(R_m) = \text{Dividend Yield} + g = 1.8\% + 10.2\% = 12.0\%$$ $$ERP = E(R_m) - R_f = 12.0\% - 7.2\% = 4.80\%$$
Implied Forward-Looking ERP = 4.80% p.a.

Required Return on Equity

📌 Content in ICAI Book (Official Study Material)

"If equity risk premium is calculated as indicated above, required rate of return can be easily calculated with the help of Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). The main insight of the model is that the investors evaluate the risk of an asset in terms of the asset's contribution to the systematic risk (cannot be reduced by portfolio diversification) of their total portfolio. CAPM model provides a relatively objective procedure for required return estimation; it has been widely used in valuation." "So, the required return on the share of particular company can be computed as below:" Return on share 'A' = Risk free return + β x Market Risk Premium Example: "Risk free rate 5%," β 1.5 "and, Market risk premium 4.5% Calculate Required return on equity." Solution Required return on share A = Risk free return + β x Market Risk Premium = 0.05 + 1.5 (0.045) = 0.1175 or 11.75%

💡 Clear Pedagogical Explanation & Analytical Framework

Required Return on Equity ($K_e$): Represents the minimum rate of return a company must earn on equity-financed projects to leave its stock price unchanged. Three primary methodologies are recognized under AFM:

  • 1. Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM):
    CAPM Formula
    $$K_e = R_f + \beta_i \times [E(R_m) - R_f]$$
    Where $\beta_i = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\sigma_m^2} = \rho_{im} \times \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}$.
  • 2. Build-Up Method: Used commonly in unlisted or private companies where beta cannot be directly observed: $$K_e = R_f + ERP + \text{Size Premium} + \text{Industry Risk Premium} + \text{Company-Specific Risk Premium}$$
  • 3. Bond Yield Plus Risk Premium (BYPRP): $$K_e = \text{Yield on Company's Long-Term Debt} + \text{Equity Risk Premium (typically 3% to 5%)}$$
🎯 Exam Relevance & High-Scoring Focus

Scoring Checklist:

  • Ensure beta is properly unlevered and relevered if capital structure changes are involved: $$\beta_L = \beta_U \times [1 + (1 - t) \times (D/E)]$$
  • In build-up methods, clearly list every additive premium component separately in your answer sheet.
📝 Worked Example: CAPM Required Return on Equity

Question (ICAI Book): Risk-free rate is 5%, Beta is 1.5, and Expected Return on Market Portfolio is 11%. Compute the required return on equity share.

Solution:

$$K_e = R_f + \beta \times (R_m - R_f)$$ $$K_e = 5\% + 1.5 \times (11\% - 5\%) = 5\% + 1.5 \times 6\% = 5\% + 9\% = 14.00\%$$
Required Return on Equity ($K_e$) = 14.00% p.a.
Topic 05

Discount Rate Selection in Relation to Cash Flows

📌 Content in ICAI Book (Official Study Material)

"Cash flows are discounted at a suitable rate to arrive at the present value of future cash flows. Cash flows are required by any organization to settle their debt claims and taxes. Whatever amount remains are the cash flows available to equity shareholders. When cashflows to be available to equity shareholders are discounted, the required rate of return on equity is an appropriate discount rate. Further, when cash flows are available to meet the claims of all of company's stakeholders, then the cost of capital is the appropriate discount rate."
5.1 Concept of Nominal Cash Flow and Real Cash Flow
"Nominal cash flow is the amount of future revenues the company expects to receive and expenses it expects to pay out, without any adjustments for inflation. For instance, a company which wants to invest in a utility plant wants to forecast its future revenues and expenses it has to incur while earning its income (i.e. wages to labour, electricity, water, gas pipeline etc.)."
"On the other hand, Real cash flow shows a company's cash flow with adjustments for inflation. Since inflation reduces the spending power of money over time, the real cash flow shows the effects of inflation on a company's cash flow."
"In the short term and under conditions of low inflation, the nominal and real cash flows are almost same. However, in conditions of high inflation rates, the nominal cash flows will be higher than the real cash flows."
5.2 Discount rate selection in Equity Valuation
"From the above discussion, it can be concluded that cash flows can be nominal or real. When cash flows are stated in real terms, then they are adjusted for inflation. However, in case of nominal cash flow, inflation is not adjusted."
"For nominal cash flow, nominal rate of discount is used and for real cash flow, real rate of discount is used. While valuing equity shares, only nominal cash flows are considered. Therefore, only nominal discount rate is considered. The reason is that the tax applying to corporate earnings is generally stated in nominal terms. Therefore, using nominal cash flow in equity valuation is the right approach because it reflects taxes accurately."
"Moreover, when the cash flows are available to equity shareholders only, nominal discount rate applicable in case of equity is used. And, the nominal after tax weighted average cost of capital is used when the cash flows are available to all the company's capital providers."

💡 Clear Pedagogical Explanation & Analytical Framework

Matching Principle of Cash Flows and Discount Rates: The golden rule in security valuation is that the discount rate must strictly correspond to the definition and risk of the cash flow being discounted:

Cash Flow Type Applicable Discount Rate Valuation Output
Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC / $K_o$) Enterprise Value ($EV = \text{Value of Debt} + \text{Value of Equity}$)
Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE) Cost of Equity ($K_e$) Equity Value (Direct Market Value of Equity)
Nominal Cash Flows Nominal Discount Rate ($K_{nom}$) Valid Present Value
Real Cash Flows (adjusted for inflation) Real Discount Rate ($K_{real}$) Valid Present Value
Fisher Relationship for Real vs. Nominal Rates
$$(1 + K_{nom}) = (1 + K_{real}) \times (1 + i) \implies K_{real} = \frac{1 + K_{nom}}{1 + i} - 1$$
🎯 Exam Relevance & High-Scoring Focus

Critical Examiner Warning:

  • Never discount FCFF at $K_e$, and never discount FCFE at WACC. Doing so is considered a fundamental conceptual error by ICAI evaluators.
  • If the question specifies cash flows in constant (real) rupees, you must convert the discount rate to real terms before discounting, or inflate the cash flows to nominal rupees.
📝 Worked Example: Converting Nominal to Real Discount Rate

Question: A company's nominal cost of capital is 15.5% and the expected inflation rate is 5% p.a. What is the real discount rate to be applied to constant price cash flows?

Solution:

$$1 + K_{real} = \frac{1 + 0.155}{1 + 0.05} = \frac{1.155}{1.05} = 1.10 \implies K_{real} = 10.00\%$$
Real Discount Rate = 10.00% p.a.

6.3 Cash Flow Based Models
In the case of Dividend Discounting Valuation model (DDM) the cash flows are dividend which are to be distributed among equity shareholders. This cash flow does not take into consideration the cash flows which can be utilised by the business to meet its long-term capital expenditure requirements and short-term working capital requirement. Hence dividend discount model does not reflect the true free cash flow available to a firm or the equity shareholders after adjusting for its capex and working capital requirement.
"Free cash flow valuation models discount the cash flows available to a firm and equity shareholders after meeting its long term and short-term capital requirements. Based on the perspective from which valuations are done, the free cash flow valuation models are classified as:"
 Free Cash Flow to Firm Model (FCFF)
 Free Cash Flow to Equity Model (FCFE)
"In the case of FCFF model, the discounting factor is the cost of capital (Ko) whereas in the case of FCFE model the cost of equity (Ke) is used as the discounting factor."
FCFE along with DDM is used for valuation of the equity whereas FCFF model is used to find out the overall value of the firm.
6.3.1 Calculation of Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF): FCFF can be calculated as follows:
(a) Based on its Net Income:
FCFF= Net Income + Interest expense *(1-tax) + Depreciation -/+ Capital Expenditure -/+ Change in Non-Cash Net Working Capital

6.4 Dividend Discount Model versus Free Cash Flow to Equity Model
In the dividend discount model the analyst considers the stream of expected dividends to value the company's stock. It is assumed that the company follows a consistent dividend payout ratio which can be less than the actual cash available with the firm.
Dividend discount model values a stock based on the cash paid to shareholders as dividend.
A stock's intrinsic value based on the dividend discount model may not represent the fair value for the shareholders because dividends are distributed in the form of cash from profits. In case the company is maintaining healthy cash in its balance sheet then it means that dividend pay-outs is low
which could result in undervaluation of the stock.
"In the case of free cash flow to equity model a stock is valued on the cash flow available for distribution after all the reinvestment needs of capex and incremental working capital are met. Thus, using the free cash flow to equity model provides a better measure for valuations in comparison to"

Comprehensive Equity Valuation Framework: Equity shares can be valued using three distinct methodological families:

1. Dividend Discount Models (DDM)

  • Zero Growth Model: Assumes constant dividend perpetually: $$P_0 = \frac{D}{K_e}$$
  • Constant Growth Model (Gordon Model):
    Gordon Growth Formula
    $$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{K_e - g} = \frac{D_0(1 + g)}{K_e - g}$$
    Where $g = b \times r$ ($b = $ Retention Ratio $= 1 - \text{Payout Ratio}$, $r = $ Return on Equity / ROE). Condition: $K_e > g$.
  • Two-Stage / Multi-Stage Growth Model: Useful for firms experiencing high growth in initial years transitioning to stable growth: $$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{D_t}{(1 + K_e)^t} + \frac{P_n}{(1 + K_e)^n}, \quad \text{where } P_n = \frac{D_{n+1}}{K_e - g_n}$$
  • H-Model (Linear Declining Growth): $$P_0 = \frac{D_0(1 + g_n)}{K_e - g_n} + \frac{D_0 \times H \times (g_a - g_n)}{K_e - g_n}$$ Where $H = \frac{\text{High Growth Transition Period}}{2}$, $g_a = $ initial abnormal growth, $g_n = $ normal terminal growth.

2. Earnings Based Models

  • Walter's Model:
    Walter's Share Valuation Formula
    $$P = \frac{D + \frac{r}{K_e}(E - D)}{K_e}$$
    Where $E = $ EPS, $D = $ DPS, $r = $ Return on Investment, $K_e = $ Cost of Equity.
    • If $r > K_e$ (Growth firm): Optimum dividend payout = 0% (Retain 100%).
    • If $r < K_e$ (Declining firm): Optimum dividend payout=100% (Retain 0%).
    • If $r = K_e$ (Normal firm): Dividend policy is indifferent (Value remains constant at all payout ratios).
  • P/E Multiple Approach: $P_0 = \text{EPS} \times \text{Industry P/E Ratio}$.

3. Cash Flow Valuation Models (FCFF & FCFE)

Cash Flow Formulas
$$FCFF = EBIT(1 - t) + \text{Depreciation} - \text{Capital Expenditures} - \Delta \text{Working Capital}$$ $$FCFE = FCFF - \text{Interest}(1 - t) + \text{Net Borrowings} - \text{Preference Dividends}$$

4. Rights Issue & Buyback Mechanics

  • Theoretical Ex-Rights Price (TERP / $P_{ex}$): $$P_{ex} = \frac{N \times P_{cum} + S}{N + 1}$$ Where $N = $ number of existing shares required to get 1 right share, $P_{cum} = $ cum-right market price, $S = $ subscription/issue price.
  • Value of a Right ($R$): $$R = P_{cum} - P_{ex} = \frac{P_{cum} - S}{N + 1} = \frac{P_{ex} - S}{N}$$
  • Buyback of Shares:
    • Number of shares bought back $= \frac{\text{Cash deployed in Buyback}}{\text{Buyback Price per share}}$
    • Post-Buyback EPS $= \frac{\text{Total PAT}}{\text{Original Shares} - \text{Shares Bought Back}}$
    • Post-Buyback Market Price $= \text{Post-Buyback EPS} \times \text{P/E Ratio}$.

High-Frequency Exam Area (10-15 Marks):

  • Walter's Model: Always calculate share price at the current payout AND at the optimum payout to demonstrate the difference in value.
  • Right Shares: When asked to prove the impact on shareholder wealth, present three cases: (i) Exercises rights, (ii) Renounces rights and sells in market, (iii) Does nothing (suffers wealth dilution).
  • $D_0$ vs. $D_1$: Look closely at the wording: "just paid" or "recently declared" means $D_0$; "expected to pay" or "dividend next year" means $D_1$.
📝 Worked Example: Gordon Growth Model & Share Valuation

Question: A company recently paid a dividend of ₹4.00 per share. It is expected to grow at 8% per annum indefinitely. If the required rate of return is 12%, calculate the current intrinsic value of the share.

Valuation of Preference Shares: Preference shares represent a hybrid security having a prior claim over ordinary equity for dividend payments and capital return upon liquidation:

  • 1. Irredeemable (Perpetual) Preference Shares:
    Perpetual Preference Share Formula
    $$P_0 = \frac{PD}{K_p}$$
    Where $PD = $ annual preference dividend (Face Value $\times$ Coupon Rate), $K_p = $ required return on preference shares.
  • 2. Redeemable Preference Shares:
    Redeemable Preference Share Formula
    $$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{PD}{(1 + K_p)^t} + \frac{RV}{(1 + K_p)^n} = PD \times PVIFA(K_p, n) + RV \times PVIF(K_p, n)$$
    Where $RV = $ redemption value at maturity (at par or at premium), $n = $ years to redemption.

8.11 Term Structure Theories
The term structure theories explain the relationship between interest rates or bond yields and different terms or maturities. The different term structures theories are as follows:
(a) Expectation Theory: As per this theory the long-term interest rates can be used to forecast short-term interest rates in the future as long-term interest rates are assumed to unbiased estimator of the short term interest rate in future.
"(b) Liquidity Preference Theory: As per this theory investors are risk averse and they want a premium for taking risk. Long-term bonds have higher interest rate risk because of higher maturity, hence, long-term interest rates should have a premium for such a risk. Further, people prefer liquidity and if they are forced to sacrifice the same for a longer period, they need a higher compensation for the same. Hence, as per this theory, the normal shape of a yield curve is Positive sloped one."
"(c) Preferred Habitat Theory (Market Segmentation Theory): This theory states that though different investors may be having different preference for shorter and longer maturity periods and therefore, they have their own preferred habitat. Hence, the interest rate structure depends on the demand and supply of fund for different maturity periods for different market segments. In case there is a mismatch between these forces, the players of a particular segment should be compensated at a higher rate to pull them out from their preferred habitat; hence, that will determine the shape of the yield curve. Mastering Fixed Income & Bond Valuation: Bond valuation forms the theoretical and mathematical backbone of CA Final AFM Paper 2:

1. Basic Bond Pricing Equation

Bond Valuation Formula
$$B_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1 + YTM)^t} + \frac{M}{(1 + YTM)^n} = C \times PVIFA(YTM, n) + M \times PVIF(YTM, n)$$

Semi-Annual Coupon Rule: Replace $C$ with $C/2$, replace $YTM$ with $YTM/2$, and replace $n$ with $2n$.

2. Yield Measures & Approximation Formula

  • Current Yield ($CY$): $CY = \frac{\text{Annual Coupon Interest}}{B_0}$.
  • Yield to Maturity (YTM) Approximation:
    YTM Approximation Formula
    $$YTM \approx \frac{C + \frac{M - B_0}{n}}{\frac{M + B_0}{2}}$$
  • Coupon vs. YTM Relationships:
    • If Coupon Rate $=$ YTM $\implies B_0 = M$ (Par Bond).
    • If Coupon Rate $>$ YTM $\implies B_0 > M$ (Premium Bond).
    • If Coupon Rate $<$ YTM $\implies B_0 < M$ (Discount Bond).

3. Malkiel's 5 Bond Theorems

  1. Bond price moves inversely to changes in interest rates/yields.
  2. For a given change in yield, the percentage price increase from a yield decrease is larger than the percentage price decrease from an equal yield increase (Convexity effect).
  3. For a given change in yield, long-term bonds experience greater percentage price volatility than short-term bonds.
  4. The price sensitivity of long-term bonds increases at a diminishing rate as maturity lengthens.
  5. Lower-coupon bonds are more price sensitive than higher-coupon bonds for a given change in yield.

4. Macaulay Duration & Modified Duration (Volatility)

Macaulay Duration ($D$): The weighted-average time (in years) until all cash flows are received, where the weights are the present values of each cash flow:

Macaulay Duration Formula
$$D = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n} t \times \frac{CF_t}{(1 + y)^t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + y)^t}} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} [t \times w_t]$$
  • Modified Duration ($D^*$ or Volatility): Measures percentage change in bond price for a 1% change in yield: $$D^* = \frac{D}{1 + y}$$
  • Price Sensitivity Approximation: $$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx - D^* \times \Delta y$$
  • Convexity Adjustment: $$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx - D^* \times \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Convexity} \times (\Delta y)^2$$

5. Portfolio Immunization

A bond portfolio is immunized against interest rate risk when the weighted Macaulay duration of the portfolio equals the investor's planned holding period ($D_{portfolio} = H$). This ensures that price risk and reinvestment risk offset each other perfectly.

6. Convertible Bonds Analysis

  • Conversion Ratio ($CR$): Number of equity shares received per bond.
  • Conversion Value ($CV$): $CV = CR \times \text{Current Market Price of Equity Share}$.
  • Market Conversion Price: $\frac{\text{Market Price of Convertible Bond}}{CR}$.
  • Conversion Premium: $\frac{\text{Market Price of Convertible} - CV}{CV} \times 100$.
  • Downside Risk: $\frac{\text{Market Price of Convertible} - \text{Straight Debt Value}}{\text{Straight Debt Value}} \times 100$.

7. Money Market Instruments & Repos

  • Treasury Bill Yield: $$Y = \left[ \frac{F - P}{P} \right] \times \frac{365}{M} \times 100$$
  • Repo Transactions:
    • Leg 1 (Start): Dirty Price $= \text{Clean Price} + \text{Accrued Interest}$.
    • Leg 2 (Repurchase): Settlement Amount $= \text{Leg 1 Amount} \times \left[ 1 + \text{Repo Rate} \times \frac{\text{Days}}{360 \text{ or } 365} \right]$.

Question: Why should the duration of a coupon carrying bond always be less than the time to its maturity?
Comprehensive Answer & Pedagogical Breakdown

Theoretical Analysis & Mathematical Proof:

Macaulay Duration measures the weighted-average time (in years) until an investor receives all future cash flows from a bond, where the weight assigned to each period is the present value of that period's cash flow divided by the total current bond price:

Macaulay Duration Equation
$$D = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \left[ t \times \frac{PV(CF_t)}{P_0} \right]$$

For a coupon-carrying bond, the investor receives intermediate cash flows (annual or semi-annual coupon interest payments) at periods $t = 1, 2, \dots, n-1$, prior to the final maturity date $t = n$ (when the final coupon plus principal redemption is received):

  • Each intermediate coupon payment has a time weight $t < n$.
  • Since the weights $\frac{PV(CF_t)}{P_0}$ are strictly positive and sum to 1.0 (i.e., $\sum w_t = 1.0$), the duration is a weighted average of numbers that are strictly less than $n$ (the coupon dates) and $n$ (the maturity date).
  • A weighted average of a set of values where some values are less than $n$ and some equal $n$ must mathematically be strictly less than $n$: $$D < n$$

Contrast with Zero-Coupon Bonds: In a Zero-Coupon Bond (ZCB) or Pure Discount Bond, there are no intermediate cash flows ($CF_1 = CF_2 = \dots = CF_{n-1} = 0$). The only cash flow occurs at maturity $t = n$. Consequently, the weight at maturity is $w_n = 1.0$, and its duration exactly equals its maturity ($D = n$).

Conclusion: Intermediate coupon payments return a portion of the investor's capital early, shortening the economic waiting time. Therefore, the duration of any coupon-bearing bond is always strictly less than its term to maturity ($D < n$).

Question: Write short notes on Zero Coupon Bonds.
Comprehensive Answer & Pedagogical Breakdown

Comprehensive Short Note on Zero Coupon Bonds (ZCBs):

  • 1. Definition & Mechanics: A Zero Coupon Bond (also called a Deep Discount Bond) is a debt security that pays no periodic interest (coupons) during its life. Instead, it is issued at a substantial discount to its nominal (face) value and redeemed at par upon maturity. The entire return to the investor is the capital appreciation between the issue price and the redemption value: $$P_0 = \frac{M}{(1 + YTM)^n}$$
  • 2. Duration Property: Because there are no intermediate coupon cash flows, the Macaulay Duration of a Zero Coupon Bond is identical to its term to maturity: $$D_{ZCB} = n \text{ years}$$ This makes ZCBs the ideal instrument for asset-liability matching and portfolio immunization when an investor has a specific, fixed future liability date.
  • 3. Elimination of Reinvestment Risk: Traditional coupon bonds carry significant reinvestment risk because coupon payments must be reinvested at uncertain future interest rates to achieve the stated YTM. Since ZCBs pay no intermediate coupons, reinvestment risk is completely eliminated; the investor's realized return precisely matches the YTM if held to maturity.
  • 4. High Price Volatility (Interest Rate Risk): For a given maturity, a Zero Coupon Bond exhibits the highest price sensitivity (Modified Duration) among all bonds to changes in market yields because all cash flows are concentrated at the furthest point in time.
  • 5. Tax Treatment in India: Under the Income-tax Act, 1961, notified Zero Coupon Bonds issued by infrastructure capital companies or public sector companies are treated as capital assets, and the difference between redemption price and issue price is taxed as Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) if held for more than 12 months.
Summary: ZCBs offer zero reinvestment risk, duration equal to maturity, maximum price volatility, and predictable capital appreciation for long-term target funding.
