1. Essential Formula Cheat Sheet

(a) Net Asset Value (NAV): $$\mathbf{NAV = \frac{\text{Market Value of Investments} + \text{Receivables} + \text{Cash/Liquid Balances} - \text{Accrued Liabilities}}{\text{Total Number of Units Outstanding}}}$$
(b) Front-End & Exit Loads: $$\text{Public Offer Price (POP)} = \text{NAV} \times (1 + \text{Front-End Load \%}) \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{\text{NAV}}{1 - \text{Front-End Load \%}}$$ $$\text{Repurchase Price (Redemption)} = \text{NAV} \times (1 - \text{Exit Load \%}) \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{\text{NAV}}{1 + \text{Back-End Load \%}}$$
(c) Holding Period Return & Yield: $$R = \frac{(\text{NAV}_1 - \text{NAV}_0) + \text{Dividend} + \text{Capital Gain Distribution}}{\text{NAV}_0} \times 100$$ $$\text{Simple Annualized Return} = R \times \frac{12}{M} \quad \text{or} \quad R \times \frac{365}{\text{Days}}$$ $$\text{Effective Annualized Yield (EAY)} = (1 + r_{period})^N - 1$$
(d) Risk-Adjusted Performance Ratios: $$\text{Sharpe Ratio (Total Risk)} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}$$ $$\text{Treynor Ratio (Systematic Risk)} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\beta_p}$$ $$\text{Jensen's Alpha (Abnormal Gain)} = R_p - [R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f)]$$
(e) Fama's Performance Attribution: $$\text{Total Excess Return } (R_p - R_f) = \text{Risk Premium } [\beta_p(R_m - R_f)] + \text{Selectivity}$$ $$\text{Diversification Penalty} = \left(\frac{\sigma_p}{\sigma_m} - \beta_p ight)(R_m - R_f)$$ $$\mathbf{\text{Net Selectivity} = \text{Gross Selectivity} - \text{Diversification Penalty} = (R_p - R_f) - \frac{\sigma_p}{\sigma_m}(R_m - R_f)}$$
💡 Formula Application • NAV Calculation
Question 1: A mutual fund scheme has market value of investments of ₹450 Cr, accrued dividend receivable of ₹12 Cr, liquid cash balance of ₹8 Cr, accrued management and advisory fees of ₹5 Cr, and other current liabilities of ₹15 Cr. The scheme has 10 Cr units outstanding. What is the Net Asset Value (NAV) per unit?
A) ₹44.50
B) ₹45.00
C) ₹47.00
D) ₹46.50
Correct Answer: Option B (₹45.00)
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Total Scheme Assets} = 450 + 12 + 8 = ₹470\text{ Cr}$$ $$\text{Total Scheme Liabilities} = 5 + 15 = ₹20\text{ Cr}$$ $$\text{Net Assets} = 470 - 20 = ₹450\text{ Cr}$$ $$\text{NAV per Unit} = \frac{\text{Net Assets}}{\text{Total Units Outstanding}} = \frac{₹450\text{ Cr}}{10\text{ Cr units}} = \mathbf{₹45.00}$$
💡 Formula Application • Entry & Exit Loads
Question 2: An investor purchases units in an open-ended mutual fund scheme when the NAV is ₹25.00. The fund levies an entry load (front-end load) of 2.0% on the purchase. Two years later, the investor redeems all units when the NAV has grown to ₹32.00, subject to an exit load (back-end load) of 1.5%. What is the purchase price (Public Offer Price) and the net redemption price per unit?
A) Purchase Price: ₹25.50; Redemption Price: ₹31.52
B) Purchase Price: ₹25.00; Redemption Price: ₹32.00
C) Purchase Price: ₹24.50; Redemption Price: ₹32.48
D) Purchase Price: ₹26.00; Redemption Price: ₹30.50
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Purchase Price (POP)} = \text{NAV} \times (1 + \text{Front-End Load \%}) = 25.00 \times (1 + 0.02) = \mathbf{₹25.50}$$ $$\text{Net Redemption Price} = \text{NAV} \times (1 - \text{Exit Load \%}) = 32.00 \times (1 - 0.015) = 32.00 \times 0.985 = \mathbf{₹31.52}$$
💡 Formula Application • Holding Period Return & Annualized Yield
Question 3: An investor purchases mutual fund units at an NAV of ₹40.00 on 1st April. On 30th September (after 6 months), the fund distributes a cash dividend of ₹2.00 per unit, and the NAV stands at ₹44.00. What is the holding period return (HPR) for the 6-month period and its simple annualized return?
A) HPR = 10.0%; Annualized Return = 20.0%
B) HPR = 15.0%; Annualized Return = 30.0%
C) HPR = 15.0%; Annualized Return = 32.25%
D) HPR = 12.5%; Annualized Return = 25.0%
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Capital Appreciation} = \text{NAV}_1 - \text{NAV}_0 = 44.00 - 40.00 = ₹4.00$$ $$\text{Total Return per Unit} = \text{Capital Appreciation} + \text{Dividend} = 4.00 + 2.00 = ₹6.00$$ $$\text{6-Month HPR} = \frac{₹6.00}{₹40.00} \times 100 = \mathbf{15.0\%}$$ $$\text{Simple Annualized Return} = 15.0\% \times \frac{12}{6} = \mathbf{30.0\%}$$
💡 Formula Application • Treynor & Jensen Evaluation
Question 4: A mutual fund portfolio delivered an annual return of 18%, with a portfolio beta ($\beta_p$) of 1.25 and portfolio standard deviation ($\sigma_p$) of 20%. The risk-free rate ($R_f$) is 6%, and the market benchmark return ($R_m$) is 14%. What is the Treynor Ratio and Jensen's Alpha ($\alpha$) of the fund?
A) Treynor Ratio = 9.60%; Jensen's Alpha = +2.00%
B) Treynor Ratio = 12.00%; Jensen's Alpha = +4.00%
C) Treynor Ratio = 0.60; Jensen's Alpha = -1.50%
D) Treynor Ratio = 14.40%; Jensen's Alpha = +3.50%
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Treynor Ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\beta_p} = \frac{18\% - 6\%}{1.25} = \frac{12\%}{1.25} = \mathbf{9.60\%}$$ $$\text{CAPM Expected Benchmark Return} = R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f) = 6\% + 1.25(14\% - 6\%) = 6\% + 10\% = 16\%$$ $$\text{Jensen's Alpha } (\alpha) = R_p - [R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f)] = 18\% - 16\% = \mathbf{+2.00\%}$$

💡 Concept Check • Structure & Pricing
Question 5: Which of the following accurately describes the operational and pricing distinctions between an Open-Ended Scheme, a Close-Ended Scheme, and an Exchange Traded Fund (ETF)?
A) Open-ended schemes trade continuously at intraday market prices on stock exchanges, whereas ETFs are priced only at end-of-day NAV
B) Open-ended funds permit continuous daily subscription and redemption at end-of-day NAV; close-ended funds have fixed unit capitalization and trade on stock exchanges often at a discount to NAV; ETFs trade continuously intraday on stock exchanges with arbitrage-driven creation/redemption baskets aligning market prices with NAV
C) Close-ended funds expand their unit capital daily through open market issuance, while open-ended funds have fixed capitalization
D) ETFs are unlisted offshore hedge funds that charge high performance carry fees
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: Open-ended funds offer continuous entry and exit directly with the AMC at end-of-day NAV. Close-ended funds raise a fixed corpus during the NFO and can only be traded on exchanges thereafter (often trading at a discount due to illiquidity). ETFs combine open-ended liquidity with exchange trading: Authorized Participants (APs) create and redeem large creation unit baskets, maintaining arbitrage parity between secondary market prices and underlying NAV.
💡 Concept Check • Direct vs Regular Plans
Question 6: Why does a Direct Plan of a mutual fund scheme consistently maintain a higher Net Asset Value (NAV) and generate higher compounded returns over time compared to the Regular Plan of the exact same scheme?
A) Direct plans invest in a completely different, higher-alpha portfolio of stocks
B) Direct plans bypass distributors and intermediaries, incurring zero distributor commissions and trail fees, resulting in a lower Total Expense Ratio (TER) which allows more capital to compound
C) Direct plans are legally exempt from paying securities transaction taxes and GST
D) Direct plans receive sovereign return subsidies from the capital market regulator
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: Direct plans and Regular plans hold the identical underlying portfolio. However, because Direct plans eliminate distributor commissions and trailing advisory fees, their Total Expense Ratio (TER) is 0.5%–1.5% lower per annum. This recurring operational saving directly increases daily NAV and compounds over multi-year horizons into substantial additional returns.
💡 Concept Check • Side Pocketing
Question 7: In mutual fund debt portfolio management, what is the core rationale and operational mechanism of "Side Pocketing" (creating a segregated portfolio)?
A) Transferring cash balances into offshore tax havens to avoid capital gains taxes
B) Separating illiquid or distressed credit-downgraded debt securities from the main portfolio to prevent distress panic selling and ensure existing unitholders receive future recoveries while protecting fund liquidity
C) Artificially elevating the scheme's beta to generate higher Sharpe ratios during bull markets
D) Bypassing statutory limits on single-issuer debt concentration
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: When a debt issuer experiences a credit event or default, Side Pocketing carves out that specific toxic asset into a segregated portfolio. Existing investors receive pro-rata units in the side pocket. This prevents panic redemption runs from depleting liquid assets, avoids fire-sale liquidation of illiquid debt at throwaway prices, and ensures that any future recoveries are distributed fairly to investors who bore the loss.

3. Master Exam Traps & Scoring Pointers

Dividend Reinvestment Units: When dividends are reinvested, always compute additional units as $\frac{\text{Total Dividend Amount}}{\text{Ex-Dividend NAV}}$. Add these to existing units for all subsequent dividend and redemption calculations.
Bonus Units vs. Dividend Equalization: When bonus units are issued (e.g. 1:5), total units increase by 20%, but the cost of bonus units is â‚¹ Nil for capital gains computation.
High-Water Mark Provision (Hedge Funds): Performance incentive fees (e.g. 20%) are payable only on the net wealth created above the previous historical peak, never on value already rewarded.
Constant Ratio Plan: Always rebalance back to the 50:50 benchmark by selling appreciated equity units or buying depressed equity units. This method enforces 'Buy Low, Sell High' discipline automatically.
💡 Exam Trap Check • Dividend Reinvestment Units
Question 8: An investor holds 10,000 units in a mutual fund scheme. The fund declares a cash dividend of ₹3.00 per unit. Under the Dividend Reinvestment Plan, the total dividend is automatically utilized to purchase additional units at the prevailing ex-dividend NAV of ₹24.00 per unit. How many new units are credited to the investor, and what is the new total unit holding?
A) 1,000 new units; Total = 11,000 units
B) 1,250 new units; Total = 11,250 units
C) 1,500 new units; Total = 11,500 units
D) 800 new units; Total = 10,800 units
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Total Dividend Amount Earned} = 10,000\text{ units} \times ₹3.00 = ₹30,000$$ $$\text{New Reinvestment Units Allotted} = \frac{\text{Total Dividend}}{\text{Ex-Dividend NAV}} = \frac{₹30,000}{₹24.00} = \mathbf{1,250\text{ units}}$$ $$\text{New Total Unit Holding} = 10,000 + 1,250 = \mathbf{11,250\text{ units}}$$
💡 Exam Trap Check • Constant Ratio Plan Rebalancing
Question 9: An investor establishes a Constant Ratio Plan maintaining a target 50:50 equity-to-debt asset allocation on an initial portfolio corpus of ₹10,00,000 (₹5,00,000 in equity and ₹5,00,000 in debt). After one year, the equity component surges to ₹7,50,000 while the debt component grows with interest to ₹5,50,000. Under the rules of the Constant Ratio Plan, what rebalancing transaction must be executed?
A) Purchase ₹1,00,000 of equity by selling ₹1,00,000 of debt
B) Sell ₹1,00,000 of equity and transfer the proceeds to buy ₹1,00,000 of debt
C) Liquidate all equity to lock in gains and hold 100% debt
D) Maintain status quo without rebalancing because portfolio value increased
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Total Portfolio Value} = 7,50,000 + 5,50,000 = ₹13,00,000$$ $$\text{Target Allocation (50:50)} = 50\% \text{ of } 13,00,000 = ₹6,50,000\text{ in Equity and } ₹6,50,000\text{ in Debt}$$ $$\text{Current Equity} = ₹7,50,000 \implies \text{Excess Equity to Sell} = 7,50,000 - 6,50,000 = \mathbf{₹1,00,000}$$ The investor must sell ₹1,00,000 of equity and purchase ₹1,00,000 of debt to restore the target 50:50 allocation.
💡 Exam Trap Check • Tracking Error Drivers
Question 10: For an index fund or ETF mirroring the benchmark index, which combination of structural factors causes "Tracking Error" (divergence of fund returns from index returns)?
A) Corporate default of index constituent companies exclusively
B) Fund operating expense ratio (TER), cash drag held for liquidity/redemptions, portfolio rebalancing transaction costs, and dividend receipt timing lags
C) Movements in foreign sovereign bond yields exclusively
D) Changes in statutory cash reserve ratios of commercial banks
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: Tracking error in passive funds arises because the theoretical benchmark is a frictionless cost-free index, whereas the actual fund incurs: (1) Total Expense Ratio (TER), (2) Cash drag (holding 1%–5% liquid cash for daily redemptions that earns money-market rates instead of equity returns), (3) Brokerage, stamp duty, and impact costs during rebalancing, and (4) Delays in reinvesting corporate dividends.

⚡ Master TYK 1 • Effective Annualized Yield (EAY)
Question 11: An investor invested in a debt mutual fund scheme for 90 days. The NAV increased from ₹20.00 to ₹20.50 over this period, with no interim dividend distributions. What is the Simple Annualized Return and the Effective Annualized Yield (EAY) assuming a 365-day year?
A) Simple Annual Return = 10.14%; EAY = 10.53%
B) Simple Annual Return = 10.00%; EAY = 10.00%
C) Simple Annual Return = 12.50%; EAY = 13.15%
D) Simple Annual Return = 8.50%; EAY = 8.75%
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Holding Period Return (HPR)} = \frac{20.50 - 20.00}{20.00} = \frac{0.50}{20.00} = 2.50\%$$ $$\text{Simple Annualized Return} = 2.50\% \times \frac{365}{90} = 2.50\% \times 4.0556 = \mathbf{10.14\%}$$ $$\text{Effective Annualized Yield (EAY)} = (1 + \text{HPR})^{\frac{365}{90}} - 1 = (1 + 0.025)^{4.0556} - 1 = 1.1053 - 1 = \mathbf{10.53\%}$$
⚡ Master TYK 2 • Fama Performance Attribution
Question 12: A fund manager achieved a portfolio return of 22% with portfolio standard deviation $\sigma_p = 18\%$ and portfolio beta $\beta_p = 1.20$. The market benchmark return ($R_m$) is 16% with market standard deviation $\sigma_m = 12\%$. The risk-free rate ($R_f$) is 7%. What is the fund's Total Excess Return, Diversification Penalty, and Fama's Net Selectivity?
A) Total Excess Return = 15.00%; Diversification Penalty = 2.70%; Net Selectivity = +1.50%
B) Total Excess Return = 15.00%; Diversification Penalty = 4.50%; Net Selectivity = +3.50%
C) Total Excess Return = 12.00%; Diversification Penalty = 1.50%; Net Selectivity = +0.50%
D) Total Excess Return = 15.00%; Diversification Penalty = 0.00%; Net Selectivity = +4.20%
Correct Answer: Option A
Detailed Working:
$$\text{Total Excess Return } (R_p - R_f) = 22\% - 7\% = \mathbf{15.00\%}$$ $$\text{Market Risk Premium } (R_m - R_f) = 16\% - 7\% = 9.00\%$$ $$\text{Total Risk Ratio} = \frac{\sigma_p}{\sigma_m} = \frac{18\%}{12\%} = 1.50$$ $$\text{Gross Selectivity (Jensen's Alpha)} = (R_p - R_f) - \beta_p(R_m - R_f) = 15\% - (1.20 \times 9\%) = 15\% - 10.80\% = 4.20\%$$ $$\text{Diversification Penalty} = \left(\frac{\sigma_p}{\sigma_m} - \beta_p\right)(R_m - R_f) = (1.50 - 1.20) \times 9\% = 0.30 \times 9\% = \mathbf{2.70\%}$$ $$\text{Fama's Net Selectivity} = \text{Gross Selectivity} - \text{Diversification Penalty} = 4.20\% - 2.70\% = \mathbf{+1.50\%}$$
⚡ Master TYK 3 • Sharpe vs Treynor Divergence
Question 13: Fund Alpha has a Sharpe ratio of 0.80 and a Treynor ratio of 16.0%. Fund Beta has a Sharpe ratio of 0.60 and a Treynor ratio of 18.0%. The market benchmark has a Sharpe ratio of 0.50. Which analytical conclusion regarding portfolio diversification and investor utility is correct?
A) Fund Beta is strictly superior to Fund Alpha for an investor whose entire net worth is invested in that single fund
B) Fund Alpha is better diversified (lower un-systematic risk relative to total risk), making it superior as a standalone portfolio; Fund Beta provides higher excess return per unit of systematic risk, making it superior as a component in a well-diversified multi-asset basket
C) Sharpe and Treynor ratios must always rank mutual funds in the identical order
D) Both funds underperformed the market benchmark index
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: The Sharpe ratio evaluates excess return per unit of Total Risk ($\sigma$). For an investor investing their entire wealth into a single fund, total risk matters, so Fund Alpha (Sharpe 0.80) is superior. The Treynor ratio measures excess return per unit of Systematic Risk ($\beta$), assuming un-systematic risk is fully eliminated by diversification. Thus, Fund Beta (Treynor 18.0%) is superior when added as a sub-component into a widely diversified portfolio.
⚡ Master TYK 4 • High-Water Mark Incentive Fee
Question 14: A hedge fund operates under a "2 and 20" fee structure (2% management fee on starting AUM + 20% incentive fee with a High-Water Mark provision). In Year 1, AUM grows from ₹100 Cr to ₹130 Cr. In Year 2, AUM declines to ₹110 Cr. In Year 3, AUM rises to ₹140 Cr. Disregarding management fees for the incentive calculation, what incentive fee is payable to the fund manager at the end of Year 3?
A) ₹6.00 Cr (20% on the ₹30 Cr gain from ₹110 Cr to ₹140 Cr)
B) ₹2.00 Cr (20% on the ₹10 Cr net new wealth created above the previous historical peak of ₹130 Cr)
C) ₹8.00 Cr (20% on ₹40 Cr total cumulative gain)
D) ₹0.00 Cr because Year 2 suffered negative returns
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Working:
Under the High-Water Mark principle, incentive performance fees are payable only on net new wealth created above the highest historical peak previously rewarded.
• Year 1 Peak (High-Water Mark) = ₹130 Cr (Fee paid on ₹30 Cr gain).
• Year 2 AUM = ₹110 Cr (Below HWM of ₹130 Cr $\implies$ No incentive fee).
• Year 3 AUM = ₹140 Cr. New wealth generated above previous HWM $= 140 - 130 = ₹10\text{ Cr}$.
$$\text{Incentive Fee Year 3} = 20\% \times ₹10\text{ Cr} = \mathbf{₹2.00\text{ Cr}}$$
⚡ Master TYK 5 • Bonus Units & Capital Gains
Question 15: An investor holds 5,000 mutual fund units acquired at an original cost of ₹30.00 per unit (Total Investment = ₹1,50,000). The scheme announces a bonus issue in the ratio of 1:2 (1 bonus unit for every 2 held), allotting 2,500 bonus units, after which the NAV adjusts to ₹20.00 per unit. Under standard capital gains tax provisions:
A) The cost of acquisition of the 2,500 bonus units is deemed to be ₹20.00 per unit, creating an immediate deductible tax loss
B) The cost of acquisition of the 2,500 bonus units is ₹Nil, while the cost of acquisition of the original 5,000 units remains intact at ₹30.00 per unit
C) The acquisition cost of all 7,500 units is blended and averaged to ₹20.00 per unit
D) Bonus units are fully taxed as ordinary income in the year of allotment
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Explanation: Under the Income Tax Act, the cost of acquisition of bonus units allotted on or after 1st April 2001 is deemed to be ₹Nil. The original 5,000 units retain their historical purchase cost of ₹30.00 per unit. When bonus units are subsequently redeemed, the entire redemption proceeds constitute capital gains.
