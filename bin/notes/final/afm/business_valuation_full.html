1. Core Concepts & Theoretical Architecture

13.1 Conceptual Framework & Core Purpose of Business Valuation

Business Valuation is the analytical process and set of procedures utilized to estimate the economic fair value of an owner's interest in an operating enterprise. The demand for rigorous business valuation arises in diverse corporate finance contexts:

  • Mergers, Acquisitions & Takeovers: Determining the target entity's standalone equity value, assessing acquisition synergies, setting swap exchange ratios, and establishing negotiation bidding price bands.
  • Capital Raising & IPO Listings: Determining initial public offering (IPO) issue pricing, private equity (PE) / venture capital (VC) entry and exit valuations, and rights issue pricing.
  • Strategic Management & Value-Based Management: Identifying key business value drivers (revenue growth, operating margins, capital efficiency), and assessing divisional performance via Economic Value Added (EVA).
  • Regulatory & Legal Compliance: Tax compliance (capital gains, transfer pricing), dispute resolutions, insolvency resolution under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), and financial reporting under Ind AS (e.g., Ind AS 113 Fair Value Measurement).

Distinction Between Price and Value

"Price is what you pay; value is what you get." — Warren Buffett.
Value is an intrinsic economic estimate derived from fundamental financial analysis of projected cash flows, assets, risks, and earnings capacity. Price is the actual transacted monetary amount agreed upon between a willing buyer and a willing seller in the open market, influenced by liquidity, negotiation power, market sentiment, and competitive bidding dynamics.

13.3 Asset-Based Valuation Methodologies

Under the Net Asset Value (NAV) method, equity value is the residual value after paying all external liabilities:

$$\text{NAV for Equity} = \text{Total Revalued Assets (Operating + Non-Operating)} - \text{Total External Liabilities} - \text{Preference Capital}$$

Valuation Bases for Assets

  • Book Value: Historical cost less accumulated depreciation as per balance sheet. Generally understates true economic value due to inflation and omitted intangibles (brand, human capital).
  • Net Realizable Value (NRV): Net cash proceeds expected from sale of individual assets in the ordinary course of business, less disposal costs.
  • Replacement Cost: Current expenditure required to replace existing operational assets with equivalent new assets possessing identical production capacity.
  • Liquidation Value: Net cash realized if assets are sold under forced or orderly distressed sale conditions, typically during bankruptcy or winding up.
  • Chop-Shop / Break-up Value Approach: Values an enterprise with multiple diversified divisions by applying industry-specific valuation multiples (Sales, Assets, Operating Income) to each business segment separately, summing the segment values, and subtracting corporate debt to arrive at total break-up equity value.

13.4 Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Valuation: FCFF vs FCFE

DCF is widely regarded as the most theoretically robust valuation approach because it models intrinsic cash-generating capability independent of accounting conventions.

Dimension Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF) Model Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE) Model
Definition Cash flow available to all capital providers (equity shareholders and debt holders) after meeting operating expenses, taxes, and capex/working capital investments. Cash flow remaining for equity shareholders after meeting all operating expenses, taxes, reinvestment needs, and debt service (interest and net principal repayments).
Computation Formula $$\begin{aligned} \text{FCFF} = &\ \text{EBIT}(1 - T) + \text{Depreciation} \\ &- \text{Capex} - \Delta\text{Working Capital} \end{aligned}$$ Or: $\text{PAT} + \text{Interest}(1 - T) + \text{Dep} - \text{Capex} - \Delta\text{WC}$. $$\begin{aligned} \text{FCFE} = &\ \text{PAT} + \text{Depreciation} - \text{Capex} \\ &- \Delta\text{Working Capital} + \text{Net Borrowing} \end{aligned}$$ Where Net Borrowing = New Debt Issued - Debt Repaid.
Applicable Discount Rate Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC): $$WACC = k_e \left(\frac{E}{V}\right) + k_d(1 - T)\left(\frac{D}{V}\right)$$ Cost of Equity ($k_e$): Computed via CAPM: $$k_e = R_f + \beta (R_m - R_f)$$
Resulting Output Value Enterprise Value (Firm Value): $$\text{Equity Value} = \text{Enterprise Value} - \text{Debt} + \text{Cash}$$ Direct Equity Value: $$\text{Value per Share} = \frac{\text{Equity Value}}{\text{Number of Equity Shares}}$$

Terminal Value ($TV_n$) in Multi-Stage Models

When forecasting cash flows over a discrete high-growth explicit period of $n$ years, the business value beyond year $n$ is captured via the Terminal Value:

$$\text{Terminal Value}_n = \frac{\text{FCFF}_{n+1}}{\text{WACC} - g_n} = \frac{\text{FCFF}_n \times (1 + g_n)}{\text{WACC} - g_n}$$

Where $g_n$ is the perpetual stable growth rate (which cannot logically exceed the long-term GDP growth rate of the economy).

13.5 Economic Value Added (EVA) & Market Value Added (MVA)

Economic Value Added (EVA), developed by Stern Stewart & Co., measures the true economic profit generated by a firm by charging for the opportunity cost of all invested capital:

Master Formula for Economic Value Added (EVA):
$$\mathbf{\text{EVA} = \text{NOPAT} - (\text{WACC} \times \text{Invested Capital})}$$ Where:
• $\text{NOPAT} = \text{Net Operating Profit After Tax} = \text{EBIT} \times (1 - T)$.
• $\text{Invested Capital} = \text{Total Debt} + \text{Total Equity} = \text{Net Fixed Assets} + \text{Net Working Capital}$.
• $\text{EVA} = (\text{ROIC} - \text{WACC}) \times \text{Invested Capital}$, where $\text{ROIC} = \frac{\text{NOPAT}}{\text{Invested Capital}}$.

Key Accounting Adjustments for True Economic Capital

To eliminate accounting distortions, standard reported GAAP figures are adjusted:

  • R&D and Marketing: Expensed under GAAP, but treated as capital investments in EVA (capitalized and amortized over useful life).
  • Operating Leases: Capitalized on balance sheet as debt and productive assets.
  • Provisions and Non-Cash Reserves: Added back to capital employed to reflect actual cash committed.

Market Value Added (MVA)

MVA represents the cumulative wealth created by management for shareholders over the entire lifetime of the company:

$$\mathbf{\text{MVA} = \text{Market Value of the Firm} - \text{Total Capital Invested by Stakeholders}}$$

$$\text{MVA} = \text{Market Value of Equity} - \text{Book Value of Equity}$$

Theoretically, MVA equals the Present Value of all future expected EVAs discounted at WACC: $\text{MVA} = \sum_{t=1}^\infty \frac{\text{EVA}_t}{(1 + \text{WACC})^t}$.

EVA Dividend

$$\text{EVA Dividend per share} = \frac{\text{Total EVA}}{\text{Number of Equity Shares}}$$

13.6 Fair Value Determination & Specialized Entity Valuations

Arriving at Fair Value (Dual Approach)

Under regulatory practice (e.g., Erstwhile Controller of Capital Issues / CCI guidelines and High Court schemes of arrangement), Fair Value per share is calculated as the simple average of:

$$\mathbf{\text{Fair Value per Share}} = \frac{\text{Net Asset Value (NAV) per share} + \text{Profit-Earning Capacity Value (PECV) per share}}{2}$$

Valuation of Specialized Entities

Entity Type Valuation Challenges Adapted Methodologies
Startups Negative earnings, zero historical track record, high failure rates, non-linear hyper-growth potential. Berkus Method, Scorecard Method, Venture Capital (VC) Method (post-money vs pre-money valuation), First Chicago Method.
Digital Platforms High intangible assets, two-sided network effects, customer lifetime value (LTV) vs customer acquisition cost (CAC). Cohort analysis, GMV multiples, EV/MAU (Monthly Active Users), LTV/CAC ratio framework.
Distressed Companies Imminent default, negative cash flows, debt overhang, non-going concern threat. Orderly vs forced liquidation value, Adjusted Present Value (APV) model, Black-Scholes option pricing (Equity as a call option on firm assets).
Professional / Consulting Firms Assets leave in the elevator every evening (human capital), client transferability risk, low tangible capex. Super-profit capitalization, excess earnings method, replacement cost of key personnel talent.
ESG Impact on Valuation Environmental, Social & Governance risks directly alter corporate risk profile, regulatory penalties, and green cost of capital. Higher ESG scores lower cost of debt and equity (lower discount rate); green capex improves long-term terminal value; poor ESG incurs regulatory carbon fines.

X Pvt. Ltd. - Angel Investment Valuation (DCF & Levered Beta)

There is a privately held company X Pvt. Ltd. that is operating into the retail space, and is now scouting for angel investors. The details pertinent to valuing X Pvt. Ltd. are as follows:

  • The company has achieved break-even this year and has an EBITDA of 90.
  • The unleveraged beta based on the industry in which it operates is 1.8, and the average debt-to-equity ratio is hovering at 40:60.
  • The rate of return provided by risk-free liquid bonds is 5%.
  • The EV is to be taken at a multiple of 5 on EBITDA.
  • The accountant has informed that the EBITDA of 90 includes an extraordinary gain of 10 for the year, and a potential write-off of preliminary sales promotion costs of 20 are still pending.
  • The internal assessment of rate of market return for the industry is 11%.
  • The future cash flows for the next 3 years are: Year 1: 100, Year 2: 120, Year 3: 150.
  • The pre-tax cost of debt is 12%. Assume a tax regime of 30%.

What is the potential value to be placed on X Pvt. Ltd.?

ICAI Study Material Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown

Step 1: Re-leveraging the Industry Beta

Using the Hamada equation with Debt/Equity = 40/60 and Tax Rate = 30%:

$$\beta_L = \beta_U \left[ 1 + (1 - T)\frac{D}{E} \right] = 1.8 \left[ 1 + (1 - 0.30) \times \frac{40}{60} \right] = 1.8 [1 + 0.70 \times 0.6667] = 1.8 \times 1.4667 = \mathbf{2.64}$$

Step 2: Adjusted EBITDA & Enterprise Value Multiple

$$\text{Adjusted EBITDA} = 90 - 10 \text{ (Extraordinary Gain)} - 20 \text{ (Pending Write-off)} = \mathbf{60}$$

$$\text{Enterprise Value using Multiple} = 60 \times 5 = \mathbf{300}$$

Step 3: Cost of Equity ($k_e$) via CAPM

$$k_e = R_f + \beta_L (R_m - R_f) = 5\% + 2.64 (11\% - 5\%) = 5\% + 2.64 \times 6\% = 5\% + 15.84\% = \mathbf{20.84\%}$$

Step 4: Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

$$k_d(\text{post-tax}) = 12\% \times (1 - 0.30) = 8.40\%$$

$$\text{WACC} = k_e \left(\frac{E}{V}\right) + k_d(1-T)\left(\frac{D}{V}\right) = 20.84\% \times 0.60 + 8.40\% \times 0.40 = 12.504\% + 3.36\% = \mathbf{15.864\%}$$

Step 5: Discounting Future Cash Flows at WACC (15.864%)

Conclusion:
• Enterprise Value based on EBITDA multiple (5x): 300.00.
• Intrinsic Value based on DCF of explicit 3-year cash flows at WACC 15.864%: 272.15.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Invested Capital} &= \text{Equity} + \text{Reserves} + \text{Non-Current Borrowings} + \text{Bad Debt Provision} \\ &= 700 + 100 + 100 + 20 = \mathbf{₹ 920 \text{ Lac}} \end{aligned}$$

(Asset Approach: $\text{PPE } 1000 + \text{Adjusted CA } (300+20) - \text{CL } 400 = 1320 - 400 = \mathbf{920 \text{ Lac}}$).

Step 3: Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

Total Equity Component = $700 + 100 + 20 = ₹ 820 \text{ Lac}$.

Debt Component = ₹ 100 Lac.

$$\text{WACC} = 8.45\% \times \left(\frac{820}{920}\right) + 8.40\% \times \left(\frac{100}{920}\right)$$

$$\text{WACC} = (8.45\% \times 0.8913) + (8.40\% \times 0.1087) = 7.5315\% + 0.9131\% = \mathbf{8.4446\% \approx 8.44\%}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{EVA} = \text{NOPAT} - \text{Capital Charge} = 301.00 - 77.69 = \mathbf{+₹ 223.31 \text{ Lac}}}$$

Economic Value Added (EVA) = +₹ 223.31 Lac (or ₹ 223.35 Lac depending on rounding of WACC).
The positive EVA indicates that management has generated significant surplus wealth above the opportunity cost of both debt and equity capital.

TYK-TH-02

Theoretical Question 2: Relative Valuation & Peer Comparative Analysis

Relative Valuation is the method to arrive at a ‘relative’ value using a ‘comparative’ analysis to its peers or similar enterprises. Elaborate this statement.

ICAI Study Material Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown

Elaboration on Relative Valuation (Valuation by Multiples)

While Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) models estimate the intrinsic value of an asset based on its fundamental cash flows and risk profile, Relative Valuation estimates value by benchmarking an enterprise against how similar, comparable assets are priced in the open market.

  1. Underlying Economic Premise: Similar assets should sell at similar prices (Law of One Price). By comparing standardized financial ratios across peer group companies in the same industry, analysts identify whether a target company is undervalued, fairly priced, or overvalued relative to the sector.
  2. Standardized Multiples Classification:
    Multiple Family Popular Ratios Analytical Strengths & Context
    Equity Multiples • Price-to-Earnings ($P/E$)
    • Price-to-Book Value ($P/BV$)
    • Price-to-Sales ($P/S$)     		Relates market equity capitalization directly to equity metrics (PAT, Net Worth). Sensitive to differing financial leverage and capital structures.
    Enterprise Value Multiples • Enterprise Value to EBITDA ($EV/\text{EBITDA}$)
    • Enterprise Value to Sales ($EV/\text{Sales}$)
    • Enterprise Value to Invested Capital     		Capital-structure neutral; widely preferred in cross-border acquisitions because EBITDA eliminates distortions from differing depreciation policies, tax rates, and debt gearing.
    Sector-Specific Multiples • EV / Monthly Active Users (Digital)
    • EV / Bed (Hospitals)
    • EV / Room (Hotels)     		Captures physical operating capacity and network scale where accounting profits are not yet mature.
  3. Practical Steps in Execution:
    • Select a true "peer group" with similar operating risk, size, growth prospects, and business models.
    • Normalize historical earnings by removing non-recurring, extraordinary, and non-operating gains/losses.
    • Apply peer median or average multiples to the target company's metrics to derive indicated equity value.
  4. Key Limitations:
    • Prone to market bubbles and collective mispricing: if the entire industry is overvalued, relative valuation will simply confirm the bubble.
    • True "pure-play" clones rarely exist; adjustments for scale, debt differences, and competitive moats are necessary.
